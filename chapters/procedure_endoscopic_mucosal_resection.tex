\chapter{Endoscopic Mucosal Resection}

\section*{Overview}
Endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) removes abnormal tissue from the inner lining of the digestive tract using a flexible camera (endoscope) passed through the mouth or anus. It can treat selected larger polyps and some early cancers or precancerous changes in the esophagus, stomach, small intestine, or colon. The removed tissue is examined in a laboratory to guide further care. EMR avoids an incision through the skin, but it is not suitable for every lesion.

\section*{Alternative Names}
EMR; endoscopic mucosal resection; mucosal resection. The exact method may be described as injection-assisted, cap-assisted, or underwater EMR.

\section*{Principle}
The endoscopist separates the abnormal lining from deeper layers and removes it, usually with a wire loop called a snare. In conventional EMR, fluid is often injected beneath the lesion to lift it and help protect the deeper wall. Some methods do not use an injection. The tissue may be removed in one piece (en bloc) or in several pieces (piecemeal), depending on its size, location, and appearance. A pathologist examines the specimen for the type of abnormality, depth, and other features that affect whether more treatment is needed.

\section*{History}
EMR developed from endoscopic techniques used to remove early gastrointestinal lesions, including early gastric cancers. It is now used at selected sites in the digestive tract. Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), surgery, or other treatments may be preferred for some lesions, particularly when removal in one piece is important or deeper invasion is suspected.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
EMR may be recommended for a larger or flat polyp, a precancerous area, or a carefully selected early cancer that appears limited to the inner layers of the digestive tract. It may remove a lesion that was too large or difficult to remove during a routine endoscopy and provide a larger specimen for diagnosis. The aim is complete removal while avoiding major surgery when this is appropriate. EMR cannot remove lymph nodes or treat disease that has spread beyond the area being removed.

\section*{Role in Care}
EMR is a treatment that also provides diagnostic tissue. A prior endoscopy, imaging, or biopsy may help plan it, but a small biopsy alone may not show the full depth or extent of a lesion. An endoscopist with training in advanced resection assesses the lesion and discusses EMR, other endoscopic methods, surgery, or monitoring as appropriate.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure takes place in an endoscopy unit or hospital. The approach depends on the lesion's location. For an upper digestive tract lesion, the endoscope passes through the mouth; for a colon or rectal lesion, it passes through the anus. Sedation or anesthesia is commonly used. The endoscopist examines the area, may inject fluid under the lesion or use another lifting method, then removes it with a snare or related device. The tissue is retrieved for pathology. Clips or other methods may be used to close or protect the removal site or to control bleeding. After removal of some large colon lesions, particularly on the right side, clips may be used to close the wound and lower the risk of delayed bleeding. The procedure can take longer than a routine endoscopy, especially for a large or difficult lesion.

\section*{Preparation}
Follow the endoscopy unit's instructions. Colon EMR usually requires bowel cleansing; upper-tract EMR usually requires fasting. Tell the team about all medicines, allergies, pregnancy, major health conditions, implanted devices, and prior reactions to sedation or anesthesia. Blood thinners and diabetes medicines may need a specific plan. Do not stop or change medicines unless the treating team tells you to. Arrange an escort and follow instructions about driving or other activities after sedation.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
EMR may not be the right approach if the lesion appears to extend deeply, cannot be safely accessed or removed, or has features suggesting a higher risk of spread. A lesion that does not lift after injection may be scarred from prior biopsy or treatment, or may have deeper involvement; this finding needs expert assessment and does not by itself establish cancer. Bleeding risk, other health conditions, and ability to undergo endoscopy or sedation also affect planning. Alternatives may include ESD, surgery, another endoscopic technique, or surveillance.

\section*{Risks}
Risks vary with the location, size, removal method, and a person's health. They include bleeding during or after the procedure, a tear or hole (perforation) in the digestive tract, and reactions to sedation or anesthesia. Thermal removal in the colon can rarely cause post-polypectomy electrocoagulation syndrome, a burn reaction in the bowel wall that causes abdominal pain and fever without a hole. Scar narrowing (stricture) can occur after removal of a large area, especially in the esophagus. Tissue may remain or grow back at the site, particularly after piecemeal removal, and another endoscopy or treatment may be needed. Rarely, a complication requires hospitalization, transfusion, surgery, or other intervention.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After EMR, staff monitor the patient and provide instructions for eating, activity, medicines, and warning signs. Some people go home the same day; others need hospital observation, depending on the lesion and how the procedure went. Temporary bloating, cramping, or a sore throat may occur. Diet and activity restrictions differ by the treated site and amount removed. Follow the instructions given for sedation and for restarting blood-thinning medicines. Do not rely on generic advice to stop pain medicines or blood thinners; ask the treating team if instructions are unclear.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The pathology report describes the lesion and whether its features suggest that endoscopic removal was adequate. The result, including depth of invasion, differentiation, lymphovascular invasion, and margins when assessable, helps the team decide whether endoscopic follow-up is enough or whether surgery or other treatment should be discussed. Piecemeal specimens may make margin assessment less certain. A technically complete removal does not by itself guarantee cure if pathology shows higher-risk features.

\section*{Expected Results}
The goal is to remove the intended lesion and obtain tissue for diagnosis. The outcome depends on pathology and follow-up examination, not just how the site looks at the end of the procedure. Some people need additional endoscopic treatment, surgery, or cancer care.

\section*{Follow-up}
The timing and type of surveillance depend on the organ, lesion size and pathology, and whether it was removed in one piece. For example, U.S. guidance recommends a first surveillance colonoscopy at about 6 months after piecemeal removal of a colorectal lesion 20 mm or larger; this interval does not apply to every EMR. Upper digestive tract lesions have different follow-up plans. Attend the appointments arranged by the specialist, even if you feel well.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent medical care for severe or worsening abdominal, chest, or throat pain; vomiting blood; black or bloody stools; fever; shortness of breath; difficulty swallowing that is worsening; fainting; or marked weakness. Follow the endoscopy unit's contact instructions, since bleeding or a perforation can occur after leaving hospital.
