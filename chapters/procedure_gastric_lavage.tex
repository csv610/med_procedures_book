\chapter{Gastric Lavage}

\section*{Overview}
Gastric lavage, sometimes called stomach pumping, is an uncommon emergency procedure in which a tube is passed through the mouth into the stomach and fluid is used to wash out stomach contents. It is not a routine treatment for poisoning. Evidence that it improves outcomes is weak, and it can cause serious complications. A poison center or medical toxicologist should help guide decisions about poisoning treatment.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item large-bore orogastric/nasogastric tube
  \item syringe or irrigation setup and collection basin
  \item suction and vital-signs monitoring equipment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Stomach pumping, gastric irrigation, gastric washout

\section*{Why It Is Done}
In very rare cases, a specialist may consider lavage after a potentially life-threatening ingestion. Some current guidance restricts consideration to selected people who arrive within about one hour of swallowing a potentially lethal amount, but evidence of benefit remains weak. The decision depends on the substance, amount, timing, the person's condition, and whether safer or more effective treatments are available. Lavage is not standard treatment for gastrointestinal bleeding and should not be used simply to obtain a stomach sample.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Gastric lavage is a rare, selective decontamination procedure. Most people with poisoning are treated with supportive care and, when appropriate, a specific antidote or another decontamination method. When gut decontamination is appropriate, activated charcoal is generally preferred over lavage for poisons it can bind; it does not work for every poison and should only be given on medical or poison-center advice. Lavage should only be considered by a trained team with appropriate expertise; it should never be attempted at home.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
If specialists decide lavage is appropriate, it is performed in a monitored medical setting. A large tube is passed through the mouth into the stomach, and fluid is introduced and removed through the tube. The team monitors breathing, circulation, and other vital signs throughout. The procedure can be uncomfortable and may require airway or other supportive measures depending on the person's condition. The exact method is determined by the treating team and is not suitable for self-treatment.

\section*{Preparation}
This is an emergency procedure, so treatment decisions are made promptly. The team identifies the suspected substance, amount, and time of exposure; assesses the person's breathing and vital signs; and contacts poison-control or toxicology experts as needed. If the person is drowsy, having seizures, or unable to protect their airway, lavage must not be done unless a breathing tube has been placed into the windpipe to protect the lungs. Placing a breathing tube has its own risks and is not done automatically just to allow lavage.

\section*{Contraindications}
Lavage should not be performed when it could increase harm. Important reasons to avoid it include an unprotected airway, swallowing a corrosive substance such as a strong acid or alkali, or swallowing a hydrocarbon with a high risk of entering the lungs if vomited. It is also avoided when the person has conditions that increase the risk of bleeding or a hole in the stomach or esophagus, such as certain recent gastrointestinal operations or significant gastrointestinal disease.

\section*{Precautions}
The treating toxicology team assesses each case.

\section*{Risks}
Possible complications include stomach contents entering the lungs, low oxygen, airway spasm, injury or a hole in the esophagus or stomach, bleeding, changes in heart rate, and disturbances in the body's fluid and mineral balance. The procedure may delay other treatment and does not reliably remove all of the substance from the stomach. In some cases, lavage may move material into the intestine or increase absorption.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The person is monitored for breathing problems, injury, and effects of the ingested substance. Treatment may include supportive care, antidotes, activated charcoal or other measures when appropriate, and laboratory or heart monitoring. Follow-up depends on the substance and its possible delayed effects.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Lavage does not guarantee that a toxic substance has been removed or prevent poisoning complications. The outcome depends mainly on the substance, dose, time to care, and the person's response to treatment. Intentional ingestion also requires assessment of immediate safety and appropriate mental-health follow-up once the person is medically stable.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no reliable visual endpoint that proves all harmful material has been removed. The medical team follows the person's symptoms, examination, test results, and the known effects of the substance to determine whether further treatment or observation is needed.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow-up is individualized and may include observation for delayed effects, repeat laboratory tests or heart monitoring, and specialist care. After intentional self-harm, the team should arrange a psychosocial and safety assessment.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
If poisoning or overdose may have occurred, contact emergency services or a poison center immediately. Do not wait for symptoms and do not induce vomiting or try to wash out the stomach at home. If the person is unconscious, having a seizure, struggling to breathe, or difficult to wake, call emergency services now and do not give them anything by mouth.
