\chapter{Blood Transfusion}

\section*{Overview}
A blood transfusion gives donated blood or a blood component through a vein. Most transfusions use a specific component rather than whole blood. Red blood cells help carry oxygen; platelets and plasma help blood clot; cryoprecipitate provides fibrinogen and other clotting proteins. The choice depends on the person's condition and the problem being treated.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item filtered blood-administration tubing and IV access
  \item infusion pump or blood warmer when indicated
  \item vital-signs monitor and identification supplies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Blood component transfusion; red blood cell transfusion; platelet transfusion; plasma transfusion; cryoprecipitate transfusion

\section*{Principle}
Donated blood is separated into components so a patient can receive what is needed. Red blood cells can improve the blood's ability to carry oxygen. Platelets help form clots. Plasma contains clotting proteins, and cryoprecipitate is a concentrated source of fibrinogen and some other clotting proteins. Whole blood is used in selected situations, such as some cases of major bleeding. A transfusion does not treat every cause of anemia or bleeding, and it does not replace the need to treat the underlying problem.

\section*{History}
Early attempts to transfuse blood were often unsuccessful. Karl Landsteiner's discovery of the ABO blood groups in 1900 helped make transfusion safer. Blood banking and the separation of donated blood into components developed during the 20th century.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Red blood cells may be given for serious blood loss or anemia when the body needs more oxygen-carrying capacity. Platelets may be used for certain patients with a very low platelet count or a platelet problem that causes or raises the risk of bleeding. Plasma or cryoprecipitate may be used for selected bleeding or clotting problems. In some conditions, an exchange transfusion replaces some or most of a person's blood with donor blood. A low blood count alone does not always mean a transfusion is needed; the team considers symptoms, bleeding, the cause of the problem, other treatments, and the person's overall condition.

\section*{Role in Care}
Transfusion is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. The clinician discusses its expected benefits, risks, and alternatives with the patient whenever circumstances allow. Some causes of anemia can be treated with iron, vitamin B12, folate, or other medicines instead, if the person's condition allows time for them to work. For many hospitalized adults who are stable and not actively bleeding, guidelines generally consider red blood cell transfusion when hemoglobin is below 7 g/dL (70 g/L). A slightly higher threshold may be used for cardiac surgery (about 7.5 g/dL or 75 g/L) and for orthopedic surgery or pre-existing cardiovascular disease (about 8 g/dL or 80 g/L). Guidance differs for acute coronary syndrome (reduced blood flow to the heart): NICE advises considering a threshold of 8 g/dL (80 g/L), while a 2025 AABB guideline conditionally suggests a more liberal approach below 10 g/dL (100 g/L) for hospitalized patients with acute myocardial infarction (heart attack). The AABB recommendation is based on low-certainty evidence. These thresholds guide decisions but are not rules for every patient; the diagnosis, the person's condition and preferences, and local guidance matter. A patient may ask questions or decline transfusion.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
Before most transfusions, a blood sample is tested to determine the patient's blood group and to look for antibodies that could react with donor blood. Red blood cell units are usually checked for compatibility; testing and matching requirements differ for other components. In an emergency, the team may issue blood before all compatibility testing is complete if waiting would be unsafe.

The component is connected to an intravenous line through filtered tubing. Staff check the patient's identity and the product details carefully before starting, record baseline vital signs, and monitor the patient's condition and vital signs during and after the transfusion. The patient is watched especially closely during the first 15 minutes of each unit or component bag, because a serious reaction can begin after only a small amount has been given. The time depends on the component, the amount given, and the person's condition. A bag of red blood cells may take up to about 4 hours. If a reaction is suspected, staff stop the transfusion immediately, assess the patient, and maintain intravenous access according to local protocol. They do not flush blood remaining in the tubing into the patient; if needed, they use a new line for fluids.

\section*{Preparation}
The team explains why transfusion is being considered and discusses expected benefits, risks, and alternatives; consent is obtained when possible. A blood sample is usually collected for compatibility testing. Tell the team about any past transfusions, reactions, pregnancies, blood antibodies, allergies, heart or kidney problems, and medicines. Do not take premedication or change medicines unless the clinician instructs you. Ask the team what symptoms to report during and after the transfusion.

\section*{Contraindications}
No specific absolute contraindication is identified here; the treating team assesses suitability for the individual.

\section*{Precautions}
There is no single rule that applies to every patient or component. A prior transfusion reaction or known blood antibodies may require specially selected blood and extra planning. People with heart or kidney problems, or those at risk of fluid overload, may need a slower or more carefully monitored transfusion. Some patients need specially processed components, such as irradiated or antigen-matched blood, for specific medical reasons; these products are not required for every person with a weakened immune system. A patient who does not want transfusion should tell the care team so alternatives can be discussed.

\section*{Risks}
Many patients have no reaction. Mild reactions can include fever, chills, itching, or a rash. Less common but serious problems include an incompatible-blood reaction that destroys red blood cells, a severe allergic reaction, transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO), in which extra fluid strains the heart and lungs, or transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), a serious lung reaction. Some antibody-related reactions that destroy red blood cells can appear days or weeks later. Infections transmitted from donors are rare because donations are screened and tested. Bacterial contamination remains a risk, especially with platelet transfusions, and can cause severe illness. Repeated red blood cell transfusions can cause iron to build up in the body, and the immune system may develop antibodies that make future transfusions harder to match.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Staff monitor the patient during and after the transfusion for a reaction. Blood counts or clotting tests may be repeated when they will help guide care; they are not automatically expected to return to a normal range after every transfusion. If the patient is stable and not actively bleeding, the team may give one red blood cell unit and reassess before deciding whether more is needed. Follow the team's instructions about follow-up and any additional treatment for the underlying condition.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The intended effect depends on the component: red blood cells support oxygen delivery, platelets support clotting, and plasma or cryoprecipitate replace selected clotting proteins. Symptoms, bleeding, examination findings, and laboratory tests help show whether the transfusion helped. The response varies, and ongoing bleeding, fluid shifts, or destruction of blood cells can affect test results. Some people need repeat transfusions; others need further testing or treatment for the cause of the problem.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no single expected result for every transfusion. The care team considers the patient's symptoms, condition, and relevant blood tests rather than expecting every result to reach a standard reference range.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow-up depends on the reason for transfusion and the component given. Patients who receive repeated transfusions may need monitoring for iron overload or antibodies. The team will explain whether blood tests, specialist review, or additional treatment is needed. The transfusion and its reason should be recorded in the medical record, and this information should be included in discharge information and shared with the patient's usual clinician when appropriate.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Tell the nurse or doctor immediately if you feel feverish or chilled, itchy, short of breath, dizzy, nauseated, or develop a rash, chest or back pain, or any sudden change while the transfusion is running. After leaving the care setting, seek urgent medical advice for fever or chills, a new rash, unusual tiredness, yellowing of the eyes or skin, dark or bloody urine, reduced urination, or unexplained bleeding or bruising. Call emergency services for severe trouble breathing, swelling of the mouth or throat, chest pain, fainting, or rapidly worsening symptoms. Tell the clinician that you recently received a transfusion.
