\chapter{Amniotomy}

\section*{Overview}
Amniotomy is the deliberate opening of the amniotic membranes (the bag of waters) during labor or induction of labor. It may be used in selected situations to help labor progress or to allow placement of an internal fetal monitor. It is not routinely needed when labor is progressing normally. The clinician and patient should discuss why it is being considered, expected benefits, and risks.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item sterile gloves and amniotomy hook
  \item fetal heart-rate monitor
  \item sterile pads and basic delivery-room supplies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Artificial rupture of membranes, ARM, Breaking the waters

\section*{Principle}
During a vaginal examination, the clinician uses a sterile instrument to make a small opening in the membranes. The fluid drains through the vagina, and the patient may feel a warm gush. Contractions may become stronger and labor may progress faster, but this is not guaranteed. Once the membranes are open, an internal fetal monitor can be used if clinically needed and appropriate; monitoring can also remain external. The fluid's color and appearance can be observed, but this alone does not diagnose the baby's wellbeing.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Amniotomy may be part of labor induction (starting labor) or be considered when there is a confirmed delay in the first stage of labor and the membranes are intact. ACOG recommends it during induction or augmentation (helping labor that has already started progress) to reduce labor duration. For induction, NICE recommends offering amniotomy with an intravenous oxytocin infusion when the Bishop score is greater than 6. The Bishop score is based on cervical findings and the station of the baby's head; it helps guide the choice of induction method. The patient may choose to delay or decline oxytocin, understanding that labor may take longer and the risk of newborn infection may increase.

For a confirmed delay in the established first stage, NICE advises discussing amniotomy; it may shorten labor by about an hour but can make contractions stronger and more painful. NICE advises against routinely combining early amniotomy with oxytocin when labor is progressing normally. If oxytocin is used after the membranes open, it can bring birth forward but does not change the mode of birth or newborn outcomes. Oxytocin increases the strength and frequency of contractions and can cause contractions that are too frequent or prolonged, which may temporarily reduce the baby's oxygen supply. Contractions and the baby's heart rate therefore need close, usually continuous, monitoring while oxytocin is used; the dose can be reduced or stopped if this problem occurs. Amniotomy may also be needed to place an internal fetal heart-rate electrode or an intrauterine pressure catheter (a sensor placed inside the uterus to measure contractions) when external monitoring is inadequate and internal monitoring is appropriate. It should not be performed just to look for meconium or routinely when uncomplicated labor is progressing normally.

\section*{Role in Care}
An optional obstetric intervention used in selected labor or induction plans. Whether and when it is appropriate depends on labor progress, the position and descent of the presenting part, fetal and maternal condition, and the patient's preferences.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician explains the procedure and obtains the patient's consent. Beforehand, the clinician checks the fetal heart rate, the baby's position, and how low and firmly settled the presenting part (usually the head) is in the pelvis. During a vaginal examination, a sterile amnihook (a small instrument for opening the membranes) is passed to the membranes and used to make a small opening. The clinician observes the fluid and checks the fetal heart rate promptly afterward for changes that could signal cord compression or prolapse. The ongoing fetal monitoring plan depends on the clinical situation.

\section*{Preparation}
The team confirms the reason for amniotomy, the baby's position, and that the presenting part is low and stable enough to reduce the risk of the umbilical cord slipping down ahead of the baby (cord prolapse). The fetal heart rate is checked. The team also considers whether there is a condition that makes labor or vaginal birth unsafe and reviews relevant infection risks, including group B streptococcus status, so that indicated antibiotics are given. The patient may ask about pain relief, since contractions can feel stronger after the membranes are opened.

\section*{Contraindications}
Avoid amniotomy when vaginal birth is contraindicated, such as with placenta previa, vasa previa, or active genital herpes lesions at labor. Do not perform it when the umbilical cord is felt or seen below the presenting part. For a patient with HIV, the team should consider the most recent viral-load result and treatment plan; current U.S. guidance advises avoiding amniotomy when the viral load is detectable (50 or more copies/mL), if possible, unless there is a clear obstetric reason. Fetal scalp electrodes should generally be avoided even when the viral load is suppressed and should not be used when suppression is absent or unknown. A previous cesarean is not by itself an absolute contraindication to amniotomy, but the uterine scar affects the labor plan; discuss the reason, benefits, and risks and use appropriate monitoring, especially if induction or oxytocin is planned.

\section*{Precautions}
An intrauterine pressure catheter should be used cautiously and only for a clear obstetric reason because data are limited. A high or not-yet-settled presenting part, unstable lie, or a position other than head-first can raise the risk of cord prolapse and needs an individualized specialist plan; a controlled procedure in an appropriate setting may be considered only when clinically justified. The risks and alternatives should be discussed with the patient.

\section*{Risks}
The most serious immediate risk is umbilical cord prolapse, when the cord slips down ahead of the baby, especially when the presenting part is high or not well settled against the cervix. The cord can be compressed, causing sudden changes in the baby's heart rate and sometimes requiring urgent birth. Other risks include bleeding, rare accidental injury to the baby's scalp, and infection; infection risk can increase the longer the membranes are ruptured before birth. Contractions may become stronger and more painful. Amniotomy may not start or speed labor and cannot be reversed once performed.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The fetal heart rate is checked promptly after the procedure and monitored according to the clinical situation. The care team tracks the time since membrane rupture, labor progress, temperature, and other signs of infection. Group B streptococcus antibiotics are given when indicated. Pain relief is available if contractions become more uncomfortable.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The care team assesses labor progress and fetal and maternal wellbeing. The membranes being open is not by itself a successful outcome; labor may or may not progress faster, and further treatment may be needed depending on the reason for the procedure and the patient's condition.

\section*{Expected Results}
The intended outcome is to support the labor plan or enable necessary internal monitoring. Labor progress varies. Amniotic fluid may be clear or may contain meconium; meconium does not by itself determine the mode of birth, but the team will assess it with the fetal heart rate and overall clinical picture and plan newborn care as needed.

\section*{Follow-up}
Fetal monitoring and labor assessment continue according to the clinical situation. When amniotomy is performed for a diagnosed delay in the established first stage, NICE advises a repeat vaginal examination about 2 hours later to assess progress. There is no fixed time by which birth must occur after amniotomy. Further steps, including oxytocin or cesarean birth, depend on labor progress, the reason for induction or augmentation, and maternal and fetal wellbeing; amniotomy alone does not mean cesarean birth will be needed.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
During labor, tell the care team immediately about a sudden change in the baby's movement, vaginal bleeding, fever, severe pain, or fluid with an unusual color or odor. If you see or feel something cord-like in the vagina after the membranes open, alert staff immediately and do not touch or push it. If this happens outside a care setting, call emergency services immediately and do not try to push the cord back inside. After leaving the birth setting, contact the maternity team urgently for fever, foul-smelling fluid, heavy bleeding, severe abdominal pain, or feeling unwell. Follow the care team's instructions for urgent assessment.
