\chapter{Endotracheal Intubation}

\section*{Overview}
Endotracheal intubation places a breathing tube through the mouth, or sometimes the nose, past the vocal cords into the windpipe (trachea). The tube can keep an airway open and connect a person to a ventilator or other breathing support. It is used during some operations and in emergencies or critical illness. A tube can support breathing and reduce the chance of material entering the lungs, but it does not remove all aspiration risk.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item direct or video laryngoscope
  \item endotracheal tube with stylet or bougie
  \item suction, bag-mask, and end-tidal carbon dioxide monitor
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Endotracheal intubation; tracheal intubation; intubation. Oral intubation passes through the mouth; nasal intubation passes through the nose.

\section*{Principle}
A clinician uses a laryngoscope or another airway device to see or guide the tube through the vocal cords into the trachea. An inflatable cuff near the tube's tip may be inflated to help hold the tube in place and reduce air leakage. The cuff does not fully prevent aspiration. The tube is connected to a breathing bag or ventilator, which can deliver oxygen and support breathing.

\section*{History}
The development of laryngoscopes and flexible breathing tubes made it possible to place and guide a tube through the vocal cords under direct or camera-assisted vision. Video laryngoscopes and other airway tools now provide additional options, especially when the airway may be difficult.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Intubation may be needed when a person cannot breathe adequately, needs mechanical ventilation during general anesthesia, has an airway that is blocked or at risk of closing, or cannot protect the airway from secretions or vomit. A low level of consciousness alone does not automatically mean that intubation is required; clinicians consider the cause, breathing, airway protection, and the overall situation.

\section*{Role in Care}
Intubation is an airway and breathing intervention, not a test. It is one option for supporting an airway. Depending on the situation, clinicians may use oxygen, a face mask, noninvasive ventilation, or a supraglottic airway instead. A tracheostomy or emergency front-of-neck airway may be needed in some circumstances.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The approach depends on urgency, the person's condition, and whether a difficult airway is expected. A clinician with airway training positions the person and uses a laryngoscope, often with a camera, to guide the tube between the vocal cords. Medicines to reduce discomfort, induce anesthesia, or relax muscles may be used; planned awake intubation is an option in selected situations. After placement, the tube is secured and connected to a breathing device. If the first approach is difficult, the team may use another device or technique, restore breathing with a mask or airway device, or use an emergency alternative.

\section*{Preparation}
In a planned procedure, the team reviews the person's airway, medical history, medicines, allergies, and anesthesia risks, then explains the plan. In an emergency, there may be little time for preparation. The clinical team prepares oxygen, suction, monitoring, ventilation equipment, an appropriate tube and airway devices, medicines when needed, and a backup plan. These are clinician tasks; patients should follow the treating team's instructions.

\section*{Contraindications}
No specific absolute contraindication is identified here; the treating team assesses suitability for the individual.

\section*{Precautions}
There may be no time or safe alternative to intubation when breathing or airway protection is failing. Facial injury, airway swelling, limited mouth opening, neck injury, or changes in airway anatomy can make some approaches difficult or unsafe. These factors call for an individualized plan and backup options rather than a single universal technique. If intubation is not immediately necessary, the team weighs its risks against other ways to support breathing.

\section*{Risks}
Possible complications include low oxygen during attempts, injury to the lips, teeth, throat, vocal cords, or windpipe, bleeding, aspiration, and placement in the esophagus or too far into one lung. A misplaced tube can be life-threatening if not recognized. Medicines and positive-pressure ventilation can affect blood pressure or heart rate. A breathing tube can also cause throat discomfort or hoarseness; longer use may cause additional airway or lung complications.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The team confirms tube placement with continuous waveform capnography, which detects carbon dioxide in exhaled breath, and checks chest movement and breath sounds. The tube is secured and its position is monitored because it can move. A chest X-ray is commonly used after placement in the hospital to assess tube depth and the lungs, but it does not replace capnography for confirming that the tube is in the trachea. The patient receives ongoing breathing support, comfort medicines as needed, and monitoring. The team regularly checks whether the tube can be removed safely.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The immediate goal is a secure airway and adequate oxygen and ventilation. Continuous exhaled carbon dioxide detection supports confirmation that the tube is in the trachea; chest rise and breath sounds provide additional checks. The tube's depth and the patient's response are reassessed over time. Intubation supports the underlying care but does not itself treat the cause of breathing failure.

\section*{Expected Results}
The tube should remain in the trachea and allow effective breathing support. The length of time it is needed depends on why it was placed and how the person's condition changes. Some people need it only during anesthesia; others may need a ventilator for longer or require another airway plan.

\section*{Follow-up}
While intubated, clinicians monitor oxygenation, ventilation, comfort, and tube position and treat the underlying condition. They assess readiness for extubation (tube removal) when breathing and airway protection have improved. After removal, some temporary sore throat or hoarseness can occur. Persistent voice changes, noisy breathing, difficulty swallowing, or breathing problems require clinical assessment.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
For someone recently extubated, seek urgent medical help for new or worsening trouble breathing, noisy breathing, inability to swallow, coughing up blood, severe throat or chest pain, or rapidly worsening symptoms. In hospital, alert the care team immediately if the tube becomes loose, disconnected, or appears to move.
