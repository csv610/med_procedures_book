\chapter{Immunotherapy for Cancer}

\section*{Overview}
Cancer immunotherapy is a group of treatments that help the immune system recognize or attack cancer. It includes immune checkpoint inhibitors, some antibody medicines, cancer vaccines, immune-cell treatments such as CAR T-cell therapy, and other immune-modifying medicines. These treatments work in different ways and have different risks. This chapter covers cancer treatment; allergen immunotherapy, such as allergy shots, is a separate treatment.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Cancer immunotherapy, biological therapy, immuno-oncology treatment

\section*{Principle}
Some cancer cells can hide from or switch off immune cells. Checkpoint inhibitors block some of the signals that act like brakes on immune cells. Other treatments attach to targets on cancer cells, add immune cells changed in a laboratory, or otherwise change the immune response. The approach depends on the medicine and cancer type.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Immunotherapy may be used for certain cancers, at different stages and treatment points. Whether it is an option may depend on the cancer type and stage, test results such as biomarkers, previous treatment, overall health, and the treatment goal. It may be given alone or with chemotherapy, radiation, surgery, or another cancer medicine. It does not work for every cancer or every person.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Immunotherapy is a cancer treatment, not a diagnostic test. It may be used before or after surgery, to treat cancer that has spread or returned, or as part of another treatment plan. Some medicines require testing of the tumor for particular features before they are considered. The oncology team explains the goal, alternatives, and how response will be assessed.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The method depends on the treatment. Many medicines are given through a vein, but some are pills, injections, skin creams, or medicines placed directly into the bladder. Treatment may be given on a repeating schedule, with rest periods, and may be combined with other therapies.

CAR T-cell therapy is different from a routine infusion: immune cells are collected from the blood, changed and multiplied in a laboratory, and then returned through an IV. It may require chemotherapy beforehand, close monitoring, and a hospital stay or prolonged nearby follow-up. Ask the team which treatment you will receive and what monitoring it requires.

\section*{Preparation}
The care team reviews the cancer diagnosis, test results, medicines, allergies, and health conditions before treatment. Tell them about autoimmune disease, an organ transplant, infections, pregnancy or breastfeeding, and all prescription medicines, supplements, or recent cancer treatments. These factors do not automatically rule out treatment but can affect the risks and plan. Blood tests, scans, heart or lung checks, or tumor biomarker tests may be needed depending on the medicine. There is no single set of tests required before every immunotherapy dose.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Eligibility and precautions vary by treatment. An autoimmune condition or transplant may increase the chance of immune complications or organ rejection, while some medicines can affect infection risk or treatment effects. The oncology team weighs these risks against the expected benefit and may coordinate with other specialists. Do not start, stop, or change medicines or supplements without discussing them with the cancer team.

\section*{Risks}
Side effects vary by treatment and may occur during therapy or after it ends. Some treatments can cause infusion or injection reactions, fatigue, rash, fever, chills, nausea, or diarrhea. Checkpoint inhibitors can cause the immune system to inflame healthy organs, including the bowel, lungs, liver, kidneys, thyroid, pituitary gland, pancreas, heart, or skin. Symptoms may be mild at first but can become serious; early reporting helps the team assess them. Some immune-related problems are treated by pausing therapy or using medicines that calm the immune system.

CAR T-cell therapy and some other T-cell engaging treatments can cause cytokine release syndrome, a serious reaction that may involve fever, low blood pressure, fast heartbeat, or breathing difficulty. They can also cause nervous-system effects such as confusion, trouble speaking, tremors, severe headache, or seizures. These treatments require specialized monitoring and clear emergency instructions.

Some genetically modified CAR T-cell products also carry a warning about a rare risk of a new T-cell cancer after treatment. The treatment center will explain any long-term monitoring required for the specific product.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the treatment team's instructions about medicines, infection precautions, activity, and when to call. Keep a record of treatment and tell other health professionals that you are receiving or have received immunotherapy. For CAR T-cell or similar cellular treatment, follow the center's instructions about staying nearby, having a caregiver, and avoiding driving or other risky activities for the period specified.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The team checks how the cancer and the person's health respond using examinations, blood tests, scans, and sometimes other tests. The schedule depends on the medicine and cancer. Some responses take time, and scan changes need interpretation by the oncology team; apparent growth should not be assumed to be a beneficial treatment effect. Treatment may be continued, paused, changed, or stopped based on benefit, side effects, and the patient's goals.

\section*{Expected Results}
The goal may be to cure the cancer, reduce or control it, or ease symptoms. Some people have a lasting response, while others have little response or develop side effects that require a change in treatment. The expected benefit depends on the cancer, its biomarkers, the particular immunotherapy, and other health factors.

\section*{Follow-up}
Keep scheduled oncology appointments and complete the tests recommended for your specific medicine. Side effects can appear after treatment ends, so continue to report new or worsening symptoms and tell future clinicians about your immunotherapy history. People treated with certain genetically modified CAR T-cell products need lifelong monitoring for new cancers. Ask whether you should carry a treatment alert card and keep the oncology team's urgent contact information with you.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the oncology team promptly about new or worsening diarrhea, abdominal pain, rash, cough, shortness of breath, yellowing of the skin or eyes, dark urine, unusual weakness, severe headache, vision changes, or other new symptoms. Seek emergency care for severe breathing difficulty, chest pain, fainting, confusion, trouble speaking, seizure, or another sudden serious symptom. If you are receiving CAR T-cell or another cellular therapy, follow the center's specific emergency instructions, including what to do for fever.
