\chapter{Fracture Reduction}

\section*{Overview}
Fracture reduction is the process of bringing broken bone pieces into a better position. In a closed reduction, a trained clinician moves the bone from outside the body without making an incision. In an open reduction, a surgeon exposes the fracture through an incision and aligns the bone, often securing it with hardware. Open reduction with internal fixation (ORIF) is one type of surgery; it is not another name for every fracture reduction.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Setting a broken bone, closed reduction, open reduction, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF)

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A fracture may need reduction if the bone pieces are out of position and are unlikely to heal in an acceptable alignment without being moved. The goal is to improve alignment, relieve pressure on nearby skin or other tissues, and support healing and function. Some fractures do not need reduction and can be treated with a splint or cast in the position they are in. Others need surgery because of the fracture pattern, an open wound, instability, or unsuccessful closed treatment.

\section*{Role in Care}
Reduction is part of fracture treatment, not a single approach used for every broken bone. The clinician considers the bone and joint involved, the fracture pattern, skin and soft-tissue condition, circulation and nerve function, other injuries, and the person's health. Imaging helps guide the treatment plan and check the position afterward.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
Before treatment, the clinician examines the injured area, including circulation, movement, and sensation beyond the fracture. X-rays are commonly taken; other imaging may be needed for some injuries. A closed reduction is performed by a trained clinician, usually after medicine is given to control pain and relax the muscles. The clinician gently applies traction or pressure to improve the bone's position. A splint or cast is then used to hold and protect the limb. Follow-up imaging checks alignment.

For an open reduction, the surgeon makes an incision to see and align the bone. The bone may be held with plates, screws, pins, rods, or other devices. The wound is closed, and the limb is protected while healing begins. The exact operation and anesthesia depend on the fracture and the patient's condition.

\section*{Preparation}
Treatment may be urgent, especially if the fracture is open, the limb's blood supply is threatened, or skin is under severe pressure. Tell the team about medicines, allergies, medical conditions, pregnancy, and when you last ate or drank. If sedation or anesthesia is planned, follow the team's instructions; do not delay emergency care to fast. The team assesses circulation, movement, and sensation before the procedure and plans pain control. Surgery may require additional tests and preparation.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
There is no single contraindication list for all fractures. The risks of reduction and anesthesia are weighed against the risks of leaving the bone displaced. Open fractures, severe soft-tissue injury, suspected blood vessel or nerve damage, and multiple injuries need prompt specialist assessment. Infection, skin condition, swelling, and other health problems may affect the timing or method of fixation. Decisions are specific to the injury; a patient should not try to straighten or set a broken bone themselves.

\section*{Risks}
Possible risks include pain, bruising, or swelling; injury to nearby nerves, blood vessels, or soft tissues; additional bone injury; and a reduction that does not hold or does not align the bone well enough. A cast or splint can become too tight as swelling changes. Surgery adds risks such as infection, bleeding, blood clots, anesthesia complications, and problems related to implants. The fracture may heal slowly, heal out of position, or fail to unite, and stiffness or weakness may occur. The risks differ by fracture and treatment.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The limb is protected with a splint, cast, brace, or surgical fixation. Follow instructions about elevation, ice, wound or cast care, activity, and whether you may put weight on the limb. Do not insert objects under a cast or remove it yourself. Keep the cast dry unless the team says it is waterproof. Recovery time varies with the bone, fracture pattern, age, general health, and treatment; healing often takes weeks to months.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The team checks alignment with imaging and repeats the circulation, movement, and sensation examination after reduction and immobilization. The bone's position, pain, and healing are reviewed at follow-up visits. Additional imaging, a change in the cast or splint, physical therapy, or surgery may be needed if alignment changes or healing is not progressing. There is no fixed schedule that applies to every fracture.

\section*{Expected Results}
The aim is a bone position that is acceptable for that specific fracture and likely to heal while preserving function. Perfect anatomical alignment is not always needed, and the acceptable position differs by bone, age, and injury. The treatment team explains the expected healing course and activity limits.

\section*{Follow-up}
Keep all scheduled visits so the team can check the fracture and adjust protection or treatment. Gradual movement and rehabilitation are introduced when appropriate. Follow weight-bearing and return-to-work, driving, and sports instructions from the treating clinician.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent medical care for increasing or severe pain that is not relieved as instructed, rapidly increasing tightness or swelling, pain when gently moving the fingers or toes, new numbness or tingling, inability to move fingers or toes, or fingers or toes that become pale, blue, or cold. These can signal dangerous pressure in the limb or reduced blood flow. Contact the care team if a cast or splint becomes very tight, loose, wet, cracked, or causes burning or skin problems. After a leg fracture, cast, or surgery, new calf pain or swelling needs prompt medical assessment because it may be a blood clot. After surgery, seek advice for fever, worsening wound redness, drainage, or increasing pain. Sudden shortness of breath or chest pain requires emergency care.
