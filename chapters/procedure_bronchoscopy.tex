\chapter{Bronchoscopy}

\section*{Overview}
Bronchoscopy is a procedure that lets a clinician examine the voice box, windpipe, and branching airways in the lungs. A thin flexible bronchoscope is most often used. A rigid bronchoscope, which is wider and used under general anesthesia, may be chosen for certain treatments or when larger instruments are needed.

The clinician may only examine the airways or may also collect samples or treat a problem. The exact procedure and risks depend on what is done in addition to looking.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Flexible bronchoscopy; rigid bronchoscopy; bronchoscopic examination.

\section*{How It Works}
A bronchoscope has a light and camera and is passed through the nose or mouth into the airways. Instruments can pass through its working channel to collect fluid or tissue, remove secretions or a foreign object, control bleeding, or perform selected airway treatments. Endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) bronchoscopy uses an ultrasound probe on the scope to view structures next to the airways and guide needle sampling.

\section*{History}
Flexible bronchoscopy became an important diagnostic and treatment tool after Shigeto Ikeda introduced the fiberoptic bronchoscope in 1966. Rigid bronchoscopy was developed earlier and remains useful for selected airway procedures.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Bronchoscopy may be used to investigate a persistent cough, coughing up blood, an abnormal chest scan, a suspected infection or tumor, or a possible foreign object or airway blockage. It may also be used to collect samples, clear secretions, treat a narrowed airway, or place or check an airway stent. EBUS bronchoscopy can sample lymph nodes or other structures near the airways. The reason for the procedure determines which tools or samples are needed.

\section*{Role in Diagnosis and Treatment}
Bronchoscopy can be used for diagnosis, treatment, or both. It may be selected after imaging or other tests, or sooner when direct airway assessment or a sample is needed. A normal visual examination does not rule out disease in lung tissue beyond the airways.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
Most flexible bronchoscopies are done in a procedure room or hospital. The patient is positioned and given monitoring. The nose and throat are usually numbed with local anesthetic, and sedation may be given through a vein. The scope is passed through the nose or mouth, or sometimes through a breathing tube, into the airways. The clinician examines the airway lining and may suction secretions or take samples. The procedure usually takes from several minutes to longer than an hour, depending on its purpose and complexity.

Possible sampling methods include washing or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), brushing cells from an airway, needle sampling, and tissue biopsy. EBUS uses ultrasound to help guide needle samples. Rigid bronchoscopy is performed under general anesthesia and may be used to remove a large foreign object, manage some airway bleeding, or perform other treatments. The team monitors breathing, oxygen level, heart rate, and blood pressure throughout.

\section*{Preparation}
Follow the procedure team's instructions about fasting and medicines. Tell the team about allergies, pregnancy or possible pregnancy, oxygen use, sleep apnea, heart or lung disease, bleeding problems, and all medicines, especially anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs, and diabetes medicines. Do not stop or change prescribed medicines unless the prescribing clinician and bronchoscopy team provide a plan. Instructions can differ depending on whether a biopsy or another procedure is planned.

If sedation or general anesthesia is planned, arrange for an adult to take you home and follow instructions about supervision and when you may resume driving, work, alcohol, or important decisions. Bring a list of medicines and allergies.

Before the procedure, the clinician should explain whether it is only an examination or will also include sampling or treatment, and discuss the expected benefits, alternatives, and risks before asking for consent.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
The team weighs the expected benefit against the risk for each patient. Severe oxygen deficiency that cannot be corrected, unstable heart or blood pressure, a dangerous uncontrolled heart rhythm, or a severe bleeding problem may make bronchoscopy unsafe or require additional support, a modified procedure, or delay. The type of sampling matters: a tissue biopsy generally carries more bleeding and pneumothorax risk than looking or washing alone. There is no single list of conditions that applies to every patient and every bronchoscopy.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary cough, sore throat, hoarseness, fever, or a brief drop in oxygen can occur. Sedation or anesthesia can affect breathing, blood pressure, or heart rhythm. Bleeding risk depends on the sampling or treatment performed; a small amount of blood-streaked sputum may occur after biopsy. A biopsy that takes a small piece of lung through the airway (transbronchial lung biopsy) can cause pneumothorax (air around the lung), which may require treatment. Other uncommon complications include airway tightening (bronchospasm), infection, significant bleeding, or injury to the airway. Serious complications are uncommon, but risk is higher in people who are medically unstable and with more complex procedures.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After the procedure, staff monitor breathing, oxygen level, and other vital signs until it is safe to leave. If the throat was numbed, do not eat or drink until the care team confirms that swallowing and the gag reflex have returned; timing varies. After sedation or general anesthesia, follow discharge instructions and do not drive yourself. A mild sore throat or cough may last a short time. The team will tell you whether a small amount of blood in sputum is expected after your specific procedure.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The clinician may discuss what was seen during the procedure, but laboratory results can take several days or longer. Test results depend on what samples were collected: fluid may be sent for infection testing or cell analysis, while tissue is examined by a pathologist. A negative or nondiagnostic sample does not always exclude disease. The treating clinician interprets results with symptoms, imaging, and other tests and explains whether further evaluation or treatment is needed.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Follow the discharge instructions. Contact the bronchoscopy team for a fever that persists or worsens, increasing shortness of breath, worsening chest pain, or more than a small amount of blood in sputum. Seek emergency care for severe breathing difficulty, heavy bleeding, fainting, or severe chest pain.
