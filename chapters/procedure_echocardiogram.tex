\chapter{Echocardiogram}

\section*{Overview}
An echocardiogram (echo) uses ultrasound to make moving pictures of the heart. It can show the heart's size and structure, how strongly it pumps, how its valves move, and how blood flows through it. The standard test is transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), in which a probe is moved over the chest. Other types include transesophageal echo (TEE), stress echo, and echo with an ultrasound contrast agent.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Echo; echocardiography; cardiac ultrasound. TEE is also called transesophageal echocardiography (or TOE in some countries).

\section*{Principle}
The probe sends sound waves into the body and records the echoes that return from the heart. A computer turns these echoes into images. Doppler ultrasound measures the direction and speed of blood flow. Echo uses sound waves, not X-rays.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
An echo may help assess a heart murmur, shortness of breath, heart failure, valve disease, cardiomyopathy, congenital heart disease, fluid around the heart, or suspected problems with the heart's structure or pumping. TEE can provide closer views of some structures, such as valves or clots. A stress echo compares heart function at rest and during exercise or after medicine that makes the heart work harder. The clinician chooses the type based on the question being investigated.

\section*{Role in Care}
Echocardiography is an imaging test. It can help diagnose or monitor heart conditions, but findings are interpreted along with symptoms, examination, and other tests. A normal echo does not rule out every heart problem.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
\textbf{Transthoracic echo (TTE):} The patient lies on a bed, usually with the upper body partly turned to the left. A sonographer places gel on the chest and moves a handheld probe over the skin to record images. Stick-on ECG leads may monitor the heartbeat. The test usually takes about 30 to 60 minutes.

\textbf{Transesophageal echo (TEE):} After the throat is numbed and sedation may be given, a flexible ultrasound probe is passed through the mouth into the esophagus (food pipe), which lies close behind the heart. The clinician records images and then removes the probe. The test is monitored by trained staff.

\textbf{Stress echo:} Images are taken before and after exercise on a treadmill or stationary bicycle, or after medicine is given to increase the heart's workload. Heart rhythm and blood pressure are monitored.

\textbf{Contrast echo:} If standard images are not clear enough, an ultrasound contrast agent may be given through a vein to make parts of the heart easier to see. This is different from CT or MRI contrast.

\section*{Preparation}
For a standard TTE, people can usually eat, drink, and take their medicines as usual. A TEE usually requires fasting for several hours and may require someone to take the patient home after sedation. Follow the facility's exact instructions and ask which medicines to take, especially diabetes medicines or blood thinners; do not stop medicines on your own. Tell the team about swallowing problems, esophageal disease or surgery, dentures, allergies, and prior reactions to sedation or contrast agents. For a stress echo, instructions about food, caffeine, and heart medicines vary; follow the test center's directions and wear comfortable clothing and shoes.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
TTE is noninvasive and has few limitations, though chest wounds, dressings, body position, or lung and chest-wall anatomy can affect image quality. TEE requires extra assessment for esophageal narrowing or disease, swallowing difficulty, bleeding risk, and sedation safety. Stress testing may need to be modified or avoided for some conditions that make exercise or stress medicines unsafe. The clinical team weighs the benefits and risks for each person.

\section*{Risks}
TTE is painless and has no known harmful effects from the ultrasound waves. TEE can cause temporary throat soreness or, rarely, injury to the mouth, throat, or esophagus, breathing or aspiration problems, or complications from sedation. Stress echo can cause symptoms related to exertion or the medicine used, such as shortness of breath, chest discomfort, or an abnormal rhythm; staff monitor for these effects. Serious reactions to ultrasound contrast are rare, but should be reported immediately.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After a TTE, normal activities can usually resume right away. After TEE, wait to eat or drink until throat numbness has worn off and swallowing is safe; follow the recovery team's instructions. If sedated, do not drive or make important decisions for the time specified by the facility, and arrange a ride home. After stress echo, staff observe the patient until heart rate and symptoms recover. Most patients leave the same day.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The images are reviewed by a qualified clinician, who reports findings such as chamber size, pumping function, valve structure, blood flow, and estimates of pressure. Some results are available the same day; others require review. The referring clinician explains what the results mean and whether more tests or treatment are needed. Echo estimates have limits and do not measure every aspect of heart function.

\section*{Expected Results}
The test should provide images that help answer the clinical question. Sometimes the images are limited, and another type of echo or a different test may be recommended. Results describe findings; they do not by themselves determine a diagnosis or treatment plan.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow up with the clinician who ordered the test to discuss the report. Repeat echoes may be recommended to monitor a known condition or its treatment, but the timing depends on the diagnosis and clinical situation.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
After TEE or stress echo, contact the care team for severe or worsening throat, chest, or abdominal pain; trouble swallowing or breathing; persistent vomiting; or symptoms that do not improve as expected. Seek emergency care for severe chest pain, fainting, or severe breathing difficulty.
