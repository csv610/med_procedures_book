\chapter{Intracranial Pressure Monitoring}

\section*{Overview}
Intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring measures pressure inside the skull in selected patients with serious brain injury or illness. A catheter placed in a brain ventricle (an external ventricular drain, or EVD) can measure pressure and drain cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). A sensor placed in brain tissue measures pressure but does not drain CSF. The choice of device depends on the clinical problem and the need to drain fluid.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item pressure-monitoring catheter or bolt system
  \item transducer and bedside monitor
  \item neurosurgical sterile kit and imaging for placement checks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
ICP monitoring; intracranial pressure monitor; ventriculostomy; external ventricular drain (EVD); intraparenchymal pressure monitor; ICP bolt

\section*{Principle}
The skull contains the brain, blood, and CSF. If the volume of one increases and the others cannot compensate, pressure inside the skull can rise and reduce blood flow to the brain. A monitor provides pressure readings and waveforms to help the neurocritical care team assess trends alongside the examination and brain imaging. An EVD can also remove CSF; it must be kept at a prescribed height and managed carefully because its position affects drainage.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Monitoring may be used when examination and imaging indicate a risk of dangerously raised pressure, such as in selected cases of severe traumatic brain injury, bleeding around or within the brain, hydrocephalus, or brain swelling. It can help guide treatment, including CSF drainage. It is not needed for every person with a head injury, brain hemorrhage, or hydrocephalus. Decisions depend on the diagnosis, imaging, level of consciousness, and overall treatment plan.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
This is an invasive monitoring procedure. An EVD can also treat pressure buildup by draining CSF. Readings are interpreted with the patient's condition and other test results; a number alone does not determine treatment or predict outcome.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is performed by a trained neurosurgical clinician in an operating room, intensive care unit, or other suitable setting using sterile technique and local anesthesia, with sedation or other support as needed. After reviewing brain imaging, the clinician makes a small opening in the skull and places either a ventricular catheter or a pressure sensor in the planned location. The catheter or sensor is connected to a monitor. An EVD is leveled to the prescribed anatomical reference point and set at a height ordered by the treating team; CSF drains only when pressure exceeds that level. Staff re-level the system when the patient's position changes. When the EVD is used for pressure measurement, the team may need to temporarily close it to drainage according to the unit's protocol. The insertion site is covered with a sterile dressing.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews the brain scans, neurological examination, medications, allergies, blood clotting tests, and the risks and expected benefits. Blood thinners or a clotting problem may need an individualized plan; prescribed medicines should not be stopped without direction. The procedure is explained and consent is obtained from the patient or an appropriate decision-maker when circumstances allow. In an emergency, treatment may need to proceed under applicable emergency consent rules.

\section*{Contraindications}
No specific absolute contraindication is identified here; the treating team assesses suitability for the individual.

\section*{Precautions}
Infection at the planned insertion site is a serious concern. Small or shifted ventricles, brain swelling, or prior surgery can make placement more difficult. The team weighs the risk of insertion against the need for monitoring or CSF drainage. A bleeding disorder or blood-thinning medicine can increase the risk of bleeding and may need correction or another plan. Imaging guidance, sterile technique, careful leveling, and frequent checks help reduce complications. An EVD should be handled only by trained staff; changing its height, opening or closing it, or repositioning it without authorization can cause harm.

\section*{Risks}
Possible complications include bleeding in the brain or along the catheter path, incorrect placement, blockage, infection of the CSF or surrounding tissues, and injury to brain tissue. An EVD can drain too little or too much CSF. Overdrainage can cause the ventricles to become smaller and may contribute to bleeding around the brain or other serious complications. The monitor may fail or give inaccurate readings, and treatment based on a misleading reading can be harmful. The underlying brain condition also carries substantial risks independent of the monitor.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The patient is usually cared for in an intensive care or neurosurgical unit. Staff check neurological status, the insertion site, pressure readings, and—if present—the amount and appearance of drained CSF. EVD height and position are checked whenever the patient is moved. The system is kept closed and sterile except when trained staff access it for a clinical reason. The care team may use medicines, CSF drainage, or surgery if pressure remains high. In severe traumatic brain injury, guidelines recommend treating ICP above 22 mm Hg; the threshold and treatment plan depend on diagnosis, age, pressure trends, and the full clinical picture.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
In a supine adult, ICP is often about 5 to 15 mm Hg, but readings vary with body position, measurement method, and clinical state. A single value is not enough to diagnose a problem or decide treatment. In adults with severe traumatic brain injury, sustained ICP above 22 mm Hg is associated with worse outcomes and is a treatment threshold in current guidelines; this threshold should not be applied automatically to every diagnosis or to children. The team also considers cerebral perfusion pressure, imaging, and examination. Persistent high pressure may require additional treatment, while a stable clinical course may allow the team to gradually reduce CSF drainage and remove the device.

\section*{Expected Results}
The monitor provides usable pressure data to support care, and an EVD may allow controlled CSF drainage when indicated. A particular “normal” reading does not guarantee that the brain injury is resolving, and monitoring itself does not improve outcome unless the information guides appropriate care.

\section*{Follow-up}
The neurosurgical and intensive care teams decide when to clamp, wean, or remove an EVD based on the patient's condition and local protocol. Imaging and neurological examination may be repeated as needed. CSF is tested when infection is suspected; routine or repeated sampling without a clinical reason can increase infection risk. If hydrocephalus persists after temporary drainage, the team may assess whether longer-term CSF diversion, such as a shunt, is needed.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
For patients with an EVD in hospital, immediately alert the nurse or medical team if the drain disconnects, leaks, shifts, stops draining unexpectedly, or if there is a change in alertness, new weakness, seizure, worsening headache, vomiting, fever, or redness or drainage at the insertion site. Family members should not adjust or handle the drain. After discharge, follow the neurosurgical team's instructions and seek urgent care for new neurological symptoms, fever, wound drainage, or rapidly worsening headache or vomiting.
