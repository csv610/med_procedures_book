\chapter{Allergy Skin Testing}

\section*{Overview}
Allergy skin tests look for reactions to carefully selected substances that may trigger a person's symptoms. The result shows sensitization, which must be interpreted with the person's history and exposures; a positive result alone does not prove that the substance causes symptoms. The main methods are skin-prick, intradermal, and patch testing, which assess different types of allergic reaction.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item allergen extracts and positive/negative control solutions
  \item single-use skin-test lancets or prick devices
  \item skin marker and ruler for recording reactions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Skin-prick tests may help assess suspected immediate allergies, such as allergic rhinitis, selected allergic asthma triggers, food allergy, and insect-venom allergy. Intradermal testing is reserved for selected situations, including some venom or medication evaluations, and is not used for food allergy. Patch testing evaluates delayed allergic contact dermatitis, such as a rash caused by contact with a substance. Medication allergy testing is appropriate only for selected medicines and requires a clinician with relevant expertise.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
For skin-prick testing, small drops of selected allergen extracts and control solutions are placed on the forearm or back. A small lancet pricks the skin through each drop without drawing blood. The clinician reads and measures raised, itchy wheals, usually after 15 to 20 minutes, and compares them with the control reactions. Intradermal testing places a small amount of extract into the skin with a shallow injection; it is used selectively because it can produce more false-positive and systemic reactions. Patch testing uses patches containing suspected contact allergens, which are usually left on the skin for about 48 hours and read at later visits. The method depends on the suspected allergy.

\section*{Preparation}
Some antihistamines and other medicines can suppress skin reactions. The clinician will say which medicines to pause, if any, and for how long; do not stop prescribed medicines without instructions. Asthma inhalers and nasal steroid sprays generally do not need to be stopped for skin-prick testing. Tell the clinician about all medicines, asthma symptoms, pregnancy, extensive skin disease, and any previous severe allergic reaction. If medicines cannot safely be paused or the skin cannot be reliably tested, a blood test for allergen-specific IgE may be considered instead.

\section*{Risks and Results}
Temporary itching, redness, or swelling at test sites is common. Skin-prick testing rarely causes a reaction beyond the tested area; severe reactions are uncommon but possible, especially with some food or venom tests and intradermal testing. Testing should be performed by trained personnel with the ability to recognize and treat anaphylaxis. A positive result means sensitization, not necessarily clinical allergy. Results help guide care when considered with the history; negative results do not exclude every allergy or reaction mechanism. A supervised food or medication challenge may be needed to resolve selected uncertain cases, but is a separate procedure and must not be attempted at home.

\section*{Contraindications}
Uncontrolled asthma, a history of severe reactions, or medicines such as beta-blockers that may complicate treatment of anaphylaxis require an individualized safety assessment and may favor blood testing or postponement. Intradermal tests should be limited to appropriate indications; they should not be used to diagnose food allergy.

\section*{Precautions}
Extensive eczema or other active skin disease, dermatographism, and medicines that suppress skin responses can make results difficult to interpret. Pregnancy is not an automatic prohibition, but the clinician should weigh whether testing is necessary and whether the result will change care.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Tell the testing team immediately if you develop symptoms beyond the test sites, such as widespread hives, facial or throat swelling, trouble breathing, vomiting, dizziness, or faintness. After leaving, seek emergency care for breathing or throat symptoms, fainting, or any rapidly worsening reaction.
