\chapter{Lumbar Drain Placement}

\section*{Overview}
A lumbar drain is a temporary, thin catheter placed into the fluid-filled space around the nerves in the lower spine. It drains cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) through a closed collection system under close hospital monitoring. Drainage can reduce CSF pressure or divert fluid away from a leak. The amount and duration of drainage are set by the treating team for the specific reason it is being used.

\section*{Alternative Names}
External lumbar CSF drain, lumbar subarachnoid drain, continuous lumbar drainage

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A lumbar drain may be used to divert CSF for selected spinal or cranial CSF leaks, to manage CSF pressure in certain neurosurgical situations, or as part of an assessment of whether a person with suspected normal-pressure hydrocephalus may benefit from a shunt. The reason, expected benefit, alternatives, and plan for removal should be explained before placement.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Lumbar drainage is a temporary treatment or specialized diagnostic trial, not a routine test. It is used only when the clinical team has determined that CSF drainage is appropriate and safe. A single lumbar puncture, imaging, or other treatment may be preferred for some conditions.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is performed by a trained clinician using sterile technique, usually in a procedure room or operating room. The patient is positioned on their side or sitting, and the skin on the lower back is cleaned and numbed. A needle is passed between the lower back bones into the CSF space, and a thin catheter is threaded through it. The needle is removed, the catheter is secured and connected to a closed drainage system, and the site is covered with a sterile dressing. The catheter may remain in place for several days or for another period determined by the clinical plan.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews the reason for drainage, examination and imaging findings, allergies, infection risk, and medicines. Tell the team about anticoagulants, antiplatelet medicines, bleeding disorders, prior spinal surgery or deformity, skin infection, and any new severe headache or neurological symptoms. Blood tests or imaging may be needed depending on the situation. Do not stop medicines unless the treating team gives a specific plan. The team explains positioning and whether sedation or fasting is needed.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Lumbar drainage may be unsafe when there is a mass or blockage that creates a dangerous pressure difference in the brain, infection at the insertion site, or a bleeding risk that has not been addressed. Spinal anatomy, CSF flow obstruction, and the person's overall condition also affect the decision. The team uses examination and imaging when needed to assess safety. If a lumbar route is not suitable, another approach or treatment may be considered.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
Possible complications include headache from low CSF pressure, leakage around the catheter, bleeding or a blood collection around the spine, infection including meningitis, nerve irritation causing pain or tingling, and catheter blockage, disconnection, or displacement. Draining too much or too quickly can cause serious pressure changes, worsening headache, bleeding around the brain, or, rarely, brain herniation in a susceptible person. The team monitors closely to reduce these risks.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The patient remains in the hospital while the drain is in place. Staff monitor neurological status, symptoms, the insertion site, and the amount and appearance of drainage. The collection system must stay closed and be kept at the height and position ordered for that patient; staff may need to adjust or clamp it when the patient's position changes. Patients and visitors should not adjust, clamp, disconnect, or flush the system. Movement and head-of-bed instructions depend on the drainage order. The team removes the catheter when it is no longer needed and checks the site afterward.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The team assesses whether the intended goal is met, such as reducing CSF leakage or observing whether symptoms change during a drainage trial. Drainage volume alone does not establish success. If infection is suspected, CSF testing may be ordered; routine daily CSF sampling is not needed for every patient. The team discusses the results and whether further treatment, such as repair of a leak or a permanent shunt, is needed.

\section*{Expected Results}
The goal is controlled CSF drainage with improvement in the condition being treated and no serious complication. The drain may not resolve a leak or symptoms, and another treatment may be required.

\section*{Follow-up}
After removal, follow instructions about wound care, activity, and monitoring for headache or fluid leakage. Attend neurosurgery or other specialist follow-up to review the response and any further treatment plan.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
While the drain is in place, immediately tell the nurse or treating team about a severe or sudden headache, nausea or vomiting, unusual sleepiness, confusion, new weakness, numbness, vision or hearing changes, fever, neck stiffness, redness or fluid leaking at the insertion site, or a sudden change or stop in drainage. Do not manipulate the tubing. After removal or discharge, seek urgent care for severe headache that worsens upright or does not improve as instructed, fever, fluid leaking from the site, new neurological symptoms, or any rapidly worsening condition.
