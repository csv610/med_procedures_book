\chapter{Cervical Cerclage}

\section*{Overview}
Cervical cerclage, often called a cervical stitch, places a strong stitch around the cervix during pregnancy to help keep it closed. It may lower the chance of a late miscarriage or preterm birth for some people at increased risk. It does not prevent every early birth, and it is not recommended for every short cervix.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item speculum and surgical retractors
  \item suture material with needle holder and forceps
  \item ultrasound equipment when used to assess the cervix
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Cervical stitch; cervical suture; transvaginal cerclage; transabdominal cerclage.

\section*{Principle}
A stitch is placed around the cervix to give it support and reduce the chance that it will open too early. Most stitches are placed through the vagina. A less common abdominal stitch is placed through the abdomen and is usually used when a vaginal stitch is not suitable or has not worked.

\section*{History}
Modern vaginal cerclage techniques were developed in the 1950s by Victor Shirodkar and Ian McDonald.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Cerclage may be offered for different reasons:
\begin{itemize}
  \item In a singleton pregnancy, a history-indicated stitch may be offered after certain previous late miscarriages or very early preterm births linked to the cervix. It is usually placed at about 12 to 14 weeks of pregnancy.
  \item An ultrasound-guided stitch may be considered when someone with a relevant history has a short cervix on transvaginal ultrasound (often 25 mm or shorter), usually before 24 weeks. Vaginal progesterone may also be an option, and the choice depends on the person's history and preferences.
  \item An emergency stitch may be considered if the cervix has already started to open, while the membranes are still intact and there are no signs of infection or labor. This procedure has greater risks and may be less likely to work.
\end{itemize}

For an asymptomatic singleton pregnancy (one baby) without a previous spontaneous preterm birth, vaginal progesterone is recommended when a transvaginal ultrasound before 24 weeks shows a cervix 20 mm or shorter; it may also be considered at 21 to 25 mm. SMFM advises against a stitch for a cervix 10 to 25 mm long if it has not started to open. For a cervix shorter than 10 mm, evidence is limited and some specialists may consider a stitch. A 2026 focused meta-analysis suggests that benefit may extend to selected singleton pregnancies with a cervix 20.9 mm or shorter, but this newer evidence has not resolved differences in recommendations. A maternal-fetal medicine specialist can explain the evidence and options.

For a twin pregnancy, a stitch is not recommended solely because there are twins or because ultrasound shows a short cervix; SMFM and ACOG advise against routine ultrasound-indicated cerclage for twins. SMFM also advises against routine progesterone or a vaginal support device for twin pregnancies with a short cervix outside a clinical trial. ACOG's 2026 guideline recommends offering an examination-indicated stitch when the cervix has dilated in the second trimester and there is no labor, ruptured membranes, or intra-amniotic infection. Some specialists may also discuss a stitch for twins with a very short cervix (about 15 mm or shorter), but evidence for this ultrasound-indicated use is limited and does not establish it as routine care. A maternal-fetal medicine specialist can discuss the individual situation.

\section*{Role in Care}
Cerclage is a preventive or urgent treatment, not a test. History, examination, and transvaginal ultrasound help determine whether it may be useful. The stitch lowers risk for some people but cannot guarantee that pregnancy will continue to term.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
A vaginal stitch is usually placed in an operating room with spinal or general anesthesia. A speculum is used to see the cervix, and the clinician places and ties a strong suture around it. The procedure usually takes less than an hour. A stitch placed through the abdomen may be done by open or keyhole surgery, often before pregnancy or early in pregnancy. The method depends on the person's history and anatomy.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews pregnancy history and ultrasound findings and checks for contractions, bleeding, infection, and whether the membranes have broken. Fetal wellbeing and gestational age are considered. Tell the team about medicines, allergies, prior cervical procedures, and anesthesia concerns. Follow instructions about fasting and medicines before surgery.

\section*{Contraindications}
No specific absolute contraindication is identified here; the treating team assesses suitability for the individual.

\section*{Precautions}
If the cervix is already very short or widely open, placement may not be possible or may be less effective. A stitch is generally not placed when there are signs of infection, active labor or contractions, significant vaginal bleeding, or ruptured membranes, because it may add risk without helping the pregnancy. The obstetric team weighs risks and benefits for each case.

\section*{Risks}
Risks include bleeding, infection, injury to the cervix or nearby structures, contractions, and the waters breaking early. Anesthesia has its own risks. Even when placement goes well, the stitch may not prevent a late miscarriage or preterm birth. If labor starts while a vaginal stitch is still in place, the cervix can be injured, so the maternity team needs to know promptly.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After the procedure, the patient is monitored and may go home the same day or stay overnight. Light bleeding, spotting, or brown discharge and mild cramps may occur for a short time. The team may recommend rest while recovering from anesthesia, but routine bed rest for the rest of pregnancy is not recommended. Follow the maternity team's advice about activity, sex, and when to return for antenatal care.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The obstetric team arranges follow-up based on why the stitch was placed and the pregnancy's progress. Ultrasound monitoring may be part of the plan. A vaginal stitch is usually removed at about 36 to 37 weeks, or sooner if labor begins; if a planned cesarean is arranged, removal may occur then. If the waters break early, the team decides whether and when to remove the stitch. An abdominal stitch is usually left in place and birth is generally by cesarean.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the maternity unit urgently for contractions or repeated cramping, vaginal bleeding, a gush or ongoing leak of fluid, fever, or foul-smelling or unusual discharge. If labor may be starting while a vaginal stitch is in place, do not wait for a routine appointment; the stitch may need prompt removal to prevent cervical injury.
