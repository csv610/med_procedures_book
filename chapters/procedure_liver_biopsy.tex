\chapter{Liver Biopsy}

\section*{Overview}
A liver biopsy takes a small sample of liver tissue for examination under a microscope. It may help diagnose a liver condition, assess inflammation or scarring, or guide treatment when the result is expected to affect care. Blood tests, imaging, and noninvasive fibrosis tests can answer many questions, so biopsy is used when tissue information is still important. The route and preparation depend on the reason for biopsy and the person's health.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item ultrasound guidance and sterile probe cover
  \item biopsy needle and local-anesthetic supplies
  \item specimen container and monitoring equipment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Hepatic biopsy, percutaneous liver biopsy, transjugular liver biopsy

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A clinician may consider biopsy when liver test results remain unexplained, when noninvasive tests are uncertain or disagree, when the degree or cause of liver injury will change treatment, or when tissue from a liver lesion is needed for diagnosis. A biopsy may also be used in selected cases to assess a transplant or response to treatment. It is not needed for every person with abnormal liver tests or chronic liver disease.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Liver biopsy is an invasive diagnostic procedure, generally used after review of safer tests when the tissue result may guide diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment. The clinician should explain what question the biopsy is intended to answer, how the result could change care, and what alternatives are available.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The approach may be percutaneous (through the skin), transjugular (through a vein in the neck into a liver vein), or, in some centers, endoscopic ultrasound-guided. For a percutaneous biopsy, imaging such as ultrasound is used to locate the liver and select a safe path. The skin is cleaned and numbed, and a biopsy needle is passed through the skin to take one or more small tissue samples. The person may be asked to hold their breath briefly. For a transjugular biopsy, a catheter is guided through a neck vein to the liver, and samples are taken from inside a liver vein; this route may be preferred when bleeding risk or abdominal fluid makes a percutaneous approach less suitable. The sample is sent to a pathology laboratory.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews the reason for biopsy, medical history, allergies, medicines and supplements, and bleeding history. Blood tests or other checks may be arranged based on the clinical situation and planned route. Tell the team about anticoagulants, antiplatelet medicines, bleeding disorders, pregnancy or possible pregnancy, and prior reactions to anesthesia or contrast materials. Do not stop or restart any prescribed medicine, including blood thinners, without an individualized plan from the treating team. Follow instructions about fasting if sedation or anesthesia is planned, and arrange a ride home if you will receive sedation.

\section*{Contraindications}
No specific absolute contraindication is identified here; the treating team assesses suitability for the individual.

\section*{Precautions}
There is no single laboratory cutoff that determines safety for every liver biopsy. The team considers bleeding risk, liver function, platelet count and other relevant results, ascites (fluid in the abdomen), infection, anatomy, and the importance of obtaining tissue. A transjugular approach may be preferred for some people with significant ascites or increased bleeding risk. If the biopsy is not likely to change care or the risk is too high, another test or no biopsy may be recommended.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
Temporary soreness or pain at the biopsy site, in the right upper abdomen, or in the right shoulder can occur. Bleeding is the most important serious complication and may require hospital treatment, transfusion, or rarely another procedure. Other uncommon risks include infection, bile leakage, injury to a nearby organ, or a collapsed lung, depending on the route. The risk varies with the person's health and the biopsy approach. The care team should explain the risks relevant to the planned procedure.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After the biopsy, staff monitor the person's condition for a period set by the facility and clinical circumstances. The observation period and discharge plan vary by route and whether sedation was used. Follow instructions about rest, activity, wound care, pain relief, and when to resume medicines. Avoid strenuous activity and heavy lifting for the period advised by the team. Pathology results may take several days or longer.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The pathologist examines the sample for findings relevant to the clinical question, such as inflammation, fibrosis, fat-related injury, infection, medication-related injury, or tumor. A biopsy samples only a small part of the liver, so findings are interpreted with blood tests, imaging, medical history, and other results. The clinician will explain the report and whether it changes diagnosis or treatment.

\section*{Expected Results}
The result may confirm a suspected diagnosis, show the degree of inflammation or scarring, identify another cause, or be nondiagnostic if the sample is too small or does not represent the area of concern. There is no single expected “normal” result; interpretation depends on why the biopsy was done.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend the appointment or contact arranged to review pathology results and the care plan. Repeat biopsy is not automatic; noninvasive tests or imaging may be used for later monitoring, depending on the liver condition.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent medical help after biopsy for severe or persistent abdominal, right shoulder, or chest pain; dizziness, fainting, or marked weakness; fever; a racing heartbeat; shortness of breath; increasing abdominal swelling; vomiting; or bleeding at the biopsy site. Black, tarry stool or visible blood in stool also needs urgent assessment. Follow the discharge instructions and use local emergency services for severe or rapidly worsening symptoms.
