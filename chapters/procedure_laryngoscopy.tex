\chapter{Laryngoscopy}

\section*{Overview}
Laryngoscopy is an examination of the larynx (voice box), including the vocal folds. It may also show parts of the throat. The clinician uses a small mirror or a lighted scope. A flexible scope is commonly passed through the nose while the patient is awake; a rigid scope may be passed through the mouth in an operating room, often under general anesthesia. The method depends on the reason for the examination and whether a biopsy or treatment is planned.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Laryngeal examination, flexible laryngoscopy, nasolaryngoscopy, direct laryngoscopy

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Laryngoscopy helps evaluate persistent hoarseness or other voice changes, noisy breathing, swallowing or throat symptoms, a suspected growth or injury, and certain airway problems. It can show vocal-fold movement and the lining of the larynx. A clinician may take a tissue sample, remove an object, or treat a lesion during a direct laryngoscopy. For a new voice change that does not improve, laryngeal examination is recommended within four weeks; it may be needed sooner when a serious cause is suspected.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Laryngoscopy directly examines the larynx and is an important test for persistent voice symptoms. The finding is interpreted together with the person's history and examination. For a primary voice complaint, imaging such as CT or MRI is generally not recommended before the larynx has been examined. Additional tests may be needed depending on what is seen.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
For flexible laryngoscopy, the patient is usually seated. The clinician may use a decongestant or local anesthetic spray in the nose, then gently pass a thin flexible scope through a nostril to view the throat and larynx. The patient may be asked to speak, breathe, or make sounds so vocal-fold movement can be seen. The examination usually takes only a few minutes. A mirror or rigid scope may be used instead. Direct laryngoscopy in an operating room allows a closer examination and can permit biopsy or treatment; it commonly requires general anesthesia.

\section*{Preparation}
Preparation depends on the type of laryngoscopy. An awake office examination usually does not require fasting, but the clinic may give specific instructions, especially if throat-numbing spray is planned. Before an examination under general anesthesia, follow the anesthesia team's instructions about food, drink, and medicines. Tell the clinician about allergies, nosebleeds or nasal blockage, bleeding problems, medicines that affect bleeding, prior airway problems, and relevant medical conditions. Do not stop prescribed medicines unless instructed.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
There is no single set of conditions that rules out every type of laryngoscopy. Nasal injury, severe blockage, recent nasal surgery, bleeding risk, or intolerance of the scope may affect the approach. If a child or adult may have acute epiglottitis or another critical airway obstruction, routine examination outside a controlled setting can worsen distress and delay airway care; urgent assessment should be led by an experienced airway team in an appropriate setting. The clinician chooses the safest method for the situation.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
Flexible office laryngoscopy may briefly cause pressure, sneezing, gagging, coughing, watery eyes, or throat irritation. A small nosebleed can occur. Local anesthetic spray can temporarily numb the throat and affect safe swallowing. Direct laryngoscopy with anesthesia has additional risks, including dental or lip injury, bleeding, infection, vocal-fold injury, and complications of anesthesia. The risks depend on the method and any biopsy or treatment performed.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After an awake examination, most people return to usual activities promptly. If local anesthetic was sprayed into the throat, do not eat or drink until normal sensation and swallowing have returned, following the clinic's timing instructions (often about an hour). After general anesthesia, follow discharge instructions and arrange a ride home; activity restrictions depend on the procedure and medicines used. Mild throat soreness or hoarseness may occur briefly. Biopsy results, if a sample was taken, take additional time.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The clinician may explain the visual findings at the visit. Laryngoscopy can identify visible swelling, irritation, lesions, or problems with vocal-fold movement, but appearance alone may not establish the cause of every symptom. A biopsy is examined in a laboratory. Follow-up may include repeat examination, voice therapy after visualization and diagnosis, medication or surgery for a specific cause, or additional tests.

\section*{Expected Results}
The report describes the areas seen, vocal-fold movement, and any abnormality such as swelling, a lesion, or narrowing. A normal examination means no visible abnormality was found in the areas examined; it does not rule out every cause of voice, swallowing, or breathing symptoms.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow the clinician's plan for biopsy results or further evaluation. Seek review if symptoms persist or worsen, even if the initial examination was reassuring. Voice therapy should be based on an appropriate laryngeal examination and diagnosis.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
After the procedure, contact the care team for persistent or increasing bleeding, worsening pain, fever, or symptoms that do not improve as expected. Seek emergency care for trouble breathing, rapidly increasing throat or neck swelling, inability to swallow saliva, or heavy bleeding. If symptoms suggest an acute airway emergency, use local emergency services.
