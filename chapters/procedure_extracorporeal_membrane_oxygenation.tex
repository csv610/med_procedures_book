\chapter{ECMO}

\section*{Overview}
Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), also called extracorporeal life support (ECLS), is intensive life support for some people whose heart or lungs cannot provide enough circulation or oxygen. Blood flows through tubes to a pump and a membrane oxygenator outside the body. The oxygenator adds oxygen and removes carbon dioxide. ECMO gives the heart and/or lungs support while the team treats the underlying problem. It is not a cure, and it is used only when the potential benefit justifies the substantial risks.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Extracorporeal life support (ECLS); venovenous ECMO (VV-ECMO); venoarterial ECMO (VA-ECMO); extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR), when ECMO is started during cardiac arrest in selected circumstances.

\section*{Principle}
ECMO moves blood through a circuit containing a pump and an artificial lung, or oxygenator. VV-ECMO takes blood from and returns it to large veins. It supports gas exchange, while the patient's own heart continues to pump blood around the body and the ventilator may be adjusted to reduce strain on the lungs. VA-ECMO takes blood from a vein and returns it to an artery; it supports circulation as well as gas exchange. This can increase the resistance against which the heart's left pumping chamber works, so the team monitors for and manages this effect. ECMO does not replace all heart or lung functions.

\section*{History}
The first reported successful adult ECMO case was in 1972. The first successful neonatal case followed in 1975, helping establish ECMO as a form of support for severe heart or lung failure.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
ECMO may be considered for severe, potentially reversible heart or lung failure when usual treatments are not enough. Examples include severe respiratory failure, cardiogenic shock, and selected cases of cardiac arrest. It may be used as a bridge to recovery, a heart or lung transplant, another form of mechanical support, or a decision about next steps. It is not appropriate for every patient, and candidacy depends on the cause of illness, other health problems, likely recovery, treatment goals, and the ECMO team's assessment.

\section*{Role in Care}
ECMO is temporary mechanical support, not a diagnostic test or a treatment of the underlying cause. It is used alongside intensive care and treatments directed at the illness that caused heart or lung failure. The length of support varies and can be days or longer.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
An ECMO team places large tubes, called cannulas, into blood vessels, usually in the neck, chest, or groin. Cannulas may be placed through the skin or during surgery, depending on the patient and configuration. Blood flows through the circuit's pump and oxygenator and returns to the body. The patient is cared for in an intensive care unit and monitored continuously by a specialist team. Medicines to reduce clotting are often used, but the plan is adjusted to balance clotting and bleeding risks. Some patients need deep sedation; others may be awake or take part in carefully supervised rehabilitation.

\section*{Preparation}
ECMO is often an emergency treatment, so there may be little time for routine preparation. The team rapidly assesses the patient's condition, heart and lung function, blood vessels, bleeding and clotting risk, and the likely goals of support. Ultrasound or echocardiography may help guide cannula placement and evaluate the heart. When possible, the team discusses likely benefits, risks, and alternatives with the patient or their representative. Blood products, monitoring, and other intensive care supports may be prepared as needed.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
There is no single checklist that rules every patient in or out. ECMO may not help when the underlying condition cannot recover and there is no suitable next treatment, or when other illness makes survival with an acceptable outcome very unlikely. Severe bleeding risk or inability to use anticoagulation can affect the plan but is not automatically an exclusion in every case. Patient selection is individualized by an experienced multidisciplinary team and depends on the mode of ECMO and the reason for support.

\section*{Risks}
Serious complications can occur. Bleeding is common because anticoagulation is often needed to keep clots from forming in the circuit; bleeding in the brain or at cannula and surgical sites can be life-threatening. Other risks include clots or stroke, infection, damage to blood vessels, reduced blood flow to a limb with VA-ECMO, kidney or other organ injury, and red blood cell damage. The circuit can also clot, leak, or fail, so it requires continuous specialist monitoring.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The patient remains in intensive care while the team treats the underlying illness and monitors the circuit, cannula sites, blood flow, oxygen and carbon dioxide levels, bleeding, clotting, infection, and organ function. The team may adjust ventilator support, medicines, nutrition, and sedation. When appropriate, the patient may receive physical therapy or mobilization while on ECMO under close supervision. Support is reduced in a planned trial when the heart or lungs appear able to manage more work; the team removes the cannulas when support is no longer needed and it is safe to do so.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The outcome depends on the original illness, other organ injuries, the patient's health before ECMO, and whether the heart or lungs recover or another treatment is available. ECMO does not guarantee recovery or survival. The team reassesses goals and options throughout treatment. After ECMO, survivors may need rehabilitation and follow-up for physical, cognitive, emotional, or organ-related effects of critical illness.

\section*{Expected Results}
The immediate aim is to support circulation and/or gas exchange while treatment addresses the cause of failure. Success may mean recovery of heart or lung function, transition to another treatment such as a transplant or ventricular assist device, or achieving the patient's agreed goals of care. Some patients cannot be weaned from ECMO.

\section*{Follow-up}
After decannulation, care continues in hospital and may include rehabilitation, specialist follow-up, and assessment for lasting effects of critical illness. Follow-up needs vary by age, reason for ECMO, complications, and recovery. Families can ask the ECMO team what support and follow-up are available after discharge.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
ECMO patients are cared for in hospital by a trained team that monitors continuously. Family members should promptly alert bedside staff to any concern or sudden change. After discharge, follow the ICU and specialist team's instructions and seek urgent care for new severe breathing difficulty, chest pain, fainting, confusion, weakness, fever, or signs of infection.
