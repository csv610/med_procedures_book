\chapter{Embolectomy and Thrombectomy}

\section*{Overview}
Embolectomy and thrombectomy are procedures that remove material blocking a blood vessel. An embolus is material, often a clot, that has travelled from elsewhere; a thrombus is a clot that forms in the vessel. The terms are sometimes used together because the goal is to restore blood flow. Treatment may use a catheter, open surgery, or a combination, depending on the vessel, cause, urgency, and the patient's condition. These procedures are not interchangeable with medicines that dissolve clots.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Clot removal; surgical embolectomy; mechanical thrombectomy; endovascular thrombectomy; catheter thrombectomy. Catheter-directed thrombolysis uses clot-dissolving medicine and is a different, though sometimes related, treatment.

\section*{Principle}
The clinician reaches the blocked vessel and removes or extracts the clot using a catheter device, an open surgical technique, or both. Restoring flow may limit damage to the brain, limb, lung, or another affected organ, but it cannot reverse tissue injury that has already occurred and does not treat the cause of clot formation by itself.

\section*{History}
Early surgical attempts to remove pulmonary emboli date to the early 20th century. Modern catheter techniques, imaging, and clot-removal devices have expanded treatment options for selected strokes, acute limb ischemia, and other serious vascular blockages.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
The procedure depends on the clot's location and the patient's clinical risk:
\begin{itemize}
  \item For an acute ischemic stroke caused by a large-vessel blockage, endovascular thrombectomy may be offered to eligible patients after brain and blood-vessel imaging. Eligibility depends on factors such as the blocked artery, time from symptom onset or last known well, scan findings, and overall clinical condition. Selected patients may benefit when treated as late as 24 hours after they were last known to be well.
  \item Acute limb ischemia is an emergency because blood flow to an arm or leg has suddenly fallen. The team rapidly assesses whether the limb can be saved and usually starts anticoagulant medicine unless it is unsafe, while arranging urgent revascularization. Options include clot removal, catheter-directed clot-dissolving medicine, or other procedures.
  \item For a pulmonary embolism causing severe heart or lung strain, low blood pressure, or clinical deterioration, advanced treatment may be considered. Options include clot-dissolving medicine, catheter-based clot removal or medicine, and surgical embolectomy. The specialist team chooses based on severity, bleeding risk, and the person's condition; these procedures are not routine for stable pulmonary embolism.
  \item Thrombectomy for deep vein clots is reserved for selected situations; anticoagulant medicine is often the main treatment for deep vein thrombosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Role in Care}
Clot removal is a treatment. Imaging and examination first identify the blocked vessel, the extent of injury, and whether a procedure is likely to help. The specific intervention is only one part of care; medicines, treatment of the clot's cause, and rehabilitation may also be needed.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The method varies by vessel and indication. For endovascular treatment, a clinician inserts a catheter through an artery or vein, often in the groin or wrist, and guides it to the blockage using X-ray imaging. A suction catheter, a stent retriever, or another device may capture or remove clot. For open embolectomy, a surgeon exposes the affected vessel and removes clot through an incision. Anesthesia may be local, regional, or general. Not every procedure uses the same device or anesthesia, and clot-dissolving medicines may be used as a separate or additional treatment in selected cases.

\section*{Preparation}
These procedures are often urgent. The team checks symptoms and timing, examines the affected organ or limb, reviews medicines and bleeding risk, and uses tests such as CT, CT angiography, ultrasound, or angiography to guide the plan. Blood tests, an ECG, intravenous access, and consent discussions may be completed as time allows. Tell the team about allergies, kidney problems, blood thinners, bleeding disorders, prior stroke, recent surgery, or pregnancy. In an emergency, do not delay assessment to gather records or change medicines yourself.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Eligibility is specific to the vessel and treatment. For stroke thrombectomy, imaging must show an ischemic blockage that may benefit from removal; this procedure does not treat bleeding in the brain. Time alone does not determine eligibility: imaging and clinical features matter. For limb ischemia, the team assesses whether the limb is still salvageable. Bleeding risk, kidney function, vessel anatomy, other illnesses, and the risks of anesthesia or contrast influence the plan but do not all act as automatic exclusions.

\section*{Risks}
Risks depend on the procedure and site. They may include bleeding or bruising where the catheter enters, damage or tearing of a blood vessel, clot fragments moving downstream, blockage returning, contrast reaction, or kidney injury associated with contrast in susceptible patients. Stroke thrombectomy can cause bleeding in the brain, vessel injury, or reperfusion injury. Pulmonary procedures can strain or injure the heart or lung vessels. Surgery and anesthesia have additional risks. The care team explains the risks relevant to the individual procedure.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After the procedure, staff monitor blood pressure, the access site, blood flow to the affected area, and signs of bleeding or complications. The monitoring and activity restrictions depend on the access route and treatment. Medicines to prevent future clots may be started or adjusted when appropriate, but not every patient needs the same antiplatelet or anticoagulant plan. Recovery may also include stroke rehabilitation, wound care, or treatment of the condition that caused the blockage.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Imaging, examination, or both may be used to check blood flow. Success does not guarantee that all tissue will recover; the result depends on how much tissue was injured before flow returned and the underlying cause. The clinical team may assess the heart, blood vessels, heart rhythm, or clotting conditions to reduce the chance of another event.

\section*{Expected Results}
The aim is to improve or restore blood flow and protect organ or limb function. The vessel may not fully reopen, and some patients need additional medicine, another procedure, surgery, or rehabilitation. Outcomes vary by indication and how quickly treatment can be provided.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow-up is tailored to the clot's cause and location. It may include antiplatelet or anticoagulant medicine, risk-factor treatment, repeat imaging, vascular or neurology visits, and rehabilitation. Do not stop blood-thinning medicine without advice from the treating clinician.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
After discharge, seek urgent care for new weakness, facial droop, trouble speaking, sudden vision loss, a cold or painful limb, new chest pain or breathing difficulty, fainting, uncontrolled bleeding, or rapidly increasing swelling at the access site. Call emergency services for possible stroke, severe breathing difficulty, or severe chest pain.
