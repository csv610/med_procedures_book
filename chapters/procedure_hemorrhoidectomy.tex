\chapter{Hemorrhoidectomy}

\section*{Overview}
Hemorrhoidectomy is an operation to remove hemorrhoidal tissue. It is most often considered for bothersome external hemorrhoids or symptomatic combined internal and external hemorrhoids that prolapse, particularly more advanced disease or when other treatments have not helped. Pain during recovery, especially with bowel movements, is common.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Excisional hemorrhoidectomy, hemorrhoid removal surgery

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Excisional hemorrhoidectomy is generally offered to selected people with external hemorrhoids or symptomatic combined internal and external hemorrhoids, especially grades III–IV. It may also be considered for internal hemorrhoids when office treatments have failed, cannot be used, or are not suitable. Surgery is not needed for most hemorrhoids. Rectal bleeding should be evaluated rather than automatically attributed to hemorrhoids; other conditions can also cause bleeding.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Hemorrhoidectomy is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. It is generally considered after assessment and discussion of nonsurgical and office-based options. The best treatment depends on whether the symptoms come from internal, external, or combined hemorrhoids, their severity, and the person's health and preferences.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The operation is usually performed under anesthesia, often as day surgery. The surgeon removes selected hemorrhoidal tissue and controls bleeding. The wound may be left open to heal or closed with stitches, depending on the technique. Stapled hemorrhoidopexy is a different operation: it removes a ring of tissue inside the rectum to lift prolapsing internal hemorrhoids, but does not treat external hemorrhoids. Because it has different benefits and risks, it is not routinely recommended as the first surgical treatment for internal hemorrhoids. The surgeon explains which procedure is planned.

\section*{Preparation}
The surgical team reviews health conditions, medicines, allergies, and the source and severity of symptoms. Tell them about anticoagulants, antiplatelet medicines, bleeding disorders, and prior anorectal surgery. Do not stop prescribed medicines without a plan from the prescribing and surgical teams. Follow the clinic's instructions about bowel preparation, fasting, and anesthesia. Arrange a ride home and support during the early recovery period if needed.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
The surgeon considers active infection, bleeding risk, other medical conditions that affect anesthesia or healing, and whether the symptoms are truly caused by hemorrhoids. Anticoagulant and antiplatelet plans must be individualized. If there is rectal bleeding, the clinician may recommend examination or other tests to check for another cause, depending on age, history, and symptoms.

\section*{Risks}
Possible risks include significant pain, bleeding, infection, difficulty urinating, constipation, and delayed wound healing. Less common complications include narrowing of the anal opening, recurrence, or changes in bowel control. The chance of each problem depends on the operation and the person's health.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Pain and soreness are expected, and bowel movements may be uncomfortable for a time. The team will provide a pain-control plan and instructions for wound care, bathing or sitz baths, activity, and bowel habits. Fluids, fiber, and any recommended stool-softening medicine can help reduce constipation and straining. Do not take laxatives or change medicines unless they are part of the care plan. Recovery often takes about two to four weeks, but varies; resume normal activities gradually as comfort and healing allow, following the surgeon's advice.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The goal is to relieve symptoms and remove the problematic tissue. Most people improve, but surgery does not prevent future hemorrhoids if constipation or straining continues. The surgeon checks healing and addresses ongoing pain, bleeding, or bowel problems at follow-up.

\section*{Expected Results}
The procedure often reduces prolapse, irritation, and bleeding, but it can involve two to four weeks of discomfort, sometimes longer. A small amount of blood with bowel movements may occur during healing; heavy or persistent bleeding is not expected and needs medical advice.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend follow-up as instructed, especially if symptoms are not steadily improving. Continue bowel-habit measures recommended by the care team. Ask when it is safe to return to work, driving, exercise, and strenuous activity.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent care for heavy bleeding or blood clots, inability to urinate, fever or chills, worsening severe pain, or feeling faint or very unwell. Contact the surgical team for increasing wound redness, swelling, drainage, persistent bleeding, or constipation that does not respond to the prescribed plan. Sudden chest pain or trouble breathing requires emergency care.
