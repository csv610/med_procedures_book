\chapter{Fine Needle Aspiration}

\section*{Overview}
Fine needle aspiration (FNA) is a biopsy that uses a thin needle and syringe to collect cells or fluid from an area of concern. A pathologist examines the sample, usually under a microscope. FNA can help diagnose a lump, cyst, enlarged lymph node, or other abnormal area. It is less invasive than an operation, but it does not answer every diagnostic question.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item fine needle and syringe or aspiration device
  \item ultrasound guidance for accessible targets when used
  \item slides, specimen vials, and labeling supplies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Fine-needle aspiration biopsy, FNA biopsy, aspiration cytology

\section*{Principle}
The clinician guides a thin needle into the target and gently draws cells or fluid into the needle. The sample is placed on slides or in a container for laboratory testing. The lab may also use special stains, cell blocks, or other tests when appropriate. FNA usually provides cells rather than a larger piece of tissue, so it may not show how cells are arranged within the tissue.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
FNA may be used to investigate an abnormality in places such as the thyroid, lymph nodes, breast, salivary glands, lung, or other organs. It can help distinguish a fluid-filled cyst from a solid lump, identify some infections or other diseases, and look for cancer cells. Whether FNA is the right test depends on the target, imaging findings, and the clinical question. For a suspicious solid breast lump, a core needle biopsy is often preferred because it provides a larger tissue sample; FNA may still be used for a fluid-filled cyst or selected lymph nodes. Other abnormalities may also be better evaluated with a core biopsy or another procedure.

\section*{Role in Care}
FNA can be an initial tissue-sampling test for selected abnormalities, or a follow-up test after examination or imaging. The care team chooses the sampling method based on the site and what information is needed. A core needle biopsy takes a larger tissue sample and may be preferred when tissue structure is important.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure depends on where the target is. For an area close to the skin, the clinician may feel and steady the lump. Ultrasound, CT, mammography, or an endoscope may guide the needle for a small or deeper target. The skin is cleaned, and local anesthetic may be used. The clinician passes the needle into the target, sometimes more than once, to collect enough material. The sample is sent to a laboratory. The procedure commonly takes several minutes, but preparation, imaging, and recovery can take longer.

\section*{Preparation}
Instructions vary with the target and the type of guidance or sedation planned. Tell the care team about all medicines and supplements, especially anticoagulants or antiplatelet medicines, bleeding problems, allergies, and possible pregnancy. Do not stop prescribed blood-thinning medicine unless the clinician who manages it gives you a plan. Fasting may be needed if sedation or anesthesia is planned. Ask whether you need someone to take you home.

\section*{Contraindications}
There is no single list of contraindications for every FNA. Pregnancy should be discussed if imaging that uses ionizing radiation or sedation is planned; ultrasound does not use ionizing radiation.

\section*{Precautions}
The team weighs the value of sampling against bleeding risk, the target's location, and nearby structures. A bleeding disorder, medicines that affect clotting, or a target near major blood vessels may require a different approach or extra planning. Some uncommon lesions require specialist assessment before needle sampling. Image guidance may be used when needed to reach the target safely.

\section*{Risks}
Risks depend on the body site, needle path, and any sedation. Possible effects include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Brief pain or tenderness, bruising, or minor bleeding at the puncture site.
    \item Infection, which is uncommon.
    \item Faintness or nausea during or shortly after the procedure.
    \item Injury to a nearby structure. For sampling in or near the chest, this can include a collapsed lung (pneumothorax); this is not a general risk for FNA at other sites.
    \item A sample that is too small, unclear, or inconsistent with imaging or examination. Another FNA, a core biopsy, follow-up imaging, or surgery may then be recommended.
    \item Complications related to sedation, if sedation is used.
\end{itemize}
Ask the clinician about risks that apply to the specific target and planned route.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people can go home soon after a superficial FNA. The team may apply pressure and a small dressing. Mild soreness or bruising can occur; follow the team's instructions about activity, wound care, and pain relief. Recovery and observation may take longer after a deep, endoscopic, or sedated procedure.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
A pathologist interprets the sample and sends a report to the clinician who ordered the test. The report may describe the sample as adequate or insufficient and may classify findings as benign, atypical, suspicious, or malignant. Reporting systems vary by organ; for example, thyroid samples may use the Bethesda System. Results do not stand alone: the clinician compares them with examination and imaging. A benign result may still need follow-up if it does not fit the other findings. An insufficient or uncertain result may lead to repeat sampling or a different test. The timing for results varies by laboratory and any additional tests required.

\section*{Expected Results}
FNA has no general normal range. The meaning of a result depends on the target and the reason for testing. A benign result means that the sampled cells did not show cancer, but it may not rule out disease elsewhere in the target or a sampling error. The ordering clinician should explain the result and whether it matches the examination and imaging.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow-up may include no further testing, clinical or imaging surveillance, repeat FNA, core needle biopsy, or surgical biopsy. The plan depends on the pathology report and how well it matches the clinical and imaging findings.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Follow the procedure team's instructions. Contact the team promptly for increasing redness, swelling, drainage, fever, worsening pain, or bleeding that does not stop with the recommended pressure. Seek urgent care for trouble breathing, chest pain, fainting that does not quickly resolve, or another severe or rapidly worsening symptom. These chest symptoms are especially important after a procedure involving the chest.
