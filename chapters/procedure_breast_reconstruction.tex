\chapter{Breast Reconstruction}

\section*{Overview}
Breast reconstruction is surgery to restore the shape of a breast after mastectomy. It can also reshape the breast after breast-conserving surgery (oncoplastic surgery), or address some differences present from birth or caused by injury. Reconstruction is optional. Choices can include an implant, the person's own tissue, a combination of both, an external breast form, or choosing to remain flat.

Reconstruction restores breast shape, but it does not recreate a natural breast. After mastectomy, reduced or absent sensation is common. Some sensation may return over time, but it is not guaranteed. More than one operation may be needed, and later revisions are common.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Breast reconstruction surgery; postmastectomy reconstruction; implant reconstruction; autologous tissue or flap reconstruction; oncoplastic breast surgery.

\section*{Principle}
The breast mound may be rebuilt with an implant, tissue moved from another part of the body (a flap), or both. Surgery may also adjust the other breast to improve symmetry. A nipple and areola may be preserved, reconstructed, or recreated later with surgery or tattooing, depending on the cancer operation and the person's preference.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Some people choose reconstruction to restore body shape or symmetry after breast removal. Others prefer an external prosthesis or no reconstruction. No option is medically required for everyone. A decision about reconstruction should not delay necessary cancer treatment; the breast cancer and reconstructive teams coordinate the timing.

\section*{Role in Treatment}
Breast reconstruction is a reconstructive procedure, not a diagnostic test. Reconstruction itself does not treat breast cancer or prevent recurrence. When oncoplastic surgery is used, it is combined with the operation that removes the tumor. The need for chemotherapy, radiation, hormone therapy, or other cancer care is decided by the cancer team.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The operation depends on the method and can be done at the same time as mastectomy (immediate reconstruction) or in a later operation (delayed reconstruction). Some reconstruction is staged over several procedures.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Implant reconstruction:} The surgeon may place a permanent implant during mastectomy (direct-to-implant) or first place a temporary tissue expander under the skin or chest muscle. The expander is gradually filled over visits to stretch the tissue, then exchanged for an implant. Implants may be saline-filled or silicone gel-filled.
  \item \textbf{Autologous tissue reconstruction:} The surgeon moves skin, fat, and sometimes muscle from another area, often the abdomen, back, thigh, or buttock, to create the breast shape. In a free flap, the surgeon reconnects the tissue's blood vessels using microsurgery. A pedicled flap stays attached to its original blood supply as it is moved to the chest.
  \item \textbf{Combined reconstruction:} A flap may be used with an implant when more tissue or volume is needed.
  \item \textbf{Oncoplastic surgery:} During breast-conserving surgery, the surgeon reshapes breast tissue or uses local tissue rearrangement to fill or reduce a surgical defect. This is different from rebuilding an entire breast after mastectomy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Preparation}
The breast surgeon, plastic surgeon, and cancer team discuss the cancer operation, likely additional treatment, reconstruction options, timing, and the person's goals. Planning considers body shape, available donor tissue, previous operations or radiation, smoking or nicotine use, diabetes and other health conditions, and recovery support. The team reviews medicines, allergies, anesthesia risks, and whether further breast imaging or other assessment is needed.

Ask about expected appearance and sensation, scars, drains, time in hospital, recovery, possible staged operations, and what may happen if radiation or chemotherapy is needed. Do not stop prescribed medicines, including blood thinners, unless the treating team gives a plan. Follow instructions about fasting and medicines before surgery.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
There is no single approach that suits everyone. Some medical conditions, smoking, poor wound healing, limited donor tissue, prior operations, or radiation exposure may increase risk or make a particular method less suitable. Radiation before or after reconstruction can affect healing, implant complications, appearance, or symmetry, but it does not automatically rule out all reconstruction. The cancer and reconstructive teams weigh these factors and may recommend immediate, delayed, or staged reconstruction, or another option.

\section*{Risks}
Risks of any reconstruction include bleeding, infection, blood clots, anesthesia complications, wound separation or slow healing, scarring, pain, changes in sensation, asymmetry, and further surgery. Reconstruction may fail or need revision.

Implant reconstruction can lead to fluid or blood collection, implant infection or exposure, rupture, movement, rippling, or capsular contracture (tight scar tissue around the implant). Implants are not lifetime devices and may later need removal or replacement. Breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL) is a rare lymphoma associated with breast implants, with higher risk for textured implants. The FDA has also received reports of squamous cell carcinoma and other lymphomas in implant capsules, and reports of mesenchymal tumors, including sarcomas, in people with implants. These cancers appear to be rare; their causes and rates are not known.

Some people with breast implants report systemic symptoms such as fatigue, difficulty concentrating, or joint and muscle pain, sometimes called breast implant illness. The causes are not well understood, and a causal link to implants has not been established.

Flap reconstruction can lead to poor blood flow or partial or complete loss of transferred tissue. It also creates a wound at the tissue donor site and can cause weakness, pain, or other problems there. The specific risks depend on the flap and operation.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Recovery depends on the operation. Implant or flap reconstruction may require a hospital stay, wound care, drains, and limits on lifting and strenuous activity. Flap patients are monitored closely after surgery to check blood flow to the transferred tissue. The team explains pain control, drain care, bathing, activity, and when to seek help. Recovery is often longer after flap surgery because both the breast and donor area need to heal. Tissue-expander reconstruction requires follow-up visits for gradual filling and may be followed by another operation to exchange the expander.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The shape and symmetry can change over time with healing, aging, weight changes, or cancer treatment. Follow-up addresses healing, implant or flap status, sensation, body image, and whether additional procedures are desired. Keep implant identification information and discuss device monitoring with the surgeon. For silicone gel implants, FDA guidance recommends ultrasound or MRI to check for silent rupture starting 5 to 6 years after placement and every 2 to 3 years after that; follow the implant labeling and local guidance.

Continue the breast cancer surveillance plan from the cancer team. After mastectomy with implant reconstruction, routine screening mammography is generally not performed on the reconstructed side, but the other breast may still need screening. After mastectomy with reconstruction using the person's own tissue, the American College of Radiology says mammography or tomosynthesis may be appropriate for some people with a breast cancer history; recommendations can vary, so follow the care team's plan. After breast-conserving surgery, mammography of the treated breast remains part of follow-up. The care team may recommend diagnostic imaging for new symptoms or other specific clinical reasons.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
After surgery, contact the surgical team promptly for fever, worsening redness or warmth, increasing pain or swelling, wound opening, or unusual drainage. After flap reconstruction, call the team immediately if the flap becomes pale, cool, purple, or rapidly swollen, as this may signal a blood-flow problem. Seek emergency care for severe breathing difficulty, chest pain, fainting, or heavy bleeding. After implant reconstruction, report a new persistent swelling, mass, or pain around the implant, even if it occurs years later.
