\chapter{Cataract Surgery}

\section*{Overview}
A cataract is clouding of the eye's natural lens. Cataract surgery removes the cloudy lens and replaces it with a clear artificial lens called an intraocular lens (IOL). The goal is to improve vision when cataracts make everyday activities difficult. Most operations are done as day surgery with numbing eye drops or a local anesthetic; the person is usually awake, though medicine to relax them may be offered. General anesthesia is used in selected cases.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Cataract extraction; cataract removal; phacoemulsification with intraocular lens implantation.

\section*{Principle}
The cloudy natural lens is removed while the thin capsule that held it is usually left in place. An artificial lens is positioned inside this capsule to focus light on the retina. The IOL's focusing power is measured before surgery. A monofocal IOL focuses at one distance, usually far, so glasses may be needed for reading. A toric IOL can correct some corneal astigmatism, a type of blur related to the uneven curve of the cornea. Multifocal or extended-depth-of-focus lenses can provide a wider range of focus and reduce the need for glasses, but some people still need glasses and may notice glare or halos, especially at night; some may also have reduced contrast. Guidelines differ on whether multifocal lenses should be offered, so discuss the trade-offs and local options with the surgeon. The best choice depends on eye health, visual needs, and preferences.

\section*{History}
Modern cataract surgery developed from procedures that removed the cloudy lens. Phacoemulsification, which uses ultrasound to break up the lens through a small incision, became a widely used technique in the late twentieth century.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Surgery is considered when cataracts interfere with activities such as reading, driving, work, or recognizing faces and glasses no longer provide enough improvement. The decision is based on the person's vision, needs, eye health, and preferences; there is no single level of visual acuity at which everyone must have surgery.

\section*{Role in Care}
This is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. An eye examination confirms that a cataract is contributing to the vision problem and checks for other eye conditions that could affect the result. Surgery may not restore vision lost from conditions such as macular degeneration, glaucoma, or diabetic eye disease.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The eye is numbed with drops or an injection around the eye. The surgeon makes a small opening at the edge of the cornea, the clear front surface of the eye. In the common phacoemulsification method, an ultrasound instrument breaks the cloudy lens into small pieces, which are removed. The foldable IOL is then placed inside the remaining lens capsule. The small incision usually seals without stitches, and a protective shield may be placed over the eye. Laser-assisted steps are an option in some settings but are not required for cataract surgery.

\section*{Preparation}
The team examines the eye and measures its shape and length to choose the IOL power. Tell the eye team about all medicines, allergies, prior eye surgery, and health conditions. Do not stop blood thinners or change other medicines unless the treating team gives you a specific plan. Fasting instructions depend on the anesthesia and facility; follow the instructions you receive. Arrange a ride home because vision may be blurred and sedating medicine may affect alertness.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
An active eye infection or significant inflammation may need treatment before elective surgery. Other eye disease, difficulty lying still or flat, or health conditions affecting anesthesia may change the plan or expected benefit. These issues do not automatically rule out surgery; the ophthalmologist discusses the options and likely result.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary blurring, mild discomfort, light sensitivity, or inflammation can occur during recovery. Less common complications include infection inside the eye, bleeding, increased eye pressure, swelling of the retina, a tear in the lens capsule, or movement of the artificial lens. Retinal detachment is uncommon but can threaten vision. The chance of complications depends on eye health and the complexity of surgery.

Months or years later, the capsule behind the IOL may become cloudy (posterior capsule opacification). This is not a new cataract; it can usually be treated with a brief laser procedure. Glasses may still be needed, and the final focus may differ from the target.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people go home the same day. Vision may be blurry at first and usually improves over days or weeks. Use prescribed eye drops and any eye shield as directed. Avoid rubbing or pressing on the eye. Do not drive until your vision is good enough and your eye team says it is safe. Follow the team's instructions about bathing, exercise, and lifting; restrictions depend on the operation and the person's recovery. Attend all follow-up visits, even if vision seems good.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Most people see better after surgery, but the amount of improvement depends on the health of the retina, optic nerve, and other parts of the eye, as well as the cataract. Some people still need glasses for reading, distance, or both. The eye team checks healing and vision and advises when an updated glasses prescription is appropriate. If both eyes need surgery, the timing is planned by the treating team.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the eye surgeon or urgent eye service immediately for sudden or rapidly worsening vision, severe or increasing eye pain, marked redness, increasing light sensitivity, or sticky discharge. New flashes of light, many new floaters, or a dark curtain or shadow in the vision need urgent assessment because they can signal retinal detachment. Use local emergency services if urgent eye care cannot be reached.
