\chapter{In Vitro Fertilization}

\section*{Overview}
In vitro fertilization (IVF) is a fertility treatment in which eggs are collected from the ovaries and fertilized with sperm in a laboratory. One embryo may then be transferred to the uterus; suitable embryos may also be frozen for later use. IVF is an effective treatment for many, but not all, causes of infertility. The chance of success varies between individuals and treatment cycles.

\section*{Alternative Names}
IVF; in vitro fertilisation (UK spelling)

\section*{Principle}
Medicines stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs. The eggs are collected from ovarian follicles under ultrasound guidance and fertilized in the laboratory, either by placing sperm with the eggs or, in selected cases, by injecting a single sperm into an egg (intracytoplasmic sperm injection, or ICSI). Embryos are monitored as they develop. An embryo may be transferred to the uterus in the same cycle or after freezing and thawing in a later cycle. The number transferred is decided with the fertility team; transferring one embryo is often recommended to reduce multiple pregnancy.

\section*{History}
The first live birth following IVF, Louise Brown, occurred in 1978 in the United Kingdom. Later advances included embryo freezing and ICSI. Genetic testing of embryos is an option in selected situations; it is not part of every IVF cycle.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
IVF may be considered for blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, some forms of male-factor infertility, endometriosis, or unexplained infertility, often after other suitable treatments have not worked. It may also be used when embryos are tested for a specific inherited condition, with donor eggs or sperm, or to preserve eggs or embryos before medical treatment that may affect fertility. The recommended approach depends on the cause of infertility, the person's health and goals, and local laws and clinic practices.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
IVF is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. It may be offered as an initial treatment for some causes of infertility or after other treatments, such as intrauterine insemination, have not worked. It is not the best or necessary treatment for everyone.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The process and timing vary by treatment plan. Medicines are used to stimulate the ovaries, with ultrasound scans and sometimes blood tests to monitor the response. Some protocols also use medicines to prevent eggs from being released too early. When the eggs are ready, a trigger medicine is given at a precisely timed interval before retrieval. The clinician collects eggs by passing a needle through the vaginal wall into the ovarian follicles, usually with sedation or anesthesia. Sperm is collected or donor sperm is prepared. In the laboratory, sperm is placed with the eggs or ICSI is used when indicated; ICSI is not needed in every cycle. Embryos are observed for several days. A selected embryo can be placed in the uterus through the cervix with a thin catheter, either in the same cycle or after freezing and thawing. The team may recommend freezing all suitable embryos and delaying transfer, for example, to reduce the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS). Suitable embryos not transferred may be frozen if the patient consents and clinic policies allow.

\section*{Preparation}
Before treatment, the team reviews fertility history, ovarian reserve, uterine health, semen or donor information, medical conditions, and medicines. Blood tests and screening for infections may be required by the clinic or local rules. The clinic explains medication timing, consent for use and storage of eggs or embryos, costs, and available support. Follow instructions about medicines and fasting: fasting may be needed before egg retrieval if sedation or anesthesia is planned. A full bladder may be requested for some embryo transfers, particularly when ultrasound guidance is used, but it is not a standard requirement for egg retrieval. Discuss smoking, alcohol, supplements, and prescription medicines with the team; do not stop or change medicines on your own.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
There is no single list of contraindications for every IVF treatment plan. An untreated infection may delay egg retrieval or embryo transfer. A uterine condition may need evaluation or treatment before an embryo is transferred. A health condition that makes pregnancy unsafe may lead the team to recommend treatment of the condition first, another option such as a gestational carrier where lawful and appropriate, or not attempting pregnancy. The team assesses the risk of OHSS and blood clots and discusses the chance and risks of multiple pregnancy before treatment.

\section*{Risks}
Risks include OHSS, which can cause painful ovarian swelling and fluid buildup and, rarely, serious illness or blood clots; bleeding, infection, or injury to nearby structures during egg retrieval; and complications of sedation or anesthesia. A pregnancy after IVF can be ectopic. Transferring more than one embryo increases the chance of twins or higher-order multiple pregnancy, which raises risks such as premature birth and complications for the pregnant person and babies. A cycle may not produce eggs, embryos, or a pregnancy, and pregnancy loss can occur. The emotional, time, and financial burdens can be substantial. The clinic can discuss ways to reduce risk, including a single-embryo transfer and, for people at higher risk of OHSS, freezing embryos for later transfer.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After egg retrieval, temporary cramping, bloating, or light spotting can occur; follow the clinic's instructions about activity, pain relief, and when to seek advice. Bed rest is not needed after embryo transfer. Most people can resume usual light activities unless the clinic advises otherwise. Take progesterone or other prescribed support exactly as directed. The clinic will set the date for a pregnancy test, commonly about 9–14 days after transfer; do not stop prescribed medicines early unless the clinic instructs you to.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The clinic interprets the pregnancy test and advises when to have an ultrasound, often around 6–7 weeks of pregnancy, to confirm the pregnancy's location and development. A positive test does not by itself confirm an ongoing or normally located pregnancy. If the test is negative, the team can discuss the result, emotional support, and options such as a frozen embryo transfer or another treatment cycle, if appropriate.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no guaranteed result. A cycle may result in no eggs, no embryos suitable for transfer or freezing, a negative pregnancy test, pregnancy loss, or a pregnancy and birth. The chance of a live birth depends on factors such as age when the eggs were collected, the cause of infertility, prior treatment, and the number and development of embryos. Ask the clinic for an estimate that reflects the outcome measure and individual circumstances.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow the clinic's plan for pregnancy testing, ultrasound, and any medication support. Once pregnancy care is transferred, continue routine prenatal care. If treatment does not result in pregnancy, the fertility team can review what happened and discuss whether another transfer or cycle is suitable.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the fertility clinic promptly for worsening abdominal pain or swelling, persistent vomiting, rapid weight gain, much less or darker urine, fever, or heavy bleeding after egg retrieval. These may be signs of OHSS or a procedure complication. Seek emergency care for difficulty breathing, chest pain, fainting, or severe weakness. After embryo transfer or a positive pregnancy test, severe one-sided lower abdominal pain, shoulder-tip pain, heavy bleeding, or dizziness/fainting needs urgent assessment because it may signal an ectopic pregnancy or internal bleeding.
