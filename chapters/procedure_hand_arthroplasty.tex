\chapter{Hand Arthroplasty}

\section*{Overview}
Hand arthroplasty is surgery to reconstruct or replace a damaged joint in the hand or fingers. This chapter focuses on finger-joint replacement, most often at the knuckle (metacarpophalangeal) or middle finger joint (proximal interphalangeal). The operation and implant depend on the joint and the person's needs. Thumb-base (carpometacarpal) arthritis is often treated with a different operation, such as trapeziectomy (removal of the trapezium, a small wrist bone), sometimes with tendon or ligament reconstruction. This differs from replacing a finger joint with an implant.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Finger-joint replacement, small-joint arthroplasty, metacarpophalangeal joint replacement, proximal interphalangeal joint replacement

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Joint replacement may be considered when osteoarthritis, inflammatory arthritis such as rheumatoid arthritis, or a prior injury causes lasting pain, deformity, or difficulty using the hand despite nonsurgical care such as medicines, splints, activity changes, injections, or therapy. It may suit someone who wants to retain some joint motion. A joint fusion, which joins the bones and removes motion at that joint, may be a better option when stability or durability is more important. The right choice depends on which joint is affected, bone and soft-tissue condition, hand use, and the person's health and goals.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Arthroplasty is an elective treatment considered after evaluation and, in many cases, nonsurgical treatment. It is not appropriate for every painful hand joint. The surgeon discusses alternatives and the likely balance between pain relief, motion, stability, and implant durability.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The operation is usually done with regional anesthesia, general anesthesia, or a combination. The surgeon makes an incision over the joint, removes the damaged joint surfaces, prepares the bone, and places an implant selected for that joint. Implant designs and materials vary; some are flexible spacers and others have separate components. Nearby tendons, ligaments, or other soft tissues may be repaired or balanced. The incision is closed and the hand is dressed and often placed in a splint. The details differ by joint and implant.

\section*{Preparation}
Before surgery, the surgeon examines the hand and reviews X-rays or other imaging, joint stability, tendon function, skin condition, and the effect of symptoms on daily tasks. Tell the team about medicines, allergies, medical conditions, smoking, and any skin infection or wound. Follow instructions about fasting and medicines; do not stop prescribed medicines without a plan. Arrange help for activities that may be difficult while the operated hand is bandaged or splinted.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Active infection, poor skin or soft-tissue coverage, insufficient bone to support an implant, severe joint instability, or inability to follow rehabilitation may make replacement unsuitable or require a different plan. The surgeon also considers the person's activity needs and expectations. Smoking, diabetes, inflammatory disease, and medicines affecting healing may change surgical or recovery risks and should be discussed with the care team.

\section*{Risks}
Risks include infection, bleeding, stiffness, persistent pain, tendon or nerve injury, instability, dislocation, implant loosening or breakage, bone loss or fracture around the implant, and the need for revision surgery. Replacement may relieve pain but may not increase motion; the amount of motion often remains similar to what the person had before surgery. Some activities, especially forceful or heavy gripping, may be limited to protect the joint.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The hand is protected with a dressing and a splint. Elevation and prescribed pain control can help with early swelling and discomfort. Hand therapy is usually an important part of recovery and may start within days or weeks, depending on the joint, implant, and soft-tissue repairs. A therapist guides safe movement and later strengthening while protecting the reconstruction. Follow the surgeon's and therapist's instructions; moving too little can cause stiffness, while stressing the joint too soon can harm the repair. Recovery takes weeks to months, and improvement may continue for up to a year.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The main goal is pain relief and useful function, with some motion preserved when possible. A replacement does not restore a normal joint, and it may not improve preoperative range of motion. The surgeon checks the incision, alignment, joint stability, implant position, and progress with therapy. Follow-up timing and imaging depend on the procedure and recovery.

\section*{Expected Results}
Results vary with the joint, underlying condition, implant, and rehabilitation. A successful result may mean less pain and better ability to perform daily tasks, even if motion is limited. Some people later need another operation if an implant wears out, loosens, breaks, or becomes unstable.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend surgical and hand-therapy appointments. Follow instructions about splint use, wound care, exercises, lifting, and returning to work, driving, or sports. Contact the surgeon before changing the exercise plan or using the hand for strenuous activity.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the surgical team for increasing pain or swelling, wound redness or drainage, fever, or a sudden change in hand function. Seek urgent medical care if the fingers become pale, blue, cold, newly numb, or difficult to move, or if severe pain does not improve as instructed.
