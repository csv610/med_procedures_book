\chapter{Cardioversion}

\section*{Overview}
Electrical cardioversion uses a brief, controlled shock through pads on the chest to restore a more effective heart rhythm. The shock is synchronized with the heart's electrical cycle to reduce the chance of triggering a dangerous rhythm. It is commonly used for atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter and selected other fast rhythms.

Electrical cardioversion is different from defibrillation. Defibrillation uses an unsynchronized shock for certain life-threatening rhythms, such as ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia. Cardioversion may also refer to using medicine to restore rhythm; this chapter focuses on the electrical procedure.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Electrical cardioversion; synchronized cardioversion; direct-current cardioversion (DCCV).

\section*{Principle}
The synchronized shock briefly resets electrical activity across much of the heart muscle. This may allow the heart's usual pacemaker to resume control. Cardioversion can restore a regular rhythm, but the abnormal rhythm may return and more treatment may be needed.

\section*{History}
Bernard Lown and colleagues developed synchronized direct-current cardioversion in the early 1960s, making planned electrical treatment of arrhythmias safer than earlier unsynchronized shocks.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Cardioversion may be used for atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or selected other fast rhythms when restoring a regular rhythm is expected to improve symptoms or circulation. It may be planned for a stable patient or performed urgently when a fast rhythm is causing severe low blood pressure, shock, acute heart failure, chest pain from poor blood flow, or altered mental status.

\section*{Role in Care}
This is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. For an unstable patient, synchronized cardioversion can be an emergency treatment and should not be delayed for elective preparation. For a stable patient, the team considers the rhythm, duration, symptoms, stroke risk, treatment alternatives, and the likelihood that the rhythm will stay normal.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
For planned cardioversion, the patient receives short-acting sedation or anesthesia so the shock is not felt. The team monitors heart rhythm, oxygen, and blood pressure and places adhesive pads on the chest, sometimes with a second pad on the back. The defibrillator is set to synchronize the shock with the heartbeat. One or more shocks may be given while the team checks the rhythm.

If cardioversion is urgently needed because the patient is unstable, the team gives sedation if it can be done without delaying treatment. After the shock, the team checks the rhythm with a monitor or ECG and continues monitoring during recovery.

\section*{Preparation}
For planned cardioversion, follow the team's fasting and medication instructions. Tell the team about all medicines, allergies, pregnancy or possible pregnancy, sleep apnea, prior reactions to anesthesia, and any implanted heart device. Do not stop heart or blood-thinning medicines unless the treating team gives a plan.

Before planned cardioversion for atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter, the team addresses the risk of a clot traveling to the brain. U.S. guidance uses the same risk-based approach to anticoagulation for atrial flutter as for atrial fibrillation. For atrial fibrillation lasting 48 hours or longer, or when its start time is unknown, U.S. guidance generally calls for at least 3 weeks of uninterrupted anticoagulation at the prescribed dose or imaging to check for a clot in the heart before the procedure. One imaging option is transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), which uses an ultrasound probe in the esophagus, close to the heart.

A person's symptoms may begin after the abnormal rhythm has already started, so the time symptoms began may not reliably show when the rhythm began. The 2024 European guideline uses a 24-hour threshold for this decision, so recommendations can differ by location and individual risk. For episodes that seem shorter, stroke risk is not always low, and imaging or anticoagulation may still be needed.

For most patients, anticoagulation is started before cardioversion and continued for at least 4 weeks afterward; longer-term use depends on stroke risk. European guidance allows the 4-week course to be omitted only for selected patients without stroke-risk factors when atrial fibrillation is definitely shorter than 24 hours and normal rhythm is restored. If imaging finds a clot, the team generally postpones elective cardioversion, gives anticoagulation for at least 3 to 6 weeks, and repeats imaging before reconsidering the procedure. These steps do not delay emergency cardioversion for a life-threatening unstable rhythm; anticoagulation is considered as soon as feasible.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
For planned cardioversion, a known clot in the left atrium or its appendage, inadequate anticoagulation when required, or a reversible problem such as a major imbalance in blood salts may lead the team to delay and treat the problem first. An abnormal rhythm caused by digoxin, a heart medicine, may need a different approach. In an emergency, the immediate danger of the rhythm may outweigh these concerns. The decision is made by the treating team.

\section*{Risks}
Risks include a clot-related stroke or other embolism, a new or worsened arrhythmia, a slow heartbeat or pause that may require treatment, skin redness or a minor burn where the pads were placed, and complications from sedation such as slowed breathing or low blood pressure. The heart may return to the abnormal rhythm soon afterward or later, so cardioversion does not guarantee lasting rhythm control.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After planned cardioversion, the patient is monitored until breathing, blood pressure, and alertness have recovered. An ECG confirms the immediate rhythm. Many patients go home the same day with an adult escort, depending on the sedation and other care needs. Mild skin soreness or redness under the pads may occur. Follow instructions about driving, activity, and medicines after sedation.

Continue anticoagulants and other prescribed medicines exactly as directed. Do not stop anticoagulation because the rhythm appears normal; longer-term use depends on stroke risk and the clinician's plan.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The team checks whether the shock restored the intended rhythm, but an immediate result does not guarantee that the rhythm will remain normal. Follow-up may include an ECG, heart monitor, medication review, or discussion of another treatment such as catheter ablation. Recurrent atrial fibrillation is common enough that symptoms and stroke risk still need ongoing care.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek emergency care for chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting, new weakness or numbness, facial drooping, or trouble speaking. Contact the cardiology team for a fast or irregular heartbeat that returns, worsening symptoms, a significant skin injury under a pad, or questions about anticoagulants. Do not skip or stop a prescribed anticoagulant without medical advice.
