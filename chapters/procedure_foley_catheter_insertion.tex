\chapter{Foley Catheter Insertion}

\section*{Overview}
A Foley catheter is a soft tube passed through the urethra into the bladder to drain urine. A small balloon filled with sterile water holds it in place. Urine flows through the tube into a collection bag. Because an indwelling catheter can cause infection and other problems, it is used only while there is a clear medical reason and removed as soon as it is no longer needed.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Indwelling urinary catheter, urethral catheter, bladder catheter

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A clinician may recommend a Foley catheter for acute urinary retention or blockage, close urine-output measurement in a critically ill person, selected operations, or certain situations requiring prolonged immobilization. It may also be used to help protect an open wound near the genitals from urine. It is generally not used simply to manage incontinence, make routine care easier, or collect a urine sample when a person can urinate into a container. Alternatives may include a urinal, bedside commode, external collection device, or intermittent catheterization, depending on the situation.

\section*{Role in Care}
Catheterization is a treatment to drain the bladder and, in selected settings, a way to measure urine output accurately. It is not usually needed for a routine urine test or for incontinence alone. If the urethra cannot be safely used, a clinician may consider another approach, such as a suprapubic catheter placed through the lower abdomen.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
A trained clinician explains the procedure, positions the patient, cleans the area around the urethral opening, and uses sterile equipment and lubricant. The smallest catheter that will drain well is chosen to reduce irritation and injury. The catheter is gently passed through the urethra into the bladder. The balloon is inflated with sterile water only after urine flows and the clinician confirms that the catheter is in the bladder; it is then connected to a drainage system. The tube is secured to reduce pulling, and the collection bag is kept below bladder level. The procedure can cause pressure or brief discomfort. Stop and seek experienced help if there is marked resistance, significant pain, or bleeding; forcing the catheter can injure the urethra.

\section*{Preparation}
The care team confirms the reason for catheterization and checks for relevant urinary or urethral problems, allergies to catheter materials or lubricant, and prior difficulty with catheter placement. Tell the clinician about previous urethral or prostate surgery, known narrowing, pelvic injury, or a history of difficult catheterization. The patient is positioned to allow access and privacy. Sterile equipment is used to reduce infection risk.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Possible urethral injury, such as after pelvic trauma with blood at the urethral opening, is an important reason not to make a blind urethral attempt until the injury has been assessed. Known urethral narrowing, recent urinary tract surgery, or a history of difficult insertion may require an experienced clinician, a different catheter, or urology guidance. Do not force a catheter against resistance. Alternatives should be considered when an indwelling catheter is not necessary.

\section*{Risks}
Risks include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Catheter-associated urinary tract infection. Infection risk increases the longer the catheter remains in place.
    \item Irritation, pain, or bladder spasms.
    \item Injury to the urethra, bleeding, or creation of a false passage, especially if insertion is difficult or the tube is forced.
    \item Blockage, leakage around the catheter, or accidental pulling out.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The catheter should remain connected to a closed drainage system. Keep the bag below the level of the bladder and off the floor, make sure the tube is not kinked, and empty the bag as instructed without letting the outlet touch the container. Wash hands before and after handling the catheter or bag. Clean the area where the catheter enters the body during regular washing; special antiseptics are not usually needed for routine daily care. Avoid disconnecting the tubing unless instructed. The care team should review daily whether the catheter is still needed and remove it promptly when it is not.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
When correctly placed and open, urine drains into the bag. The clinician checks that the catheter is draining, that the tube and bag are positioned correctly, and that the patient is comfortable. Urine color and amount vary with hydration and health; clear yellow urine is not required for correct placement. Ongoing care includes monitoring urine output when clinically needed, checking for obstruction or infection, and planning removal or a change to another bladder-management method.

\section*{Expected Results}
Expected results depend on why the catheter was placed. The bladder should drain without undue pain, and the tubing should not be kinked or pulled tight. Little or no urine in the bag, new leakage, or bladder fullness can indicate a kink or blockage and should be assessed; low urine output can also reflect a medical problem unrelated to the catheter.

\section*{Follow-up}
The clinician sets a removal or replacement plan based on the reason for catheterization and the catheter type. Do not remove or adjust the balloon yourself unless specifically trained and instructed to do so. If a catheter is needed at home, ask for instructions on bag emptying, bathing, supplies, and whom to contact for problems.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the care team promptly for fever or chills, new lower abdominal or back pain, blood clots, cloudy or foul-smelling urine with feeling unwell, leakage, or little to no urine draining despite checking that the tubing is not kinked. Seek urgent care for severe pain, significant bleeding, a catheter that has come out when it is still needed, or inability to urinate with a painful or swollen lower abdomen.
