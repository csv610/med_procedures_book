\chapter{Esophageal Manometry}

\section*{Overview}
Esophageal manometry measures pressure and muscle coordination in the esophagus, the tube that carries food from the mouth to the stomach. It also measures how well the upper and lower esophageal sphincters open and close during swallowing. High-resolution manometry, the common modern method, uses many closely spaced sensors to create a pressure map. The test does not by itself diagnose acid reflux.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Esophageal manometry; esophageal motility study; high-resolution manometry (HRM); high-resolution impedance manometry (HRIM) when the test also tracks movement of swallowed liquid.

\section*{Principle}
Pressure sensors along a thin catheter record the muscle contractions that move a swallow down the esophagus and the relaxation of the sphincters. A computer displays these pressures as patterns. A specialist interprets the patterns together with symptoms and other test results.

\section*{History}
Esophageal pressure testing developed from basic pressure measurements into high-resolution studies that show detailed pressure patterns during swallowing. Some systems also measure liquid movement through the esophagus (impedance), and test protocols may include additional swallows or a small test meal.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Manometry may be used to investigate difficulty swallowing, regurgitation, or chest pain after heart-related causes have been assessed. It can help diagnose movement disorders such as achalasia or esophageal spasm. It is also usually performed before selected anti-reflux operations or procedures to check for major movement problems that could affect treatment decisions. In some cases, it helps locate the lower esophageal sphincter before a separate reflux-monitoring catheter is placed.

\section*{Role in Care}
Manometry is a diagnostic test for esophageal muscle function. It is often used after an examination such as endoscopy has not explained swallowing symptoms, or before anti-reflux surgery. It is not the same as pH or pH-impedance monitoring, which measures reflux, and it is not sufficient by itself to diagnose gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD).

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The test is usually done while the person is awake and does not require sedation, because swallowing is part of the examination. A clinician may use numbing gel or spray in the nose. A thin catheter is passed through a nostril, down the throat and esophagus, and into the stomach. The person is asked to swallow small amounts of water, and sometimes other test swallows or soft food, while the catheter records pressure. The catheter is then removed. The test often takes about 15 to 30 minutes, though timing varies by protocol.

\section*{Preparation}
Follow the testing center's instructions about fasting; many centers ask patients not to eat or drink for several hours before the test. Tell the clinician about all medicines, including opioids and medicines that affect swallowing or esophageal movement. Some medicines may need to be paused, but only if the ordering clinician gives instructions. Also mention nasal blockage, nosebleeds, bleeding disorders, allergies to numbing medicines, prior surgery, or difficulty swallowing liquids.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
The clinician considers whether the catheter can be passed safely through the nose and esophagus. Significant nasal obstruction, recent injury or surgery, a known narrowing, or a condition that makes swallowing small sips unsafe may require a different approach or another test. Tell the team if you cannot tolerate nasal procedures or have a history of fainting during them; staff can discuss ways to make the test more manageable.

\section*{Risks}
The catheter may cause temporary nose or throat discomfort, watery eyes, gagging, or an urge to swallow. A small nosebleed can occur. Serious complications are uncommon. If numbing medicine is used, follow staff instructions about when it is safe to eat or drink afterward.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The catheter is removed immediately after the measurements. Most people can return to normal activities soon after the test because sedation is generally not used. Mild throat or nasal irritation usually settles. If the nose or throat was numbed, wait to eat or drink until sensation has returned and the clinic says it is safe.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The pressure recordings show how the esophagus and sphincters move during the test swallows. Results may be normal or may fit a motility disorder. A result needs to be interpreted with symptoms, endoscopy or imaging findings, and the reason for testing. Some patterns are borderline or do not fully explain symptoms, and another test may be needed.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no single normal pressure value for every person or device. The report evaluates the timing and strength of contractions, relaxation at the lower sphincter, and how swallows move through the esophagus. Normal manometry does not exclude GERD or every cause of swallowing difficulty.

\section*{Follow-up}
The ordering clinician reviews the report and explains whether treatment or more testing is needed. Depending on symptoms, follow-up may include endoscopy, a barium esophagram, reflux monitoring, or evaluation for a motility disorder. Manometry results are considered before anti-reflux procedures but do not alone establish that GERD is present.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the testing center if you have persistent nosebleeding or worsening pain after the test. Seek urgent care for trouble breathing, severe chest pain, inability to swallow, heavy bleeding, or rapidly worsening symptoms. Mild brief throat or nasal irritation is common.
