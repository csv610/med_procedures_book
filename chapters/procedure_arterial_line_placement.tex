\chapter{Arterial Line Placement and Arterial Blood Gas Sampling}

\section*{Overview}
An arterial line is a small catheter placed in an artery to measure blood pressure continuously and to allow repeated blood sampling. A single arterial blood gas (ABG) can usually be collected by a separate needle puncture; an arterial line is not needed just to obtain one sample. An ABG measures oxygen, carbon dioxide, and acid-base balance in arterial blood.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item arterial catheter kit, guidewire, and sterile dressing
  \item ultrasound with sterile probe cover when used
  \item pressure transducer and tubing, flush system, and blood-gas syringe
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
A-line; arterial catheter; arterial cannulation. ABG sampling may be performed through an existing arterial line or by a separate arterial puncture.

\section*{Principle}
An arterial catheter connects to a sterile pressure-monitoring system. A transducer converts the arterial pulse into a waveform and continuous blood pressure reading. The line can also be used to collect repeated arterial samples. ABG analysis measures pH and the partial pressures of oxygen (PaO2) and carbon dioxide (PaCO2); bicarbonate and oxygen saturation are often calculated by the analyzer. When needed, co-oximetry directly measures oxygen saturation and can detect abnormal hemoglobins such as carboxyhemoglobin or methemoglobin. These results help assess oxygenation, ventilation, and acid-base balance.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
An arterial line may be used when continuous blood pressure readings or frequent blood samples are expected to guide care, such as during major surgery, critical illness, or treatment with medicines to support blood pressure. A separate ABG may be requested to assess oxygenation or ventilation, investigate an acid-base problem, or check response to treatment. If oxygen level is the only question, pulse oximetry may be enough in some situations, but it does not measure carbon dioxide or acid-base balance. A venous blood gas may be useful for some acid-base or carbon dioxide questions, but venous oxygen levels cannot reliably replace an arterial sample when arterial oxygenation must be measured.

\section*{Role in Care}
An arterial line is an invasive monitoring option for selected patients when repeated or continuous arterial measurements are expected to change care. A single ABG is a separate diagnostic sample and usually does not require an indwelling line. The clinician weighs the expected benefit against bleeding, infection, and blood-flow risks.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
For an arterial line, a trained clinician selects a suitable artery, often the radial artery at the wrist, cleans the skin with an antiseptic, and uses sterile technique. CDC recommends chlorhexidine in alcohol at a concentration greater than 0.5% for peripheral arterial catheter insertion; if chlorhexidine cannot be used, another recommended antiseptic is chosen. The antiseptic is allowed to dry before insertion. Site choice also depends on age, circulation, and infection risk. In adults, CDC guidance prefers radial, brachial, or dorsalis pedis (top-of-foot) sites over femoral or axillary sites to reduce infection risk; in children, the brachial artery should not be used. For a peripheral arterial catheter, CDC guidance calls for at least a cap, mask, sterile gloves, and a small sterile drape; femoral or axillary insertion requires full sterile barriers: a cap, mask, sterile gown, sterile gloves, and full-body sterile drape. Local anesthetic may be used. The catheter is placed into the artery, sometimes with ultrasound and a guidewire, then secured and connected to sterile pressure tubing and a transducer. The transducer is leveled with the phlebostatic axis (approximately the level of the right atrium) and zeroed to room air (set to atmospheric pressure) so the pressure reading is accurate; the waveform is checked to confirm function.

For a one-time ABG, a clinician collects blood from an artery, commonly at the wrist, into a heparinized syringe. Any air bubbles are removed within 30 seconds, and the sample is gently rolled vertically for at least 10 seconds to mix the blood with the anticoagulant and prevent clotting. The oxygen being given, breathing rate, ventilator settings if used, and sampling time are recorded because they affect interpretation. The sample is analyzed as soon as practical; under ordinary conditions, guidance recommends analysis within about 15 minutes because delays can change the results. Samples for specialized high-oxygen shunt studies, which estimate how much blood passes through the lungs without picking up oxygen, need analysis within about 5 minutes. Blood drawn from an existing line is collected using a method that avoids dilution by the flush solution.

\section*{Preparation}
The clinician explains the reason for the procedure, alternatives, and risks, and obtains consent when circumstances allow. Before line placement, the team assesses the proposed site and circulation to the hand or foot, reviews bleeding risks and relevant medicines, and uses ultrasound when appropriate and available. Some clinicians use a modified Allen or Barbeau test before radial access, but these tests do not reliably predict hand ischemia and are not universal requirements. For a one-time ABG, the site is chosen based on pulse, circulation, anatomy, and ability to compress it afterward.

\section*{Contraindications}
Avoid placing a catheter through infected skin, a clot, or distorted anatomy. Poor circulation to the limb, severe peripheral vascular disease, active Raynaud symptoms, or a bleeding disorder may make a particular site unsuitable or require a different plan. An arterial line is for pressure monitoring and blood sampling; medicines must not be injected into it.

\section*{Precautions}
The line and transducer system require sterile handling, and the catheter should be removed as soon as it is no longer needed.

\section*{Risks}
Both line placement and arterial puncture can cause pain, bruising, bleeding, or a hematoma. Less commonly, the artery can spasm, clot, or be injured, reducing blood flow to the hand or foot; infection, nerve injury, or pseudoaneurysm can also occur. An indwelling line adds risks of catheter infection, disconnection with blood loss, inaccurate readings from a poorly leveled or malfunctioning system, and serious injury if medication is injected into the line.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
While the line is in place, staff keep the closed flush system sterile, secure the catheter, and regularly check the site, circulation beyond the insertion site, connections, waveform, and alarms. The transducer must stay level with the phlebostatic axis (approximately the level of the right atrium) and be zeroed to room air (set to atmospheric pressure) for reliable pressure readings. It should be re-leveled after a change in the patient's position. The line is removed when no longer needed, and firm pressure is held for at least 5 minutes and longer as needed for the insertion site and bleeding risk; the site and circulation beyond it are then rechecked. After a one-time puncture, firm pressure is held over the site for about 5 minutes or longer if needed, and the area is checked for bleeding or a growing bruise.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
ABG results are interpreted together with the patient's symptoms, oxygen or ventilator settings, and other tests. PaO2 helps assess oxygenation, PaCO2 reflects ventilation, and pH with bicarbonate helps assess acid-base balance. An arterial line provides continuous pressure measurements, but the waveform and transducer setup must be checked; readings are considered with the whole clinical picture and may differ from a cuff measurement.

\section*{Expected Results}
For a healthy adult at rest, breathing room air at sea level, common reference ranges are pH 7.35--7.45, PaCO2 35--45 mm Hg, bicarbonate 22--26 mEq/L, and PaO2 about 80--100 mm Hg. PaO2 often decreases with age. Ranges also vary by laboratory, altitude, body temperature, and whether the patient is receiving supplemental oxygen. Blood pressure targets are individualized; there is no single normal arterial-line target for every patient.

\section*{Follow-up}
Repeat ABGs are obtained only when clinically useful. The team reviews blood pressure trends and waveform quality, checks the insertion site and distal circulation, and removes the line when continuous monitoring or repeated sampling is no longer needed. Further assessment, such as ultrasound, is based on symptoms or examination findings.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Alert the care team immediately if the hand or foot beyond the line becomes unusually painful, pale, blue, cold, numb, weak, or swollen; if bleeding continues or the line disconnects; or if the site becomes red, warm, or drains fluid. After a one-time puncture, seek prompt medical help for persistent bleeding, a rapidly enlarging bruise, severe pain, or new numbness or color change in the hand or foot.
