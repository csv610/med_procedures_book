\chapter{Intravitreal Injection}

\section*{Overview}
An intravitreal injection places medicine into the vitreous, the clear gel inside the eye. It is commonly used to treat retinal disease. Anti-VEGF medicines are used for conditions such as neovascular age-related macular degeneration, diabetic macular edema, and macular edema from retinal vein occlusion. Other injected medicines, including corticosteroids or antibiotics, have different indications. The drug, expected benefit, and follow-up plan depend on the eye condition.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Intravitreal injection; eye injection; intravitreal anti-VEGF injection (when an anti-VEGF medicine is used)

\section*{Principle}
The medicine is placed directly into the vitreous so it can act within the eye. The treatment and possible effects outside the eye depend on the specific drug. Many retinal conditions require repeated injections and monitoring over time; the injection does not cure the underlying disease.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Intravitreal medicines may be used for retinal conditions including neovascular age-related macular degeneration, diabetic macular edema, retinal vein occlusion-related macular edema, and some forms of uveitis. Antibiotics may be injected urgently to treat a serious infection inside the eye. The ophthalmologist selects an approved or, in some circumstances, off-label medicine based on the diagnosis, prior response, and risks. A series of injections may be needed to preserve vision.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
This is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. Injections are a main treatment for some sight-threatening retinal diseases, but they are not appropriate for every eye condition. Response is assessed using symptoms, vision testing, eye examination, and often optical coherence tomography (OCT) or other imaging.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician confirms the eye and medicine, explains the procedure, and uses an antiseptic—usually povidone-iodine—on the eye surface to reduce infection risk. Numbing drops or another local anesthetic are used, and a device may hold the eyelids apart. A fine needle is passed through the white of the eye into the vitreous at a site chosen for the patient's age, lens status, and anatomy. The medicine is injected, the needle is removed, and the eye is checked as needed. The procedure is usually done in a clinic and takes only a short time. Routine antibiotic eye drops before or after the injection are not recommended in many current guidelines because they have not been shown to prevent infection and can contribute to antibiotic resistance; follow the ophthalmology team's instructions.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the ophthalmologist about allergies, eye symptoms or infection, medicines, blood-thinning treatment, pregnancy, and prior eye surgery or injections. Do not stop prescribed medicines, including blood thinners, unless the treating clinician specifically advises this. Fasting is not usually needed for an injection done with local anesthesia alone. Ask which medicine will be used, how often follow-up is needed, and how to reach urgent eye care after the procedure. If both eyes are treated at one visit, each eye should be prepared separately using sterile equipment and medication as directed by the clinical protocol.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
For a planned injection, infection of the eye or nearby tissues may require treatment or delay; suspected infection inside the eye is an emergency and may itself require an intravitreal antibiotic injection. The clinician considers allergy to the medicine or antiseptic, recent eye surgery, and the risks of the specific drug. Corticosteroid injections may raise eye pressure and increase cataract risk. Anti-VEGF medicines can be absorbed into the body; people with recent serious vascular events or other relevant health concerns should discuss the potential benefits and risks with the prescriber. The decision is specific to the condition and medicine.

\section*{Risks}
Common short-lived effects include mild irritation, a gritty feeling, temporary blur or floaters, and a small red blood spot on the white of the eye. Pressure inside the eye often rises briefly after injection and usually settles, but persistent pressure elevation is possible, particularly with repeated steroid injections. Rare but serious complications include infection inside the eye (endophthalmitis), severe sterile inflammation, bleeding inside the eye, retinal tear or detachment, injury to the lens with cataract, and lasting vision loss. Anti-VEGF medicines have a possible systemic risk of arterial events such as stroke or heart attack; reported risk and its relationship to treatment vary by medicine and patient.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the clinic's instructions and use prescribed drops only as directed. Mild irritation, temporary blur, floaters, or a blood spot may occur. Most people can return to usual activities, but should avoid rubbing or pressing on the eye. The ophthalmologist may check vision or eye pressure immediately after injection when indicated. Do not drive until vision is clear and safe for driving.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The treatment goal is to reduce retinal disease activity and preserve vision; some people also gain vision, while others may continue to lose vision despite appropriate treatment. Follow-up commonly includes vision testing, examination, and OCT. The interval between injections is individualized and may be adjusted based on the medicine and response. A lack of immediate improvement does not by itself mean the treatment has failed.

\section*{Expected Results}
The expected result may be less retinal fluid, bleeding, or inflammation and stable or improved vision. The degree and timing of benefit vary by diagnosis, disease severity, and medicine. Continued treatment may be needed to maintain benefit.

\section*{Follow-up}
Keep scheduled injection and monitoring appointments; delaying treatment may allow some retinal diseases to worsen. Follow the written plan for the next visit and contact the eye clinic if an appointment must be changed. Report a new decrease in vision, flashes, a sudden increase in floaters, or a curtain-like shadow promptly.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the ophthalmology service urgently—do not wait for the next appointment—for increasing eye pain, worsening redness, light sensitivity, new or worsening blurred vision, or a sudden increase in floaters after an injection. These symptoms can signal infection or another serious complication. A curtain or shadow in the vision, flashes of light, or sudden vision loss also needs urgent same-day eye assessment. If the eye clinic cannot be reached, seek emergency eye care.
