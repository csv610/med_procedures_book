\chapter{Chest Tube Insertion}

\section*{Overview}
A chest tube, also called a chest drain, is a flexible tube placed through the chest wall into the space around a lung (the pleural space). It drains air, blood, infected fluid, or other fluid so the lung can expand or the problem can be treated. The tube connects to a drainage system that helps prevent air from flowing back into the chest. The tube size and technique depend on what needs draining and how urgently it must be treated.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Chest drain; intercostal drain; tube thoracostomy; pleural drain.

\section*{Principle}
The tube provides a path for air or fluid to leave the pleural space. It is connected to a sealed drainage system, often with a water seal, which lets contents flow out while helping prevent air from returning. Suction may be used for selected reasons but is not needed for every chest tube.

\section*{History}
Closed chest drainage systems developed in the early twentieth century and became an important treatment for injuries and diseases that place air or fluid around the lung.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A chest tube may be used for a pneumothorax (air around the lung), blood in the pleural space, infected fluid (empyema), or selected pleural effusions. It is also used after some chest operations. A tension pneumothorax can be life-threatening and may need immediate emergency decompression before a chest tube is placed. The best treatment depends on the cause, size, symptoms, and the patient's condition; not every pneumothorax or effusion requires a tube. Small-bore drains are suitable for many conditions; larger drains may be needed for some unstable trauma patients or for a pneumothorax in a person receiving mechanical ventilation.

\section*{Role in Care}
This is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. Imaging and examination help identify the problem and guide treatment. If a pleural effusion is being drained, ultrasound is used to identify a safe site. In a life-threatening emergency, treatment may need to begin before imaging is available.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The patient is positioned so the clinician can reach the chest. The skin is cleaned, sterile drapes are placed, and local anesthetic is injected; pain medicine or sedation may also be used. Depending on the type of drain, the clinician uses a guidewire technique or makes a small incision and separates tissue to reach the pleural space. The tube is secured and connected to a drainage system. Imaging is used as appropriate to check the tube and the lung. The tube is placed by a trained clinician using an insertion site selected for the patient's anatomy and the reason for drainage; a sharp trocar technique is avoided because it can injure organs.

\section*{Preparation}
The team explains the reason for the drain, likely benefits, and risks, and obtains consent when the situation allows. Tell the team about blood-thinning medicines, bleeding problems, allergies, prior chest surgery, and lung or heart conditions. The team may review imaging and blood tests. Ultrasound is used to locate fluid and guide placement when possible. Emergency treatment may proceed without delay for routine preparation.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
There is no absolute reason to withhold a needed chest tube in a life-threatening emergency. Bleeding risk, infection at the proposed site, altered anatomy, or prior surgery may affect the site, technique, and precautions. For pleural fluid, placement is guided by ultrasound to reduce the chance of injury. The team balances these risks against the danger of leaving the air or fluid untreated.

\section*{Risks}
Possible complications include pain, bleeding, infection, injury to the lung or nearby blood vessels or organs, incorrect tube position, blockage, or the tube becoming displaced. Air can collect under the skin. Rapid drainage or sudden lung expansion can rarely cause fluid to enter the lung tissue (re-expansion pulmonary edema), especially when a large or long-standing effusion is drained. The risks depend on the reason for drainage, the patient's health, and the tube used.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The team monitors breathing, pain, the tube site, and the amount and appearance of drainage. The drainage system is kept upright and below the chest. A chest X-ray is commonly taken within a few hours after placement to check the tube position and lung; another imaging method may be used in some situations. If fluid drainage causes chest pain or repeated coughing, tell the team promptly because they may need to slow or pause drainage. Suction is used only when prescribed; it is not routine for every patient. Do not pull, clamp, or disconnect the tube yourself. Staff will explain how to move safely and what to do if the tube or connections shift.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The team follows symptoms, imaging, air leakage, and the amount and type of fluid draining. The tube is removed when the underlying problem has improved enough and the team judges that removal is safe; there is no fixed 24- to 48-hour schedule. The tube must be removed by a trained clinician. Whether imaging is needed after removal depends on the condition and the patient's stability.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
While the tube is in place, alert the care team immediately for worsening shortness of breath, new chest pain, increasing bleeding, fever, redness or drainage at the site, or if the tube comes out, disconnects, or stops draining while symptoms worsen. If you are at home with a chest drain, follow the supplied emergency plan and seek urgent help for sudden breathing difficulty or chest pain.
