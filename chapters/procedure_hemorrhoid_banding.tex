\chapter{Hemorrhoid Banding}

\section*{Overview}
Hemorrhoid banding, also called rubber band ligation, is an office procedure for selected internal hemorrhoids. A small elastic band is placed around the base of an internal hemorrhoid to stop its blood supply. The tissue shrinks and usually falls off within about a week. Banding does not treat external hemorrhoids.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item anoscope and band-ligator device
  \item suction or forceps system specific to the ligator
  \item exam light, gloves, and lubricant
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Rubber band ligation, hemorrhoid ligation, banding of internal hemorrhoids

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Banding may be offered for internal hemorrhoids that bleed or prolapse (bulge out) despite changes such as adding fiber and fluids and avoiding straining. It is commonly used for grade I or II and selected grade III internal hemorrhoids. Rectal bleeding should be assessed by a clinician rather than assumed to be from hemorrhoids, since other conditions can cause it.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Banding is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. It is one of several office-based treatments and is generally considered when symptoms continue despite first-line measures. Other options include injection or infrared treatment, and surgery may be considered for larger or more advanced disease or when office treatment is unsuitable.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician examines the anal canal, often using a short viewing instrument called an anoscope. A banding device places one or more small bands around the internal hemorrhoid, above the area that is especially sensitive to pain. The procedure is usually performed in an outpatient setting and often does not require anesthesia, although practice varies. You may feel pressure or a need to have a bowel movement. Tell the clinician right away about sharp or severe pain during band placement.

\section*{Preparation}
Preparation depends on the clinic. Tell the clinician about blood thinners, antiplatelet medicines, bleeding problems, allergies, immune-system conditions, and prior anorectal procedures. Do not stop prescribed medicines, including aspirin or anticoagulants, without a plan from the prescribing and procedural clinicians. Ask whether you need to empty your bowels or follow any other instructions; a full bowel preparation is not always needed.

\section*{Contraindications}
No specific absolute contraindication is identified here; the treating team assesses suitability for the individual.

\section*{Precautions}
Banding is intended for internal hemorrhoids, not external hemorrhoids. Active anorectal infection, significant bleeding problems, immune suppression, or medicines that affect clotting may change whether and when banding is appropriate. The decision about blood-thinning medicines is individualized because stopping them also carries risks. A clinician should first assess the source of bleeding and the severity of the hemorrhoids.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary pressure, a dull ache, or the feeling of needing to pass stool can occur after banding. A small amount of bleeding may happen when the banded tissue separates, often about one to two weeks later. Other risks include more significant bleeding, pain if a band is placed too low, a blood clot in an external hemorrhoid, difficulty urinating, and, rarely, serious pelvic infection or sepsis. Delayed bleeding risk may be higher in people taking medicines that affect clotting.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people return home shortly after the procedure. Follow the clinic's instructions for pain relief and activity. Drink enough fluid and eat fiber-rich foods, or use a fiber supplement or stool softener if recommended, to keep bowel movements soft and reduce straining. Avoid pushing or sitting on the toilet for long periods. A band may pass with a bowel movement without being noticed. Do not insert anything into the anus unless the clinician says it is safe.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Bleeding and prolapse often improve, but symptoms can return and some people need another treatment session. The clinician may arrange a follow-up visit based on symptoms and the number of hemorrhoids treated. Continue fiber, fluids, and healthy bowel habits to reduce recurrence.

\section*{Expected Results}
The banded tissue loses its blood supply, shrinks, and separates, usually within about a week. A small amount of bleeding can occur as it separates, sometimes as late as one to two weeks after banding, but heavy bleeding or severe pain is not expected. Improvement may take days to weeks.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow the clinician's advice about repeat treatment and evaluation of any ongoing rectal bleeding. If bleeding continues after treatment or returns, further assessment may be needed to confirm its source.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek emergency medical care for severe or increasing anal or pelvic pain, fever or chills, difficulty urinating or inability to urinate, or significant bleeding, especially if you feel faint or weak. These symptoms can signal a serious complication, including infection. Contact the clinician for persistent bleeding, worsening symptoms, or concerns about medicines. A small amount of bleeding as the banded tissue separates can occur, sometimes up to two weeks later, but heavy bleeding is not expected.
