\chapter{Lumbar Puncture}

\section*{Overview}
Lumbar puncture (LP), also called a spinal tap, uses a needle in the lower back to collect cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), measure its pressure, or deliver selected medicines into the fluid around the brain and spinal cord. The needle is placed below the usual end of the spinal cord. The reason for the procedure determines which measurements or laboratory tests are needed.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item spinal needle and sterile tray
  \item manometer when opening pressure is measured
  \item labeled cerebrospinal-fluid collection tubes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Spinal tap, lumbar tap, CSF collection

\section*{Why It Is Done}
An LP may help investigate infections such as meningitis, bleeding around the brain, inflammatory or immune conditions, cancer involving the nervous system, or other neurological problems. It can also measure CSF pressure in selected conditions or deliver medicines such as spinal anesthetic, chemotherapy, or certain antimicrobials. The clinician explains the question the LP is meant to answer and whether other tests are needed.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
LP is an important diagnostic procedure for some conditions, but it is not the only test used to make a diagnosis. CSF findings are interpreted with symptoms, examination, imaging, blood tests, and the timing of any treatment. When bacterial meningitis is suspected, antibiotics must not be delayed if it is unsafe to perform the LP promptly.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The patient is positioned on their side or sitting and leaning forward. The lower back is cleaned, and local anesthetic is injected into the skin. A thin needle is guided between lower-back bones into the CSF space, usually at a level below the spinal cord. An atraumatic (pencil-point) needle is recommended because it reduces the chance of a post-procedure headache. If pressure measurement is needed, the patient is usually positioned on their side and the opening pressure is measured before CSF is collected. A small amount of fluid is collected into labeled tubes for the tests ordered. The needle is then removed and a dressing placed. Ultrasound or X-ray guidance may be used when landmarks are difficult or a prior attempt was unsuccessful.

\section*{Preparation}
The clinician reviews the reason for the LP, medical history, neurological examination, allergies, and medicines, especially anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs. Tell the team about bleeding problems, prior spinal surgery or deformity, immune suppression, infection, or pregnancy. Some patients need blood tests or brain imaging, but imaging is not routinely required before every LP. Follow the team's instructions about eating and drinking if sedation is planned. Do not stop prescribed medicines unless the treating team gives a specific plan.

\section*{Contraindications}
An LP may need to be delayed or avoided if there is infection at the puncture site, an unaddressed bleeding risk, or clinical concern for a space-occupying lesion or dangerous pressure difference in the brain. If bacterial meningitis is suspected and the LP cannot be done safely without delay, obtain appropriate blood tests and start antibiotics promptly. Stabilize breathing, circulation, and uncontrolled seizures before the procedure.

\section*{Precautions}
In a person with suspected meningitis, brain imaging is not routinely done before LP; it is considered when there are risk factors or signs such as new focal neurological findings, abnormal pupils, markedly reduced or falling consciousness, or certain other high-risk features.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
The anesthetic injection may sting, and pressure or brief pain can occur as the needle is placed. A headache that is worse sitting or standing can develop after the procedure; it often improves when lying down, but persistent symptoms may need treatment. Back soreness is also possible. Less common complications include bleeding around the spine, infection, or temporary nerve irritation causing tingling or shooting pain. Brain herniation is a rare but serious risk when LP is performed in a person with certain brain lesions or pressure conditions, which is why safety screening matters.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the team's advice about observation, activity, and wound care. Routine prolonged bed rest or forced extra fluids have not been shown to prevent post-LP headache; individual instructions may differ because of the reason for the procedure or other treatment. Drink normally unless fluids are restricted. Results depend on the tests ordered and may be available at different times. If a headache develops, the care team can advise about pain relief and whether further treatment is needed.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
CSF may be tested for cell counts, protein, glucose, infection, blood, abnormal cells, or other markers, depending on the clinical question. A blood glucose measurement may be taken near the time of the LP to help interpret CSF glucose. No single CSF pattern proves every diagnosis, and results may be affected by age, prior antibiotics, a bloody or traumatic sample, or the disease being investigated. The clinician reviews the results and next steps with the patient.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no single expected result for every LP. Normal ranges vary by age and test method, and clear-looking CSF does not by itself rule out disease. The report should be interpreted in the context of the reason the procedure was performed.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow the plan for discussing results and any additional tests or treatment. A repeat LP is performed only when there is a clinical reason, such as measuring pressure again, clarifying an uncertain result, or assessing a particular infection or treatment response.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the care team if a positional headache is severe, lasts more than a day or two, or is not improving with the recommended care. Seek urgent medical attention for fever, increasing redness or drainage at the puncture site, severe or worsening back pain, new leg weakness or numbness, difficulty urinating, confusion, or a severe headache that does not improve when lying down. Use emergency services for sudden severe neurological symptoms or rapid deterioration.
