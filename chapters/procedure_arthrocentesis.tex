\chapter{Arthrocentesis}

\section*{Overview}
Arthrocentesis, also called joint aspiration, uses a needle to remove fluid from a joint. It can help identify the cause of swelling and pain, relieve pressure from excess fluid, or provide access for a separately planned joint injection. The approach and preparation depend on the joint, reason for the procedure, and the patient's health.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item sterile needle and syringe
  \item ultrasound machine when image guidance is used
  \item sterile prep supplies and specimen tubes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Joint aspiration; joint tap; knee tap. A joint injection is a related but distinct procedure.

\section*{Principle}
A clinician places a needle into the joint space and withdraws some of the synovial fluid, the liquid that lubricates the joint. A laboratory may examine its appearance, cell count, crystals, and—when infection is possible—stains and cultures. Fluid can also be removed to ease pressure. Medication may be injected during the same visit when appropriate, but an injection is not part of every aspiration.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
It is often used to investigate a newly swollen, painful joint, especially when a joint infection must be considered. It can also help identify crystal arthritis or bleeding into a joint, relieve pressure from a large effusion (fluid buildup in the joint), and monitor selected joint conditions. A clinician may separately decide to inject medicine for treatment. A suspected joint infection needs urgent assessment; aspiration is an important part of that assessment.

\section*{Role in Care}
This is a targeted diagnostic or treatment procedure, not a screening test. Whether it is needed depends on the examination and the question being investigated. When infection is suspected, prompt fluid sampling can be important to diagnosis and treatment.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
A clinician positions the patient, cleans the skin using sterile technique, and may numb the area with local anesthetic. A needle is placed into the joint and fluid is withdrawn. Ultrasound may help locate fluid or guide the needle, particularly in joints that are difficult to access. The sample is sent for the tests needed to answer the clinical question. If a joint injection is also planned, the medicine and its risks are discussed separately. The knee is a common site, but many joints can be aspirated.

\section*{Preparation}
Usually no fasting or special preparation is needed, but follow the care team's instructions. Tell the clinician about blood thinners, bleeding problems, allergies, prior joint surgery or replacement, and any recent infection. Do not stop a prescribed blood thinner unless the clinician who manages it tells you to; many joint aspirations can be performed while a patient is taking an anticoagulant, but bleeding risk is assessed for each person and joint.

\section*{Contraindications}
The needle must not pass through a skin abscess. If there is cellulitis (skin infection) over the joint, the clinician avoids that route if possible and may use imaging or another entry site. Suspected joint infection still needs prompt assessment and sampling; redness alone should not cause an avoidable delay. No medicine should be injected into the joint while infection is suspected or has not been ruled out. These concerns should not delay urgent evaluation of possible joint infection.

\section*{Precautions}
A severe bleeding disorder, a prosthetic joint, nearby bone infection, or a recent fracture may require specialist input or a modified plan.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary soreness or a small bruise can occur. Less common complications include bleeding into the joint, infection, or injury to nearby tissue, such as a blood vessel, nerve, tendon, or cartilage. If medicine is injected, it has additional medicine-specific risks. A steroid injection may briefly increase pain and can temporarily raise blood sugar, especially in people with diabetes.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The clinician covers the puncture site and may recommend brief ice or reduced activity if the joint is sore. Follow the instructions for the specific joint and any medicine injected. After an injection, avoid overusing the joint for about 24 hours, but prolonged immobilization is generally not needed. If a steroid was injected and you have diabetes, follow the clinician's plan to monitor your blood sugar closely for at least 48 hours and until it returns to your usual range; this may take up to a week. Test results may take longer if a culture is being done.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The clinician interprets the fluid together with symptoms, examination, and other tests. Cloudy fluid or a high white blood cell count can occur with infection or other inflammation. A high count can raise concern for infection, but no single count can confirm or rule it out. Monosodium urate crystals support gout, and calcium pyrophosphate crystals support calcium pyrophosphate crystal arthritis; finding crystals does not rule out a simultaneous infection. A positive bacterial culture can identify an infection, but a negative culture or stain does not always exclude one.

\section*{Expected Results}
In a healthy, natural joint, fluid is usually clear, light yellow, and somewhat sticky, with fewer than 200 white blood cells per microliter. Fluid from non-inflammatory conditions such as osteoarthritis commonly has fewer than 2,000 cells per microliter; higher counts may occur with inflammation or infection. A count above 50,000 can raise concern for infection, but lower counts do not rule it out. These are guides, not stand-alone diagnoses: results vary with the condition and clinical context. Prior antibiotics and a weakened immune system can change how fluid results are interpreted. These guide values mainly apply to natural (native) joints; infection in a replaced joint is assessed using different criteria and often needs specialist input.

\section*{Follow-up}
When infection is suspected, the fluid is usually sent for bacterial culture, a Gram stain (a quick test for bacteria), a white blood cell count and types of white cells, and crystal examination. If there is too little fluid for all tests, guidelines prioritize bacterial testing, followed by the cell count and crystal examination. Blood cultures are also collected when fever, bacteremia (bacteria in the blood), or sepsis is suspected. If the patient is stable, clinicians generally collect the joint sample before starting antibiotics when feasible. If the patient has sepsis, a life-threatening response to infection, antibiotics must not be delayed to obtain a sample. The treating team reviews results promptly and may arrange additional treatment or testing. A suspected joint infection requires urgent care. After a therapeutic injection, benefit and any side effects are assessed over time.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent medical care for a newly hot, red, very painful, or rapidly swelling joint, especially with fever or feeling unwell; joint infection can occur without fever. After the procedure, contact the care team promptly for worsening joint pain, increasing redness or warmth, drainage, fever, or significant swelling or bleeding. These symptoms need assessment because infection can resemble a temporary post-injection flare.
