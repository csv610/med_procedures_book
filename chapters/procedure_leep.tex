\chapter{LEEP}

\section*{Overview}
Loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP), also called loop excision or LLETZ, removes a small area of the cervix using a thin wire loop heated by electrical current. The removed tissue is sent to a laboratory. LEEP can treat certain high-grade precancerous cervical changes and provide a specimen for examination; it is not the same as treatment for invasive cervical cancer.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item colposcope and speculum
  \item loop electrode with electrosurgical generator
  \item smoke evacuator, grounding pad, and specimen container
\end{itemize}

\section*{Why It Is Done}
LEEP is commonly used for selected high-grade cervical cell changes, such as cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) 2 or CIN 3, when treatment is recommended. It may also be used when the clinician needs a larger tissue sample to clarify a diagnosis. The decision depends on biopsy and screening results, age, pregnancy status, prior treatment, and plans for future pregnancy. Not every abnormal screening test requires LEEP.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
LEEP is an excisional treatment and diagnostic procedure performed after cervical screening and usually colposcopy with biopsy have identified a reason for removal. It provides tissue for a pathologist to examine, including the grade of abnormality and whether abnormal cells reach the cut edges (margins). Margin status is one part of the result and does not, by itself, determine whether treatment has succeeded or what follow-up is needed.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
LEEP is commonly performed in a clinic or outpatient setting. The patient lies as for a pelvic examination, and a speculum is placed to view the cervix. A colposcope may be used to magnify the area. Local anesthetic is injected into the cervix; this can briefly sting and may cause a racing heartbeat if the anesthetic contains epinephrine. The clinician passes the energized wire loop over the abnormal area to remove tissue. Pressure, cramping, or a faint feeling can occur; tell the clinician if pain or faintness develops. A solution or paste, and sometimes cautery, is used to control bleeding. The procedure usually takes only a few minutes. Sedation or general anesthesia may be used in some situations.

\section*{Preparation}
The clinic may schedule the procedure when menstrual bleeding is light or absent so the cervix can be seen clearly. Tell the clinician if pregnancy is possible, if you have a bleeding disorder, or if you take anticoagulants, antiplatelet medicines, or other medicines that affect bleeding. Do not stop prescribed medicines unless instructed. Discuss pain-control options and any prior difficulty with pelvic examinations. Follow the clinic's instructions about eating, medicines, and bringing someone to accompany you if sedation is planned.

\section*{Contraindications}
Excisional treatment is generally deferred during pregnancy unless cancer is suspected; evaluation and follow-up during pregnancy require specialist care. LEEP is not appropriate for every abnormal screening result, and the clinician should explain why excision is recommended and what alternatives are reasonable.

\section*{Precautions}
If pregnancy is possible or confirmed, tell the clinician before treatment. An active cervical or vaginal infection, bleeding risk, or concern for invasive cancer may change the timing or approach.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
Cramping, light bleeding, and watery, pink, or dark discharge are common for a few weeks while the cervix heals. Heavier bleeding or infection can occur. Rarely, scar tissue narrows the cervical opening. LEEP usually does not prevent pregnancy, but removal of cervical tissue is associated with a small increased risk of preterm birth in a future pregnancy; risk is higher after deeper or repeated excisions. Tell a future pregnancy care team about a prior LEEP so they can review relevant history.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people return home soon after an office procedure. Use pads rather than tampons while the cervix heals, and avoid putting anything in the vagina, including intercourse or douching, for the period advised by the clinic (often about 3 to 4 weeks). Follow specific instructions about exercise, bathing, and pain relief. Mild cramps and discharge can continue during healing. The clinic will explain when normal activities can resume and whom to contact with concerns.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The pathology report confirms what tissue was removed and whether further evaluation is needed. Most treatments successfully remove the targeted precancer, but abnormal cells or high-risk HPV can persist or return. HPV-based testing, with or without cervical cytology, is generally recommended about 6 months after treatment; the exact surveillance plan depends on the pathology, test results, age, pregnancy status, and local guidelines. Follow-up remains important even when the margins are clear.

\section*{Expected Results}
The goal is to remove the cervical area containing the precancerous change and obtain tissue for diagnosis. LEEP reduces the risk from the treated lesion but does not eliminate the need for future cervical screening and follow-up.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend the scheduled visit or test-of-cure testing and ask how and when results will be communicated. If HPV-based follow-up is positive or the pathology shows a higher-risk finding, further colposcopy, biopsy, or treatment may be recommended. Continue cervical screening at the interval advised by the clinician.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the clinic promptly for bleeding heavier than a normal period, large clots, worsening pelvic pain, fever, chills, or foul-smelling discharge. Seek emergency care for very heavy bleeding, fainting, severe weakness, or severe or rapidly worsening pain.
