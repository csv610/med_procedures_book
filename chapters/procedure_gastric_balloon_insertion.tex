\chapter{Gastric Balloon Insertion}

\section*{Overview}
An intragastric (gastric) balloon is a temporary device placed in the stomach as one option in a medical weight-management program. By taking up space in the stomach, it may help a person feel full sooner. Balloon devices and placement methods differ: many are placed and removed using an endoscope passed through the mouth, while some are swallowed and pass out after they deflate. The time a device may remain in place and its removal plan depend on the specific product. A balloon is not a permanent treatment for obesity and works best as part of ongoing care that includes nutrition and behavior support.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item endoscope and video monitor
  \item gastric balloon with delivery/inflation catheter
  \item suction and fluid-management equipment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Intragastric balloon, gastric balloon, balloon therapy for weight management

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A clinician may discuss a gastric balloon with an adult who has obesity and wants additional help with weight management after discussing other options. Eligibility depends on the device, health history, local approval, and the person's goals. The balloon is used with a structured program; it is not a substitute for long-term obesity care. After it is removed or passes, further support may include nutrition and activity changes, medicines, another weight-management treatment, or bariatric surgery, depending on the person's needs.

\section*{Role in Care}
This is a temporary treatment, not a diagnostic test. It is one of several possible approaches to obesity care. A specialist discusses expected benefits, limitations, alternatives, and device-specific risks before deciding whether it is appropriate.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
For an endoscopically placed balloon, a clinician uses an endoscope to guide the deflated balloon through the mouth into the stomach. The balloon is then filled, commonly with sterile fluid, and the endoscope is withdrawn. Sedation or anesthesia is used. Placement usually takes less than an hour, but preparation and recovery take longer. Removal is planned according to the device instructions and is commonly performed endoscopically. Swallowable balloon systems follow a different placement and exit process; the care team explains the plan for the exact device.

\section*{Preparation}
Preparation varies by device and sedation plan. Follow the clinic's instructions about fasting, medicines, and transportation. Tell the team about prior stomach or esophageal surgery, reflux or swallowing problems, ulcers or bleeding, slow stomach emptying, allergies, pregnancy or breastfeeding, and all medicines and supplements. Do not stop prescribed medicines without instructions. Ask whom to contact if symptoms occur and keep the device identification information available for other clinicians.

\section*{Contraindications}
There is no single eligibility or contraindication list that applies to every balloon. Prior gastrointestinal surgery, ulcers or bleeding, some esophageal or stomach conditions, pregnancy, and other health issues may make a device unsuitable or require additional assessment.

\section*{Precautions}
The specialist checks the device's current instructions for use and reviews the person's medical history. People with a balloon should tell clinicians evaluating new abdominal symptoms that the device is present.

\section*{Risks}
Nausea, vomiting, abdominal discomfort, and cramping are common, especially soon after placement; symptoms may need medication and follow-up. Other risks include reflux, dehydration, stomach irritation or ulcer, bleeding, and the need for early removal if the device is not tolerated. Less common but serious problems reported with liquid-filled balloons include balloon deflation and movement that blocks the intestine, stomach perforation, acute pancreatitis, and spontaneous balloon over-inflation. Risk varies by device. A balloon does not guarantee weight loss, and weight may be regained after it is removed without continuing care.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The team provides a staged diet plan and instructions for fluids, medicines, nutrition, and activity. An acid-reducing medicine is commonly prescribed, and anti-nausea medicine may be scheduled during the early period; some teams also recommend a daily multivitamin. Nausea and discomfort are often strongest soon after placement, but severe or persistent symptoms should not be assumed to be routine. Follow-up with the weight-management team is important throughout treatment. Keep the planned removal appointment; a balloon should not be left in place beyond its device-specific recommended duration.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Weight change varies. The goal is to support weight loss while the device is in place and build habits and a long-term treatment plan that can continue afterward. Follow-up may include weight and nutrition review, management of obesity-related health conditions, and planning for device removal or passage. The balloon may need to be removed early for intolerance or a complication. The team discusses maintenance options before removal rather than treating removal as the end of care.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no single expected amount of weight loss for every person or device. A safe course includes tolerable symptoms, appropriate monitoring, removal or passage according to the device plan, and continuing weight-management support. Improvement in weight-related health conditions may occur but is not guaranteed.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend scheduled visits and follow the device-specific instructions for monitoring and removal. Keep a record of the device and its expected removal or passage date. Discuss a long-term plan for weight management before the balloon is removed or passes.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the treatment team promptly for repeated vomiting, inability to keep liquids down, worsening abdominal pain, or symptoms that are not improving as expected. Seek urgent medical care for severe or sudden abdominal pain (including pain that spreads to the back), persistent vomiting, a swollen abdomen, fever, blood in vomit or stool, fainting, or signs of dehydration such as very little urine. These can signal a serious complication. If you have a balloon, tell emergency clinicians which device you have and when it was placed.
