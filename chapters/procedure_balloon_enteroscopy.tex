\chapter{Balloon Enteroscopy}

\section*{Overview}
Balloon enteroscopy is an examination of the small intestine using a long, flexible camera scope and one or two balloons that help the clinician move it farther through the bowel than a standard endoscope can reach. The scope can be passed through the mouth or, less often, through the anus. The clinician may take tissue samples or treat some problems during the procedure. The approach and preparation depend on the target area and the patient's health.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item balloon-assisted enteroscope, overtube, and video processor
  \item monitor, irrigation, and suction equipment
  \item biopsy forceps or other endoscopic accessories as indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Balloon-assisted enteroscopy; single-balloon enteroscopy; double-balloon enteroscopy; deep enteroscopy

\section*{Principle}
A flexible overtube fits over the scope. Depending on the system, a balloon is attached to the overtube alone or to both the overtube and scope. By inflating and deflating the balloons in sequence, the endoscopy team can gently gather and hold sections of bowel to help move the scope deeper. The camera shows the lining of the small intestine on a monitor. Instruments passed through the scope can take biopsies, control bleeding, remove selected growths, or widen some narrow areas.

\section*{History}
Balloon-assisted methods were developed to make it possible to examine and treat parts of the small intestine that standard endoscopes cannot reach. Single- and double-balloon systems became available in the early 2000s.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Balloon enteroscopy may be used to examine a suspected source of small-bowel bleeding, investigate an abnormality seen on capsule endoscopy or imaging, take a biopsy, or treat a finding such as a bleeding blood vessel or selected polyp. It may also be used to assess or treat some cases of Crohn disease, retrieve a swallowed camera capsule that has not passed or another foreign object, or reach the small bowel in people with surgically altered anatomy. The clinician chooses this procedure when direct viewing or treatment is likely to help.

\section*{Role in Care}
It is a targeted diagnostic and sometimes therapeutic procedure, not a screening test. When upper and lower endoscopy have not found a source of suspected small-bowel bleeding, or when small-bowel evaluation is needed for unexplained iron-deficiency anemia, a swallowed camera capsule is often the first small-bowel test because it can view much of the lining without an invasive scope. For uncomplicated, asymptomatic iron-deficiency anemia with negative upper and lower endoscopy, the American Gastroenterological Association suggests trying iron treatment before routine capsule testing; the care team decides whether small-bowel evaluation is needed. A capsule cannot take tissue samples or treat a finding. After visible bleeding, capsule testing is ideally done within 48 hours. Balloon enteroscopy can then examine a finding more closely, take a sample, or treat it. In some people with Crohn disease or possible bowel narrowing, a dissolvable patency capsule or imaging may first help assess whether a camera capsule can pass safely. Balloon enteroscopy or imaging may be chosen first when capsule testing is unsuitable or urgent treatment is needed. For visible small-bowel bleeding, the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy recommends balloon-assisted enteroscopy ideally within 48 to 72 hours when it is used to investigate or treat the bleeding; timing depends on the clinical situation and local expertise.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is usually done with deep sedation or general anesthesia, with monitoring of breathing and heart function. Depending on the area being examined, the scope is passed through the mouth or anus. The clinician moves the scope and overtube in stages, using the balloons to help advance through the small intestine. If needed, tools passed through the scope can take biopsies or treat selected findings. The examination may not reach the entire small intestine, and another approach or procedure may sometimes be needed.

\section*{Preparation}
Follow the endoscopy team's instructions about fasting, bowel preparation, and medicines. Fasting is usually required for sedation or anesthesia, regardless of which route is used. An examination through the anus generally also requires bowel cleansing similar to a colonoscopy; preparation for a mouth approach varies by center. Tell the team about allergies, especially latex allergy, health conditions, possible pregnancy, and all medicines, especially blood thinners and diabetes medicines. Some balloon systems contain latex. Do not stop or change medicines unless the team gives you a specific plan. Arrange an adult to take you home after sedation or anesthesia, and do not drive until the team says it is safe.

\section*{Contraindications}
No specific absolute contraindication is identified here; the treating team assesses suitability for the individual.

\section*{Precautions}
The team reviews whether the expected benefit outweighs the risks, including sedation or anesthesia risks, bleeding risk, and whether the bowel can be safely examined. A suspected blockage, narrowing, perforation, or severe medical instability may change the plan or require urgent specialist care. Any change to blood thinners or antiplatelet medicines must be directed by the treating team.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary sore throat, bloating, cramping, or nausea may occur. Less common serious complications include a tear in the bowel (perforation), bleeding—especially after treatment—and pancreatitis, an inflammation of the pancreas that can occur after the mouth approach. Perforation risk may be higher when previous surgery has changed the bowel's anatomy. Stomach contents can enter the lungs (aspiration), and sedation or anesthesia can cause other breathing, heart, or medication-related problems. The risks depend on the route, the person's health, and whether treatment is performed.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
You are monitored in a recovery area until the effects of sedation or anesthesia have worn off enough for safe discharge. Follow instructions about food, medicines, and activity. Do not drive, operate machinery, drink alcohol, or make important decisions for the period specified by the team, often until the next day after sedation. Mild bloating or throat soreness should improve. If a biopsy or treatment was done, the team will give any additional instructions.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The clinician reviews the images and any treatment performed. Biopsy results may take several days or longer. The result may identify a cause or guide the next step, but the procedure cannot always examine the whole small intestine or find the source of a problem. Further capsule testing, imaging, another endoscopy, or other treatment may be recommended based on the findings.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no single normal result. The report describes how much of the small intestine was seen and whether there were abnormalities, such as bleeding, inflammation, narrowing, or a growth. Tissue samples and other test results are interpreted with the patient's symptoms and other findings.

\section*{Follow-up}
The endoscopy team explains when and how results will be shared. Follow-up depends on what was found, whether a biopsy or treatment was done, and the patient's symptoms. Continue with recommended appointments and contact the team if results have not arrived by the expected date.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent medical care for severe or worsening abdominal pain, a firm or swollen abdomen, fever, repeated vomiting, black or bloody stools, vomiting blood, fainting, or trouble breathing or chest pain after the procedure. These symptoms may signal a complication and need prompt assessment.
