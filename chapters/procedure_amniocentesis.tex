\chapter{Amniocentesis}

\section*{Overview}
Amniocentesis is an ultrasound-guided procedure in which a clinician uses a thin needle through the pregnant person's abdomen to collect a small sample of amniotic fluid. The sample can be tested for selected chromosome or genetic conditions and, in some situations, fetal infection. Amnioreduction is a separate procedure that removes excess fluid to relieve severe symptoms from polyhydramnios; it is not diagnostic amniocentesis. Diagnostic amniocentesis is not routinely used to assess fetal lung maturity or decide when to deliver.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Amniocentesis; amniotic fluid sampling; prenatal diagnostic amniocentesis; genetic amniocentesis.

\section*{Principle}
A thin needle is passed through the abdominal wall into the amniotic sac while ultrasound is used to locate the fetus, placenta, and a safe fluid pocket. The sample contains fetal cells and other substances that can be analyzed for the specific question being investigated. Tests may look for chromosome differences, a specific inherited condition, or infection. Amniotic-fluid alpha-fetoprotein, sometimes with another marker, can help assess the chance of an open neural tube defect. The selected test determines what the result can and cannot detect; amniocentesis does not test for every condition or replace ultrasound assessment of fetal structures.
\section*{History}
Amniocentesis was first used to remove excess amniotic fluid. Later, ultrasound guidance and laboratory methods made it possible to use fluid samples for prenatal diagnosis.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Amniocentesis may be offered after a prenatal screening result that suggests an increased chance of a condition, an ultrasound finding, a known family or pregnancy risk for an inherited condition, or concern about certain fetal infections. Diagnostic testing options should be discussed with all pregnant patients, regardless of age or screening results; each person may accept or decline. A screening result estimates the chance of a condition but does not diagnose it. Diagnostic amniocentesis should not be performed before 15 weeks; it is commonly done from 15 weeks onward and may be offered later when indicated. For some infections, the most reliable timing is later and depends on when the pregnant person was infected. For example, amniotic-fluid testing for cytomegalovirus is generally performed after 21 weeks of pregnancy and more than 6 weeks after maternal infection. Amniocentesis is not routinely done to check lung maturity before delivery.

\section*{Role in Care}
An optional invasive diagnostic procedure. Earlier in pregnancy, chorionic villus sampling (CVS) may be an alternative; blood-based cell-free DNA screening is noninvasive but is not diagnostic. CVS samples placental tissue rather than amniotic fluid, so it cannot provide amniotic-fluid tests such as alpha-fetoprotein and acetylcholinesterase for an open neural tube defect or some tests for fetal infection. The choice depends on gestational age, the condition being investigated, the available tests, and the patient's preferences after counseling. Counseling should include the possibility of uncertain or unexpected findings and which tests the patient wants performed.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
After reviewing the planned tests, the clinician cleans the skin and uses ultrasound to guide a thin needle through the abdomen and uterus into a pocket of amniotic fluid, avoiding the fetus and placenta when possible. A small sample is withdrawn and sent to the laboratory. The amount collected depends on the tests requested. For a multiple pregnancy, ultrasound planning identifies the sac or sacs to sample; one or more samples may be needed depending on the question. The needle is removed, and fetal cardiac activity is checked before or after the procedure according to local practice. Diagnostic sampling is performed at or after 15 weeks by a clinician trained in prenatal diagnosis. This timing guidance does not apply to separate therapeutic procedures such as amnioreduction.

\section*{Preparation}
The clinician explains the reason for testing, possible results and limitations, alternatives, and risks; the patient can ask questions and choose whether to proceed. No fasting is usually required. Ultrasound is used to assess the fetus, placenta, and fluid pocket. The team reviews blood type and relevant infection test results. If the patient is RhD-negative and has not already developed Rh antibodies, Rh(D) immune globulin is generally given after the procedure according to local guidance. Follow the clinic's instructions about bladder filling, since this varies with gestational age and local practice.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
The procedure is generally deferred until at least 15 weeks because earlier amniocentesis carries greater risk. Whether it is appropriate depends on the pregnancy; the clinician individualizes the plan for multiple pregnancy, placental or uterine anatomy, bleeding concerns, and maternal infection. Blood-borne infections such as HIV or hepatitis B or C require review of relevant test results and an individualized discussion of transmission risk and ways to reduce it before proceeding.

\section*{Risks}
For diagnostic amniocentesis performed by a skilled operator in the second trimester, the additional risk of pregnancy loss is likely below 0.5% for a singleton pregnancy. This is the estimated added risk from the procedure; the overall chance of pregnancy loss also depends on the pregnancy and its underlying health. For twin pregnancies, the additional risk is around 1%; evidence is more limited for higher-order multiples. If sampling is done later in pregnancy, possible complications can include preterm labor or delivery, and the clinician should explain the risks for that situation. Other possible complications include temporary cramping or spotting, leakage of amniotic fluid, infection, and, rarely, injury to the fetus. Occasionally, the sample does not provide a clear result and repeat sampling or another test may be discussed.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Mild cramping or spotting may occur briefly. Follow the treating team's instructions about activity; routine bed rest has not been shown to prevent pregnancy loss, although some teams advise taking it easy for a short time. RhD-negative patients who have not already developed Rh antibodies generally receive Rh(D) immune globulin after the procedure. Test results can take from a few days to several weeks depending on the laboratory tests requested.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The laboratory performs the tests agreed upon before the procedure. Depending on the reason for testing, these may include rapid checks for common chromosome-number changes, chromosome analysis, chromosomal microarray (a detailed test for missing or extra chromosome material), testing for a specific inherited condition, or testing for an infection. Amniotic-fluid alpha-fetoprotein, sometimes with acetylcholinesterase, may be measured when an open neural tube defect is being evaluated. No single test checks for every genetic condition, birth defect, or future health problem.

\section*{Expected Results}
Results may be normal, show the condition being investigated, or be uncertain or incomplete. A test can also find a genetic change whose health effects are not known, or an unexpected finding unrelated to the original reason for testing. A positive test for fetal infection shows that infection is present, but does not by itself predict how severely the fetus will be affected. A normal result is reassuring for the conditions tested but cannot guarantee that the fetus has no genetic condition, birth defect, or health problem. A clinician or genetic counselor can explain what the specific result means and discuss next steps.

\section*{Follow-up}
The clinician reviews the result with the patient and arranges genetic counseling or other specialist care when appropriate. Further decisions depend on the result, the patient's preferences, and the pregnancy. Continue routine prenatal care unless the care team recommends otherwise.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the maternity or fetal-medicine team promptly for vaginal fluid leakage, bleeding, fever or chills, persistent or severe abdominal pain or cramping, contractions, or a change in fetal movement when movement is usually felt. Seek emergency care for heavy bleeding, severe pain, or feeling very unwell. Follow any more specific instructions from the care team.
