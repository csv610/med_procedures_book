\chapter{Coronary Angioplasty}

\section*{Overview}
Coronary angioplasty, usually called percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), is a procedure to improve blood flow through a narrowed or blocked heart artery. A balloon is expanded across the narrowed area, and a small mesh tube called a stent is often placed to help keep the artery open. Most stents release medicine to reduce the chance of the artery narrowing again. The need for PCI and its timing depend on the symptoms, test results, coronary anatomy, and overall health.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item vascular sheath, guidewire, balloon catheter, and stent when needed
  \item fluoroscopy and contrast-injection system
  \item ECG and hemodynamic monitoring equipment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI); coronary angioplasty; balloon angioplasty; coronary stenting.

\section*{Principle}
A catheter is guided through an artery to the heart. A thin wire is passed across the narrowed area, and a balloon is inflated to widen the opening. The balloon presses plaque against the artery wall and may stretch or modify the vessel; it does not remove all plaque. A stent is usually expanded at the site to support the artery. Sometimes other devices or techniques are used, and not every PCI includes a stent.

\section*{History}
Andreas Grüntzig performed the first successful balloon coronary angioplasty in 1977. Coronary stents were introduced later and became a routine part of many PCI procedures.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
PCI may be performed urgently to restore blood flow during certain heart attacks, especially ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). It may also be used for selected patients with other acute coronary syndromes or for persistent angina despite appropriate medicines when the coronary anatomy is suitable. In stable coronary disease, PCI is not automatically needed for every narrowing. For many people, its main benefit is relief of angina; its effect on the risk of heart attack or death depends on factors such as the coronary anatomy and heart function. The team discusses medicines, PCI, bypass surgery, and the expected benefits and risks.

\section*{Role in Care}
PCI is a treatment, often performed during coronary angiography. In a STEMI, it may be an emergency treatment. For stable symptoms, testing and medical treatment usually help determine whether a procedure is likely to improve symptoms or outcomes.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The patient is connected to heart and blood-pressure monitors. The clinician numbs the skin and places a small tube into an artery, usually at the wrist and sometimes in the groin. Sedation or pain medicine may be given, but the patient is often awake. Using X-ray imaging and contrast dye, the cardiologist guides a catheter to the coronary arteries. A wire and balloon are moved across the narrowed area; the balloon is inflated briefly, and a stent may be deployed. The team checks blood flow and removes the catheters. Pressure or a closure device is used to limit bleeding at the access site. Emergency procedures may follow a faster preparation and recovery plan.

\section*{Preparation}
For planned PCI, follow instructions about eating, drinking, and medicines. Tell the team about allergies or a prior contrast reaction, kidney disease, bleeding problems, pregnancy, and all medicines, especially anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs, and diabetes medicines. Do not stop medicines unless the cardiology team gives a plan. In an emergency, necessary treatment may proceed without routine fasting or all planned tests. The team selects antiplatelet and anticoagulant medicines based on the reason for PCI, whether a stent is placed, bleeding risk, and other conditions.

\section*{Contraindications}
No specific absolute contraindication is identified here; the treating team assesses suitability for the individual.

\section*{Precautions}
There is no single rule that applies to every situation. In a life-threatening heart attack, the benefit of restoring blood flow may outweigh major risks. For planned PCI, active bleeding, severe kidney disease, prior serious contrast reaction, complex coronary anatomy, or other illnesses may lead the team to modify the approach, use precautions, or discuss medicines or bypass surgery instead. The decision is individualized with the patient when time permits.

\section*{Risks}
Risks include bleeding, bruising, or injury to the artery at the access site; allergic-like reaction to contrast; kidney function worsening around the procedure; abnormal heart rhythms; and blood clots or re-narrowing in the treated artery or stent. Rare serious complications include coronary artery injury, heart attack, stroke, emergency bypass surgery, or death. Risk depends on the patient's condition, urgency, anatomy, and whether additional treatment is needed. Kidney changes after PCI may be related to contrast, the procedure, blood pressure changes, or the illness that led to treatment; contrast may not be the only cause.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The care team monitors the heart rhythm, blood pressure, and access site. Recovery and length of stay depend on the reason for PCI, access route, treatment, and health status. Follow instructions for wound care, activity, bathing, and medicines. If a stent was placed, take the prescribed antiplatelet medicines exactly as directed. Stopping them without cardiology advice can cause a dangerous clot in the stent. Cardiac rehabilitation and treatment of cholesterol, blood pressure, diabetes, and smoking may be recommended.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The immediate aim is to improve blood flow through the treated artery. The procedure may reduce symptoms or limit heart damage during a heart attack, but it does not cure coronary artery disease or guarantee that symptoms will never return. The cardiologist reviews angiogram results, medicines, heart function, and the plan for follow-up. Antiplatelet treatment duration is individualized; after an acute coronary syndrome, a year of dual antiplatelet therapy is the default for patients who are not at high bleeding risk, but alternatives may be appropriate. Do not change or stop treatment without the prescribing clinician.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Call emergency services for new or severe chest pressure, trouble breathing, fainting, or stroke symptoms. For access-site bleeding, lie down and apply firm pressure; call emergency services if bleeding is heavy or does not stop. Contact the cardiology team promptly for increasing redness, warmth, drainage, fever, worsening pain, or a rapidly growing lump. Seek urgent medical care immediately if the hand or leg beyond the access site becomes cold, pale, numb, or weak, because this may signal reduced blood flow.
