\chapter{Cardiac Ablation}

\section*{Overview}
Catheter ablation is a procedure used to treat some abnormal heart rhythms (arrhythmias). Thin tubes called catheters are passed through a blood vessel to the heart. The clinician identifies the heart tissue involved in the abnormal rhythm and uses energy to create small areas of scar that interrupt the faulty electrical signals. The aim may be to reduce symptoms or the amount of time spent in an abnormal rhythm; it does not guarantee a permanent cure.

This chapter focuses on catheter ablation. Surgical and hybrid ablation are different procedures.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item electrophysiology catheters and introducer sheaths
  \item 3-D mapping system and fluoroscopy when used
  \item ECG, pressure, and rhythm-monitoring equipment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Catheter ablation; electrophysiology study and ablation; radiofrequency ablation; cryoablation; pulsed-field ablation (PFA).

\section*{Principle}
Electrode catheters record the heart's electrical activity and may pace the heart to help locate the source or pathway of an arrhythmia. The clinician then applies energy to targeted tissue. Radiofrequency uses heat, cryoablation uses cold, and pulsed-field ablation uses short electrical pulses. The method and target depend on the arrhythmia. For atrial fibrillation, a common strategy is to isolate the electrical connections around the pulmonary veins (the veins that carry blood from the lungs back to the heart); for some other rhythms, a small pathway or focus is treated.

\section*{History}
Catheter ablation developed from early direct-current methods into radiofrequency and cryoablation techniques. Pulsed-field ablation is a newer energy option used for selected atrial fibrillation procedures.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Catheter ablation may be considered for symptomatic supraventricular tachycardia, accessory pathways such as Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome, atrial flutter, atrial fibrillation, and selected other fast heart rhythms. It may be offered when medicines are ineffective, cause troublesome effects, or are not preferred. For some arrhythmias, ablation is a standard early treatment option; the decision depends on the rhythm, symptoms, heart health, and the patient's preferences.

\section*{Role in Care}
This is a treatment, sometimes performed during an electrophysiology study that maps the heart's electrical activity. The expected benefit differs by arrhythmia. Ablation may eliminate some abnormal pathways, while for atrial fibrillation it more often aims to reduce episodes and symptoms. Arrhythmia can recur, and repeat treatment or medicine may still be needed.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The patient is connected to heart and oxygen monitors. Sedation or general anesthesia is used depending on the arrhythmia, energy source, and center. After numbing the skin, the clinician inserts catheters through one or more blood vessels, most often in the groin, and guides them to the heart using imaging and electrical mapping. Some left-heart procedures require the catheter to pass through a small opening between the upper chambers.

The clinician tests electrical signals, identifies the target, and applies the selected energy. The team checks the heart rhythm and watches for complications. The procedure may take several hours. The catheters and access sheaths are removed afterward, and pressure or a closure device is used to limit bleeding. The patient is monitored in a recovery area; an overnight stay may be needed.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews the rhythm diagnosis, heart imaging, medicines, allergies, kidney function, and other health conditions. The patient may need blood tests, an ECG, or imaging to check for a clot, depending on the arrhythmia and treatment plan. Follow the center's instructions about fasting and medicines.

Tell the electrophysiology team about all medicines, especially heart-rhythm medicines, anticoagulants (blood-thinning medicines), antiplatelet medicines, and diabetes medicines. Do not stop or change any medicine unless the electrophysiologist and prescribing clinician provide a plan. After atrial fibrillation ablation, the 2023 U.S. guideline recommends an anticoagulant for at least 3 months, while the 2024 European guideline recommends at least 2 months. Longer-term use depends on stroke risk and the clinician's plan, not only on whether the procedure appears successful.

\section*{Contraindications}
An active infection, a clot in the heart, uncontrolled bleeding risk, or unstable medical condition may require treatment first, additional precautions, or postponement.

\section*{Precautions}
Whether ablation is appropriate depends on the expected benefit and the person's health. A heart clot is assessed according to the clinical situation; an ultrasound scan using a probe in the esophagus (transesophageal echocardiogram) is not required for every patient. The team discusses arrhythmia-specific risks, benefits, and alternatives.

\section*{Risks}
Risks vary with the arrhythmia, catheter route, energy source, and the patient's health. They include bleeding, bruising, or injury to a blood vessel at the access site; abnormal heart rhythms; blood clots or stroke; damage to the heart or a surrounding structure; and fluid or blood around the heart that may need urgent drainage. Rarely, damage to the heart's normal electrical system can require a pacemaker.

Some complications are specific to the target or energy used. With thermal atrial fibrillation ablation, rare complications include injury to the esophagus, including a very rare abnormal connection between the esophagus and heart, or narrowing of a pulmonary vein. These complications appear less common with pulsed-field ablation, although risk varies by system and technique. Phrenic nerve injury (to a nerve that helps the diaphragm move during breathing) can occur with some procedures. Some pulsed-field systems have also been associated with coronary artery spasm and red blood cell breakdown that can injure the kidneys. For one pulsed-field system, the manufacturer updated its use instructions after four strokes or transient ischemic attacks were reported among the first 132 U.S. patients treated during an early evaluation. This was a device-specific finding, not an event rate for all pulsed-field ablation systems. The electrophysiologist will explain the important risks for the planned ablation.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The patient is observed while heart rhythm, blood pressure, and the catheter entry site are monitored. The leg may need to remain straight for several hours after groin access; the timing depends on how the site was closed and the team's instructions. Discharge may be the same day or the next day.

Keep the access site clean and follow instructions about bathing, driving, lifting, and exercise. Avoid strenuous activity for the period advised, often several days. Continue prescribed medicines, including anticoagulants, exactly as instructed. Brief palpitations or rhythm episodes can occur during healing, especially after atrial fibrillation ablation; contact the care team if symptoms are severe, prolonged, or concerning.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
Success is assessed differently for each arrhythmia. Follow-up may include symptom review, ECG, or ambulatory rhythm monitoring. Some patients need antiarrhythmic medicine or a repeat ablation. An early rhythm episode does not always mean the procedure failed, and absence of symptoms does not prove that atrial fibrillation will never recur.

After atrial fibrillation ablation, stroke-prevention treatment is based on the person's risk factors and clinician's plan. Continue anticoagulation for at least the initial 2 to 3 months as directed; the minimum is 3 months in the 2023 U.S. guideline and 2 months in the 2024 European guideline. Do not stop it on your own because the rhythm seems normal. Follow-up timing and monitoring are set by the electrophysiology team.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek emergency care for chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting, new weakness or numbness, facial drooping, or trouble speaking. In the weeks after atrial fibrillation ablation, fever with chest pain or painful or difficult swallowing, vomiting blood, or any stroke-like symptoms need emergency assessment because they can rarely signal an abnormal connection between the esophagus and heart. If the groin site bleeds, lie flat and apply firm pressure; call emergency services if bleeding does not stop. Contact the electrophysiology team promptly for a rapidly enlarging or painful groin lump, increasing redness or drainage at the site, fever, or a sustained fast or irregular heartbeat.
