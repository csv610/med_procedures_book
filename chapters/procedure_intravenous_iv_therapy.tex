\chapter{Intravenous (IV) Therapy}

\section*{Overview}
Intravenous (IV) therapy delivers fluid or medicine into a vein through an IV catheter. It is used when a treatment needs to enter the bloodstream directly or when the person's needs cannot be met safely by drinking, eating, or another route. IV access is the route; the fluid or medicine and its reason for use are the treatment. The choice of fluid, medicine, dose, and rate depends on the person's condition.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item IV catheter or existing vascular access
  \item infusion tubing, fluid bag, and pump when needed
  \item filter and infusion monitoring equipment as indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
IV infusion; intravenous infusion; IV fluids; IV medication administration; infusion therapy

\section*{Principle}
An IV delivers fluid or medicine directly into the circulation, bypassing the digestive system. Some treatments act quickly, while the time to effect depends on the substance and condition. Fluid and medicine enter the bloodstream immediately, so the amount and rate must be prescribed carefully. IV therapy can cause harm if the wrong substance or amount is given or if the body cannot handle the added fluid.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
IV therapy may be used for fluid resuscitation, replacement of ongoing losses, or maintenance when oral or tube feeding cannot meet fluid needs. It may also deliver medicines such as antibiotics, pain medicines, or chemotherapy when the IV route is appropriate. Blood transfusion and IV nutrition require additional, specific procedures and safety checks. IV fluids are not needed for every case of dehydration; oral or enteral fluids are preferred when they can meet the person's needs.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
IV therapy is a treatment method, not a diagnostic test. It supports care for a specific problem and is stopped when it is no longer needed or when another route is suitable.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician identifies the reason for treatment and selects the appropriate fluid or medicine, dose, route, and rate. It is given through a suitable vascular access device, such as a short peripheral IV or a central catheter; insertion of a peripheral IV is described separately in the Intravenous Cannulation chapter. The medicine may be given as a single dose, intermittent infusion, or continuous infusion, sometimes using a pump. Staff check the patient's identity, the order, allergies, medication compatibility, and the line before and during administration. IV fluid prescriptions are planned around the purpose of treatment: resuscitation, routine maintenance, replacement of losses, redistribution of fluid, and reassessment.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the care team about allergies, heart, kidney or liver disease, pregnancy, medicines, and previous reactions to IV medicines or fluids. Ask what is being given and why. Fasting is not usually needed for IV fluids or medication alone, but other procedures or sedating medicines may require it. The amount and rate may be adjusted based on examination, blood tests, urine output, vital signs, and other sources of fluid such as drinks, feeding, and medication infusions.

\section*{Contraindications}
The IV site or type of access should be changed if it is infected, painful, inflamed, blocked, leaking, or otherwise unsuitable. IV fluid is generally used only when oral or enteral intake is insufficient or not appropriate.

\section*{Precautions}
There is no single fluid or medicine that is suitable for everyone. The clinician considers the diagnosis, fluid status, electrolyte and kidney results, ongoing losses, and other treatments. Heart, kidney, or liver problems can increase the risk of fluid overload and require careful fluid selection, rate, and monitoring. Some medicines can irritate veins or damage tissue if they leak, and may need a central line or a specific infusion protocol.

\section*{Risks}
Possible complications include pain, bruising, vein inflammation (phlebitis), infection, blockage, or displacement of the IV catheter. Fluid leaking into tissue is called infiltration; leakage of a medicine that can injure tissue is called extravasation. Some medicines can cause an allergic or infusion reaction. Excess fluid can cause swelling or fluid in the lungs, and inappropriate fluid or electrolyte administration can disturb blood chemistry or worsen kidney or heart problems. Serious complications are more likely when the wrong medicine, dose, rate, or access device is used.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The care team monitors the response and adjusts or stops treatment as needed. Depending on the reason for therapy, monitoring may include symptoms, blood pressure, pulse, breathing, fluid intake and output, body weight, kidney function, and blood electrolyte levels. The IV site is checked regularly for pain, redness, swelling, leakage, or warmth. The catheter is removed when it is no longer needed or if a complication develops.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The desired result depends on the reason for treatment, such as improved circulation after fluid resuscitation, replacement of a measured loss, or delivery of a prescribed medicine. The clinician reassesses whether IV therapy is helping and whether the person can switch to oral or another route. A change in symptoms alone may not show that fluid balance or blood chemistry has returned to normal; examination and tests may also be needed.

\section*{Expected Results}
The intended fluid or medicine is delivered at the prescribed rate, with improvement in the treated condition and no evidence of a line complication, adverse drug reaction, or fluid overload. The result varies with the underlying illness and the specific treatment.

\section*{Follow-up}
Continue the monitoring and laboratory tests recommended for the condition and treatment. Tell staff promptly about new symptoms during an infusion. Once IV treatment is no longer necessary, the team removes the line and updates the care plan to use oral, enteral, or other treatment when suitable.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
While receiving IV therapy, alert staff immediately for pain, burning, tightness, swelling, redness, coolness, wetness, or leakage at the IV site, or if an infusion pump alarms. Seek emergency help for difficulty breathing, facial or throat swelling, widespread hives, fainting, or sudden severe symptoms during an infusion. New shortness of breath, rapidly increasing swelling, or sudden weight gain during fluid treatment also needs prompt medical assessment.
