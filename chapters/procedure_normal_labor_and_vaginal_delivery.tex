\chapter{Labor and Vaginal Delivery}

\section*{Overview}
Labor is the process in which contractions help the cervix thin and open so the baby can be born. In a vaginal birth, the baby passes through the vagina; the placenta is delivered afterward. Labor may begin on its own or be started with medicines or other methods. Birth can be unmedicated or include pain relief and other interventions. The care team and birthing person make decisions together, adjusting the plan as labor and the health of the parent and baby change.

\section*{How Labor Progresses}
Labor is usually described in three stages. In the first stage, contractions cause the cervix to thin and open to about 10 cm. The early part can take time, and the pace varies widely. ACOG considers 6 cm the beginning of the active phase; labor is not judged to be arrested just because cervical change is slower than an older one-centimeter-per-hour rule. In the second stage, the cervix is fully open and the baby moves down and is born. Pushing may be guided or follow the person's urge, and position can vary. In the third stage, the placenta separates and is delivered. The team also observes the parent and newborn closely during the first hours after birth.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item fetal heart-rate monitor and maternal vital-signs monitor
  \item delivery instruments, sterile gloves, and clamps
  \item newborn warming, suction, and resuscitation equipment as needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Vaginal birth is one way for a baby to be born and is the usual plan when it is safe for the pregnant person and baby. The plan may change if a complication develops or if vaginal birth is not the safest option. Labor induction, assisted vaginal birth with forceps or vacuum, and cesarean birth are different interventions that may be considered for specific reasons.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Labor and vaginal birth are physiologic processes, not diagnostic tests. Maternity care includes support, assessment, and treatment when needed. The method of birth is chosen according to the clinical situation and the pregnant person's informed preferences.

\section*{Preparation and Care}
Before and during labor, the care team reviews pregnancy history, the baby's position, maternal health, and any known risks. Monitoring may include the parent's pulse, blood pressure, temperature, contractions, and the baby's heart rate. For a low-risk labor, the baby's heart rate may be checked intermittently; continuous electronic monitoring may be recommended if risk factors or concerns arise. The team explains the method and what it can and cannot show. A birth plan may describe preferred support people, movement and positions, pain relief, and newborn care. A chosen companion, respectful communication, privacy, and informed consent are important parts of care.

\section*{Pain Relief}
Pain and coping needs vary. Options can include continuous support, movement and position changes, breathing or relaxation, water immersion where available and appropriate, nitrous oxide, injected or IV medicines, and epidural or other neuraxial analgesia. The available choices and their risks depend on the setting, stage of labor, and the health of the parent and baby. A person can ask about or change pain-relief preferences during labor.

\section*{How Vaginal Birth Is Managed}
When the cervix is fully open and the baby is low enough, the birthing person pushes with contractions or follows an urge to push. The clinician or midwife monitors the baby's progress and heart rate and supports a safe birth. After the baby is born, a medicine that helps the uterus contract—usually oxytocin—is commonly offered to reduce heavy bleeding. If parent and newborn are stable, cord clamping is usually delayed for at least about one minute, and early skin-to-skin contact can begin. The placenta is delivered and checked, and the team assesses uterine contraction, bleeding, and any tears that may need repair. Newborn breathing, temperature, and transition are assessed; urgent care takes priority if either patient needs it.

\section*{Contraindications}
The specific condition and available clinical expertise matter; no single checklist determines the route for everyone.

\section*{Precautions}
Vaginal birth may not be the safest route with certain placental problems, a baby lying across the uterus, some types of prior uterine surgery, or a complication affecting the parent or baby. The team should explain concerns and options, and may recommend assisted vaginal or cesarean birth if this becomes safer. In an urgent emergency, decisions may need to happen quickly, with explanation and consent whenever circumstances allow.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
Possible complications include labor that is prolonged or stops progressing, changes in the baby's heart rate, infection, heavy bleeding after birth, retained placenta, and tears of the vagina or perineum. Shoulder dystocia, when the baby's shoulder becomes stuck after the head is born, is uncommon but requires immediate trained assistance. Forceps or vacuum may be used in selected situations; these can cause injury to the parent or baby and are not always suitable. Cesarean birth may be recommended if vaginal birth is not progressing safely. Serious complications can occur even when pregnancy and labor were previously low risk, which is why access to skilled care and emergency support matters.

\section*{Recovery}
After birth, staff monitor bleeding, uterine firmness, vital signs, pain, and urination. Tears or an episiotomy are assessed and repaired if needed. The newborn is checked and may receive routine preventive care, feeding support, and skin-to-skin contact when stable. The timing of discharge and recovery vary with the health of parent and baby, the course of labor, and local practice. Recovery can include vaginal soreness, bleeding, cramping, fatigue, and emotional changes.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The goal is a safe birth and recovery for the parent and newborn. Labor length, need for pain relief, and whether interventions are needed cannot be predicted exactly. A difficult or unexpected birth may also require emotional support.

\section*{Expected Results}
The baby and placenta are delivered, and the care team confirms that the parent and newborn are stable. Vaginal delivery does not always mean a birth without medication, instruments, stitches, or other care. Some people need additional treatment or a different route of birth for safety.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow the maternity team's instructions for postnatal visits, wound care, bleeding, pain, feeding, and newborn follow-up. Ask for support with pelvic-floor recovery, contraception, mood changes, or birth-related distress. Continue care for pregnancy conditions such as high blood pressure or diabetes as advised after delivery.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
During pregnancy or labor, contact the maternity team urgently for heavy vaginal bleeding, reduced or stopped baby movements, fluid leaking from the vagina, contractions before 37 weeks, fever, severe constant abdominal pain, or a cord or part of the baby visible at the vagina. Follow local instructions about when to come in for contractions or waters breaking. During the year after birth, seek urgent medical care for heavy bleeding (such as soaking a pad in an hour), fever of 38\,$^\circ$C (100.4\,$^\circ$F) or higher, severe headache or vision changes, chest pain, trouble breathing, fainting, severe abdominal pain, or a painful swollen leg. If the newborn has difficulty breathing, turns blue, is unusually difficult to wake, or cannot feed, seek emergency care.
