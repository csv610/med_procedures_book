\chapter{Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy}

\section*{Overview}
Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) is a medical treatment in which a person breathes near-pure oxygen inside a purpose-built chamber where air pressure is higher than normal. This allows more oxygen to dissolve in the blood. HBOT is used for specific conditions and is often combined with other urgent or ongoing treatment. It is not a general treatment for every wound, infection, or chronic illness.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item hyperbaric chamber and oxygen delivery system
  \item patient mask or hood when used
  \item chamber pressure, oxygen, and vital-signs monitors
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Hyperbaric oxygen treatment, HBOT, hyperbaric medicine

\section*{Why It Is Done}
HBOT is used for specific conditions where evidence supports treatment with oxygen under pressure. Examples include decompression sickness, air or gas bubbles in blood vessels, selected cases of carbon monoxide poisoning, gas gangrene, some severe tissue infections, crush injuries with threatened blood flow, certain severe diabetic foot ulcers that have not improved with standard wound care or after surgery, refractory bone infection, tissue injury after radiation, compromised skin grafts or flaps, and certain severe burns or other specific conditions. The exact indications vary among medical guidelines and countries. A diabetic foot ulcer or other wound is not automatically a reason for HBOT. For infections or injuries, HBOT is generally an addition to standard treatment, such as surgery, antibiotics, wound care, or oxygen at normal pressure; it should not delay those treatments. Many uses promoted by some clinics, including treatment of autism, cancer, or Alzheimer's disease, have not been cleared by the FDA.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
HBOT is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. It may be an urgent part of care for decompression sickness or gas embolism and is an adjunct for many other conditions. A clinician trained in hyperbaric medicine assesses whether the condition is an accepted indication and whether the potential benefit outweighs the risks.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
Treatment takes place in a single-person chamber or a larger chamber for several people. In a single-person chamber, the chamber itself is filled with oxygen. In a larger chamber, the room is pressurized and each person breathes oxygen through a mask or hood. Pressure and treatment time depend on the condition and protocol. Many sessions last about 90 minutes to two hours, but emergency treatment and other protocols can differ. Staff monitor the patient throughout. Some treatment plans involve only a few sessions; others require repeated sessions over days or weeks.

\section*{Preparation}
The hyperbaric team reviews the reason for treatment, medical history, medicines, lung and ear problems, and any prior chest injury or surgery. Tell the team if you have diabetes, a recent cold or sinus infection, difficulty equalizing ear pressure, a history of seizures, or anxiety in enclosed spaces. Blood sugar may be checked before and after treatment in people with diabetes, especially those using insulin. Follow the facility's instructions about eating, medicines, clothing, and personal items. For fire safety, facilities may require approved clothing and prohibit items such as electronics, lighters, oils, lotions, makeup, or hair products. Do not bring anything into the chamber unless staff approve it.

\section*{Contraindications}
An untreated pneumothorax (collapsed lung with trapped air around the lung) is a major contraindication because pressure changes can make it dangerous. These factors do not all automatically rule out treatment.

\section*{Precautions}
Other lung conditions that trap air, ear or sinus problems, fever, diabetes, seizure risk, pregnancy, and some medicines or chemotherapy may require additional assessment or changes to the treatment plan. The hyperbaric physician reviews the individual risks, including possible interactions with medicines, before therapy.

\section*{Risks}
Pressure changes can cause ear or sinus pain or injury; some people have trouble equalizing pressure. Vision may become temporarily more nearsighted, particularly after repeated treatments. Other possible effects include fatigue, low blood sugar in people with diabetes, and claustrophobia. Less common serious risks include oxygen-related seizures, lung injury or collapse, and injury to the eardrum. The oxygen-rich chamber environment has a fire risk, so staff follow strict device and facility safety procedures.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After each session, staff may check the ears, vital signs, symptoms, or blood sugar. Most people can return to usual activities, but instructions depend on the condition and treatment plan. Tell staff about ear pain, breathing problems, chest discomfort, unusual visual changes, or other symptoms during or after a session. Wound care, antibiotics, surgery, or other treatment may continue alongside HBOT.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The care team evaluates the condition being treated, such as wound healing, tissue injury, or symptoms from a diving-related illness. The number of treatments depends on the diagnosis and response; there is no standard course for every patient. HBOT may not help, and it does not replace other necessary care. The team discusses whether to continue, change, or stop treatment based on progress and side effects.

\section*{Expected Results}
The expected benefit depends on the specific condition and how early treatment begins. Some emergencies need only a few sessions, while chronic wounds or radiation injury may require many. Improvement is not guaranteed. A provider should explain the evidence and reasonable alternatives for the proposed indication.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow-up may involve the hyperbaric team and clinicians managing the underlying condition. Continue any prescribed wound care, antibiotics, or other treatment. Report new ear, lung, vision, or neurologic symptoms, and keep appointments to review progress and treatment risks.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Tell chamber staff immediately if you develop severe ear or sinus pain, chest pain, trouble breathing, severe anxiety, or any sudden change in how you feel during treatment. After a session, seek urgent medical care for a seizure, sudden vision loss, severe breathing difficulty, or chest pain. Contact the hyperbaric team about persistent ear pain, hearing changes, or vision changes.
