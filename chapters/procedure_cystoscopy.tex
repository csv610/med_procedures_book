\chapter{Cystoscopy}

\section*{Overview}
Cystoscopy uses a thin camera to look inside the urethra and bladder. A flexible cystoscope is commonly used for an office examination with numbing gel. A rigid cystoscope is used for some procedures that require biopsy or treatment and may be done with sedation, spinal anesthesia, or general anesthesia. The exact plan depends on why the procedure is needed and what may be done during it.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item cystoscope with light source or video system
  \item irrigation fluid and tubing
  \item biopsy forceps, guidewire, or catheter when required
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Cystourethroscopy; bladder endoscopy; bladder scope.

\section*{Principle}
The cystoscope passes through the urethra into the bladder. Sterile fluid is used to expand the bladder so the clinician can inspect its lining. Small instruments can be passed through some scopes to take a biopsy, remove a small bladder stone, or perform other treatment. Cystoscopy examines the lower urinary tract; imaging or other tests may also be needed to assess the kidneys and ureters.

\section*{History}
Early endoscopes appeared in the 19th century. Improvements in electric lighting and optics made modern cystoscopy possible in the late 1800s.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Cystoscopy may be used to investigate visible blood in the urine, persistent microscopic blood when evaluation is indicated, urinary symptoms, recurrent infections in selected cases, suspected bladder or urethral abnormalities, or findings on imaging. It may also be used to monitor a known bladder condition, take a biopsy, remove or place a urinary stent, or treat a lesion. The need for cystoscopy depends on the person's symptoms, risk factors, and prior test results.

\section*{Role in Care}
Cystoscopy is a direct visual examination and may also be used to treat a finding. It is one part of a broader evaluation when other parts of the urinary tract may be involved.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
For flexible cystoscopy, the patient usually lies on an exam table. The clinician cleans the area and places anesthetic lubricant into the urethra. The flexible scope is gently passed through the urethra into the bladder while sterile fluid is used to fill it. The patient may feel pressure, a need to urinate, or brief discomfort.

Rigid cystoscopy or a procedure involving biopsy or treatment may be done in an operating room with local, spinal, or general anesthesia, depending on the procedure. The clinician explains the anesthesia plan and whether a catheter may be needed afterward.

\section*{Preparation}
Preparation depends on the type of cystoscopy and anesthesia. Follow instructions about eating, drinking, medicines, and whether to arrive with a full or empty bladder. A urine sample may be checked for infection. Tell the team about urinary symptoms, allergies, pregnancy, bleeding problems, kidney disease, and medicines, especially blood thinners. Do not stop medicines unless the team gives you a plan. Antibiotics are not routinely needed for every uncomplicated diagnostic cystoscopy; they may be used when infection or individual risk factors make them appropriate.

\section*{Contraindications}
An untreated symptomatic urinary infection may lead the clinician to delay a planned cystoscopy until it is treated, unless urgent evaluation is needed. The clinician decides whether to postpone, modify, or proceed based on urgency and individual risk.

\section*{Precautions}
Bleeding risk, urethral narrowing, recent urinary surgery, pregnancy, and the planned type of anesthesia may change the approach.

\section*{Risks}
Mild burning when urinating, a small amount of blood in the urine, and urinary frequency or urgency can occur for a short time. Other risks include urinary infection, difficulty passing urine, bleeding, and injury or a tear in the urethra or bladder. The risks may be higher when biopsy, stone treatment, or another intervention is performed, or when rigid cystoscopy and anesthesia are used.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After flexible cystoscopy, most people go home shortly afterward. Drink a normal amount of fluid unless the care team has advised a fluid restriction; do not force fluids. Mild burning or a small amount of blood may occur for a day or two. Recovery after rigid cystoscopy, biopsy, or treatment depends on the anesthesia and what was done. Follow instructions for activity, wound or catheter care, and restarting medicines.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The clinician may discuss what was seen during the examination. A biopsy or sample may need laboratory analysis, and results can take days or longer. Follow-up depends on the findings and the reason for the test. Cystoscopy can identify some bladder or urethral problems, but it does not by itself assess kidney function, measure prostate size, or rule out every cause of urinary symptoms or blood in the urine.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the care team promptly for fever or chills, worsening pain, cloudy or foul-smelling urine with illness, or increasing blood in the urine. Seek urgent care if you cannot urinate, pass large clots, have heavy bleeding, or feel seriously unwell. Follow the procedure team's contact instructions, especially if a biopsy or treatment was performed.
