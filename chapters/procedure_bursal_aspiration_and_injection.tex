\chapter{Bursal Aspiration and Injection}

\section*{Overview}
A bursa is a small fluid-filled sac that helps reduce friction between tissues near a joint. Bursal aspiration uses a needle to remove fluid from a swollen bursa. The fluid may be tested for infection or crystals, or removed to ease pressure. A corticosteroid may sometimes be injected to reduce inflammation, but it is not appropriate for every type of bursitis and should not be injected when infection is suspected.

Commonly affected bursae include those near the elbow, kneecap, shoulder, and outer hip. The best approach depends on the site and suspected cause.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Bursal aspiration; bursa aspiration; bursal corticosteroid injection; aspiration and injection of a bursa.

\section*{Principle}
Fluid analysis can help distinguish infection, crystal-related inflammation, and other causes of bursitis. A corticosteroid injection may reduce inflammation and pain in selected noninfectious conditions. Aspiration or injection does not replace treatment of an underlying infection or other cause.

\section*{History}
Needle aspiration and local corticosteroid treatment became more widely used as injection medicines and sterile techniques became available. Current use depends on the bursa, diagnosis, and evidence of benefit and risk.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Aspiration may be recommended when a swollen bursa could be infected or affected by gout or another crystal condition, when the cause is unclear, or when a large painful fluid collection limits movement. It is generally avoided for typical chronic, noninfectious superficial bursitis because needle entry can introduce infection.

Corticosteroid injection may be considered for selected persistent, noninfectious bursitis that has not improved with measures such as activity modification, padding, or other appropriate treatment. For some superficial bursae, including the olecranon bursa at the elbow, steroid injection is generally used sparingly because of risks such as skin thinning and infection and limited evidence of long-term benefit.

\section*{Role in Care}
Aspiration is a diagnostic sampling procedure and may also relieve pressure. Injection is a treatment. Neither is the first step for every case of bursitis; treatment depends on the cause and may begin with noninvasive measures.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician examines the area and may use ultrasound to locate a deep bursa or confirm a fluid collection. The skin is cleaned using sterile technique, and local anesthetic may be used. A needle is passed into the bursa and fluid is drawn into a syringe. If infection or crystals are being considered, the sample may be sent for cell count, Gram stain, culture, and crystal analysis; fluid glucose may also be measured.

If a corticosteroid injection is appropriate, it may be given during the same visit or at a later visit, depending on the diagnosis and whether infection has been excluded. The clinician places the medicine in or around the bursa as appropriate for the site, taking care not to inject into a tendon. A small dressing is applied.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the clinician about allergies, diabetes, immune suppression, bleeding problems, fever or recent infection, and all medicines, including anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs. Do not stop prescribed blood thinners or other medicines unless the prescribing clinician and procedural clinician provide a plan. Tell the clinician about prior injections and whether they helped or caused a reaction.

Usually no fasting or sedation is needed. If aspiration is planned to test for infection, the sample is usually collected before antibiotics when this can be done without delaying urgent treatment, because antibiotics can reduce the chance of identifying the organism. Do not delay urgent care for a suspected infection in order to arrange aspiration.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Do not inject corticosteroid into a bursa when septic bursitis is suspected or has not been reasonably excluded. A needle should not be passed through infected skin. If a deeper infection is suspected, the clinician may choose an alternative route, imaging guidance, or another diagnostic approach. Allergy to the planned medicine or anesthetic is also important.

Bleeding disorders or anticoagulant use require an individualized assessment; they are not automatically a reason to stop medication or cancel every aspiration. Diabetes is a precaution because corticosteroid can temporarily raise blood glucose. Repeated injections or injections near tendons require caution because corticosteroids can weaken tissue.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary pain, bruising, bleeding, or a short-lived increase in pain after injection can occur. Other risks include infection, skin thinning or a lighter skin color at the injection site, and temporary elevation of blood glucose in people with diabetes. Rarely, a person may have an allergic reaction to the injected medicine or anesthetic. Tendon injury or rupture is uncommon but can occur, especially if corticosteroid is injected into a tendon or injections are repeated. Fluid may return after aspiration, and symptoms may not improve.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The site is covered with a small dressing. Follow the clinician's instructions about keeping it clean, using ice, and resting or limiting strenuous use of the area for the recommended period. Mild soreness is common. If corticosteroid was injected, pain may briefly worsen before it improves; the timing and duration of relief vary. Corticosteroid can raise blood glucose for a time. People with diabetes should follow their care team's advice about checking glucose more often for at least 48 hours; monitoring may need to continue for several days, sometimes up to a week, until levels return to usual.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
If fluid was sent to a laboratory, results may take several days. A negative initial test does not always exclude infection; the clinician interprets results with the examination and other findings. If infection is found or remains likely, treatment may include antibiotics and sometimes repeat drainage. If crystals are found, treatment is directed at the crystal condition.

Improvement after injection varies and may be temporary. If swelling or pain persists or returns, the clinician reassesses the diagnosis and may recommend imaging, different treatment, or referral. Repeated injections are not a substitute for reassessment.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the treating clinician promptly for increasing redness, warmth, swelling, or pain; drainage; fever; or feeling unwell after the procedure. Seek emergency care for trouble breathing or swelling of the face or throat after injection. Seek urgent care for rapidly worsening symptoms, severe pain, or inability to move the nearby joint.
