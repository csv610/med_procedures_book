\chapter{Intrauterine Insemination}

\section*{Overview}
Intrauterine insemination (IUI) is a fertility treatment in which prepared sperm are placed into the uterus around the time of ovulation. It can be done with sperm from a partner or a donor, in a natural cycle or with fertility medicines. IUI does not fertilize an egg in the laboratory; fertilization, if it occurs, happens inside the body. Whether IUI is appropriate depends on the cause of infertility, age, test results, and personal preferences.

\section*{Alternative Names}
IUI; intrauterine insemination; artificial insemination (a broader term); donor insemination (when donor sperm is used)

\section*{Principle}
A laboratory processes the semen sample to separate motile sperm from seminal fluid and other material. The prepared sperm are passed through a thin catheter through the cervix into the uterus near ovulation. This places sperm closer to the fallopian tubes, where fertilization may occur, but does not guarantee fertilization or pregnancy.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
IUI may be considered for unexplained infertility, some mild sperm-related problems, difficulty with ejaculation or intercourse, or when donor sperm is used. It may be combined with ovulation-inducing medicines for selected patients. Before treatment, fertility evaluation typically includes assessment of ovulation, semen, and whether at least one fallopian tube is open. IUI is less likely to help when both tubes are blocked, sperm numbers or function are severely impaired, or other factors make IVF more suitable. For unexplained infertility, guidelines commonly recommend a limited course of oral-medication ovarian stimulation with IUI before considering IVF, but treatment is individualized; age, diagnosis, previous treatment, and preferences can change the plan.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
IUI is a fertility treatment, not a diagnostic test. It may be used as an initial treatment for selected patients or after evaluation or treatment of ovulation problems. The procedure itself does not determine the cause of infertility.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinic tracks the menstrual cycle and ovulation using urine hormone tests, blood tests, ultrasound, or a combination. Some cycles use medicines to stimulate egg development, sometimes followed by a medicine that triggers ovulation; others use natural ovulation. Near the predicted time of ovulation, a fresh or thawed sperm sample is processed in the laboratory. A clinician places the prepared sample through a soft, narrow catheter into the uterus. The procedure usually takes only a few minutes and does not usually require anesthesia. The timing and monitoring plan depend on the treatment cycle.

\section*{Preparation}
Complete the fertility tests and infection screening requested by the clinic. Tell the team about medicines, allergies, prior pelvic infections or surgery, and any problems providing a semen sample. Follow the clinic's instructions about medication, sample collection, and timing; abstinence instructions vary by laboratory and situation. A full bladder is not routinely necessary unless the clinic specifically asks for one. If donor sperm is used, the clinic follows the applicable donor screening and consent requirements.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Active pelvic or genital infection should be evaluated and treated before insemination. IUI is generally not useful if both fallopian tubes are blocked. Severe sperm abnormalities or other substantial fertility factors may make IVF or another approach more appropriate. If fertility medicines are used, ultrasound monitoring helps assess the number of developing follicles. The clinician may cancel or change a cycle if too many follicles develop, because insemination in that situation can raise the risk of a multiple pregnancy. The chance of success and the safest treatment plan should be discussed before starting a cycle.

\section*{Risks}
The catheter may cause brief cramping or light spotting. Infection after IUI is uncommon. The main added risks come from fertility medicines, not the catheter: ovarian stimulation can cause a multiple pregnancy, and injectable medicines can rarely cause ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS). Severe OHSS is uncommon with IUI treatment but can be serious. A multiple pregnancy increases risks to the pregnant person and babies, including premature birth. Ectopic pregnancy is also possible, as with any pregnancy, and infertility-related factors may affect this risk.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people can return to usual activities after the procedure; a short rest at the clinic may be offered for comfort. Bed rest or avoiding ordinary exercise has not been shown to improve the chance of pregnancy. Follow the clinic's instructions about any prescribed medicines, including progesterone if used. Mild cramping or spotting can occur briefly.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The clinic will tell you when to take a pregnancy test, usually about 14 days after insemination. Testing too early can give a misleading result, particularly if an ovulation-trigger injection containing hCG was used. If the test is positive, the fertility team arranges follow-up and an ultrasound at the appropriate time to confirm the location and development of the pregnancy. If the cycle does not result in pregnancy, the team reviews the response and discusses another cycle, a change in treatment, or IVF. Pregnancy rates vary by age, diagnosis, sperm source and quality, medication, and clinic; a positive pregnancy test is not the same as a live birth.

\section*{Expected Results}
The aim is to place a suitable prepared sperm sample in the uterus at the right time. Pregnancy may or may not occur in a given cycle. No single success rate applies to everyone, and IUI is not more effective than IVF for every fertility problem.

\section*{Follow-up}
Take the pregnancy test on the date advised by the clinic and continue or stop cycle medicines only as directed. If IUI is unsuccessful, the next step depends on age, diagnosis, ovarian response, prior cycles, and treatment goals. Seek early follow-up if the pregnancy test is positive and you develop pain or bleeding, since the clinic may need to assess for an ectopic pregnancy.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the clinic for fever, worsening pelvic pain, heavy bleeding, or unusual vaginal discharge after IUI. If fertility medicines were used, seek urgent care for severe abdominal pain or swelling, persistent vomiting, rapid weight gain, shortness of breath, fainting, or markedly reduced urination; these can be signs of OHSS. With a positive pregnancy test, urgent assessment is needed for severe one-sided pelvic pain, shoulder-tip pain, dizziness or fainting, or heavy bleeding, which may signal an ectopic pregnancy or other complication.
