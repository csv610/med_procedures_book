\chapter{Endoscopic Ultrasound}

\section*{Overview}
Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) combines a flexible camera (endoscope) with an ultrasound probe. The probe sits close to the digestive tract and uses sound waves to show its wall and nearby organs and tissues. EUS can also guide a needle to collect cells, tissue, or fluid. The approach and preparation depend on the area being examined and whether sampling or treatment is planned.

\section*{Alternative Names}
EUS; echo-endoscopy; endoscopic ultrasonography. Needle sampling may be called EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA) or fine-needle biopsy (FNB).

\section*{Principle}
The endoscope carries a small ultrasound transducer at its tip. From inside the esophagus, stomach, or duodenum, it can image the layers of the digestive tract and nearby structures such as the pancreas, bile ducts, gallbladder, and lymph nodes. A lower EUS scope can be passed through the anus to examine the rectum and nearby tissues. If needed, the clinician can guide a thin needle through the endoscope to sample a target. EUS uses sound waves, not X-rays; other imaging or procedures may still be needed.

\section*{History}
EUS developed in the late 20th century by combining endoscopy with ultrasound. Improvements in imaging and needle tools expanded its use from examining the digestive tract wall to evaluating nearby organs and obtaining samples.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
EUS may help evaluate a lump, cyst, or abnormal area in or near the digestive tract; examine the pancreas, bile ducts, or gallbladder; investigate selected bile-duct stones; or assess how deeply a tumor may extend and whether nearby lymph nodes look abnormal. EUS-guided sampling may help identify the cause of a lesion. In selected cases, EUS also guides treatment, such as draining a fluid collection, but therapeutic EUS is more complex and has different risks from a diagnostic examination.

\section*{Role in Care}
EUS is usually one part of a diagnostic work-up. It may follow other tests, such as CT, MRI, ultrasound, or standard endoscopy, when more detailed images or a tissue sample could help guide decisions. It can contribute to cancer staging, but cannot determine the full stage or guarantee that a lesion is benign or malignant on its own.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
For an upper EUS, the endoscope is passed through the mouth into the esophagus, stomach, or duodenum. For a lower EUS, it is passed through the anus into the rectum. The clinician moves the ultrasound probe to obtain images. If a sample is needed, a needle is passed through the endoscope and guided into the target under ultrasound. Many examinations use intravenous sedation; some require general anesthesia. The team monitors breathing, heart rate, and blood pressure. The procedure may take longer if sampling or treatment is performed.

\section*{Preparation}
Follow the endoscopy unit's instructions, which vary by route and planned procedure. Upper EUS generally requires fasting so the stomach is empty; lower EUS may require an enema or bowel preparation. Tell the team about all medicines, allergies, health conditions, implanted devices, and prior problems with sedation or anesthesia. Blood thinners and diabetes medicines may need an individualized plan, especially if a needle sample is planned. Do not stop or change medicines without instructions. Arrange an escort and follow the unit's advice about driving after sedation.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
The team considers whether a person can safely undergo endoscopy and sedation, and whether needle sampling is safe given bleeding risk, medicines, or the location of the target. Infection risk may matter when sampling certain fluid-filled areas, such as pancreatic cysts. Pregnancy should be discussed if relevant, particularly if X-ray imaging or treatment is planned. A different test or approach may be safer in some situations.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary sore throat, bloating, or mild discomfort can occur. Uncommon complications include a tear or hole in the digestive tract, bleeding (especially after needle sampling), infection, and problems related to sedation, such as slowed breathing or an allergic reaction. Pancreatitis can occur after some pancreas-related EUS-guided procedures. Risk depends on what is done: a treatment such as drainage has different and often greater risks than imaging alone. A sample may also be too small or unclear to provide a diagnosis, and a finding can be missed.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Staff monitor the patient as sedation wears off. If the throat was numbed, the team will tell you when it is safe to eat or drink. Mild throat soreness or bloating usually settles. If sedation was used, follow instructions about an escort and avoiding driving, alcohol, machinery, and important decisions for the specified period, often 24 hours. The treating team will explain when and how biopsy results will be shared.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
EUS provides images of the digestive tract wall and nearby structures. The report may describe their appearance and any sample taken. Imaging alone cannot always establish a definite diagnosis; pathology results, other scans, symptoms, and clinical history may all be needed. The clinician discusses whether the result answers the original question and what further evaluation or treatment is appropriate.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no single normal result. EUS may show no concerning abnormality or may identify a finding that needs monitoring, sampling, or treatment. A normal-looking study does not rule out every condition, and a nondiagnostic sample does not necessarily mean that a lesion is harmless.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend the appointment or contact the clinician as directed to review the report and any pathology results. Depending on the findings, follow-up may include observation, repeat imaging or EUS, another test, specialist consultation, or treatment. If EUS-guided therapy was performed, follow the separate recovery and follow-up plan for that treatment.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the endoscopy team promptly for severe or persistent chest or abdominal pain, fever or chills, repeated vomiting, vomiting blood, black or bloody stools, or feeling faint or very unwell. Seek emergency care for trouble breathing, severe pain, heavy bleeding, or rapidly worsening symptoms. Use the contact instructions provided by the treating unit.
