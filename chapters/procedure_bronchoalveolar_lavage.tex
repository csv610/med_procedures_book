\chapter{Bronchoalveolar Lavage}

\section*{Overview}
Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) is a sampling procedure performed during bronchoscopy. The clinician guides a flexible camera tube into a selected part of the lung, puts sterile salt water through it, and suctions some of the fluid back. The recovered fluid can contain cells, germs, and other material from the lower airways and air sacs. A laboratory tests the sample for a specific clinical question.

BAL is not the same as a lung biopsy: it collects fluid and cells rather than a piece of lung tissue. It is usually an adjunct to examination, imaging, and other tests.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item flexible bronchoscope, video processor, and monitor
  \item sterile saline, suction tubing, and collection trap
  \item oxygen-delivery and cardiorespiratory monitoring equipment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
BAL; bronchoscopic lavage; bronchoalveolar washing.

\section*{How the Sample Is Tested}
The returned fluid is sent for only the tests needed for the clinical question. These may include the number and types of cells (a cell differential), tests for bacteria, fungi, or mycobacteria, selected viral or other molecular tests, or examination of cells under a microscope (cytology). A BAL sample does not automatically undergo every test, and what it can show depends on the amount and quality of fluid recovered.

\section*{History}
BAL became possible with flexible bronchoscopy and is now used to sample lower-airway fluid for selected infections and lung disorders.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
BAL may help investigate an unexplained abnormal area (or “shadow”) on lung imaging, a suspected lower respiratory infection (including some infections in people with weakened immune systems), selected interstitial lung diseases (conditions affecting the tissue around the air sacs), bleeding into the air sacs, or other uncommon lung disorders. It may also be used to assess certain cancers or to check response to treatment. For many interstitial lung diseases, BAL is used selectively, such as when infection or another diagnosis needs to be excluded. Whether BAL is useful depends on the suspected condition, imaging, other test results, and the patient's ability to tolerate bronchoscopy.

\section*{Role in Diagnosis}
BAL is a diagnostic sampling procedure, usually performed as part of bronchoscopy. It is often used when examination, imaging, sputum, blood tests, or other less invasive tests do not answer the clinical question, but it may be selected earlier when timely lower-airway samples are important. It does not provide a tissue biopsy.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The patient is positioned for bronchoscopy. The clinician usually applies numbing medicine to the nose or throat and may give sedation through a vein. The bronchoscope is guided through the nose or mouth into the airways. The clinician advances it to a chosen lung segment, gently holds its tip in place, and instills several small amounts of sterile saline. The fluid is then suctioned back into a collection trap. The volume and lung segment sampled depend on the patient's condition and reason for testing; for some lung conditions, recent imaging helps the team choose the area to sample.

The procedure may also include other sampling, such as a brush or tissue biopsy. These additional procedures have different risks; the clinician should explain which procedures are planned. The patient may cough or feel pressure during lavage. Sedation can make the procedure more comfortable, but does not guarantee that there will be no discomfort.

\section*{Preparation}
Follow the bronchoscopy team's instructions about fasting, usual medicines, and arrival time. Tell the team about allergies, pregnancy or possible pregnancy, oxygen use, sleep apnea, heart or lung conditions, bleeding problems, and all medicines, including anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs. Do not stop a prescribed medicine unless the prescribing clinician and procedure team provide a plan. Blood-thinning instructions may differ if a biopsy is planned as well as BAL.

If sedation is used, arrange a responsible adult to take you home and follow the team's instructions about driving, work, alcohol, and important decisions afterward.

\section*{Contraindications}
Severe low oxygen levels, unstable heart or lung function, significant bleeding risk, or inability to tolerate bronchoscopy may require added support, a different plan, or postponement.

\section*{Precautions}
There is no single oxygen-number cutoff that applies to everyone. The team weighs the expected diagnostic benefit against the risks. Oxygen and heart rate are monitored, and supplemental oxygen or other support may be used as needed.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary cough, sore throat, hoarseness, a small amount of blood-streaked sputum, a brief fever, and a temporary drop in oxygen can occur. Bronchospasm or an abnormal heart rhythm can occur less often. Serious complications are uncommon, but may include significant breathing or heart problems, infection, or bleeding. More substantial bleeding and a collapsed lung (pneumothorax) are more likely when a lung biopsy is also performed; they are uncommon with BAL alone.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The care team monitors breathing, oxygen level, and other vital signs until the patient is ready to leave. If the throat was numbed, do not eat or drink until the team confirms that swallowing and the gag reflex have returned; the waiting time varies. If sedation was given, follow discharge instructions and do not drive yourself home. Mild throat irritation or cough can continue for a short time.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The laboratory reports only the tests that were ordered. Some results, especially cultures, may take several days or longer. A cell differential may show an inflammatory pattern, such as more lymphocytes, neutrophils, or eosinophils, but these patterns are not specific to one disease. For example, a lymphocyte increase can occur in several interstitial lung diseases, infections, and drug reactions; a neutrophil increase can have infectious and noninfectious causes. Cell patterns must be interpreted with symptoms, imaging, and other test results. BAL cellular analysis alone usually cannot identify the exact type of interstitial lung disease, and a normal result does not rule out microscopic lung disease.

The clinician will discuss results and whether more tests or treatment are needed. A nondiagnostic result does not by itself mean that a lung biopsy is required; the next step depends on the suspected condition, risks, and other findings.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Follow the discharge instructions and contact the bronchoscopy team for a fever that persists or worsens, increasing shortness of breath, worsening chest pain, or more than a small amount of blood in sputum. Seek emergency care for severe breathing difficulty, heavy bleeding, fainting, or severe chest pain.
