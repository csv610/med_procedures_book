\chapter{Intrauterine Device (IUD) Insertion}

\section*{Overview}
An intrauterine device (IUD) is a small device placed inside the uterus to prevent pregnancy. Copper IUDs are nonhormonal; levonorgestrel-releasing IUDs use a progestin hormone. Both are highly effective, long-acting, and reversible. A levonorgestrel IUD may also reduce heavy menstrual bleeding or cramps. IUDs do not protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs).

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item speculum and uterine sound
  \item IUD with inserter and ring forceps
  \item scissors for trimming strings and antiseptic supplies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
IUD insertion; IUD placement; intrauterine contraception; coil insertion

\section*{Principle}
The clinician places the device in the uterine cavity through the cervix. Copper interferes with sperm movement and fertilization. Levonorgestrel thickens cervical mucus and affects the uterine lining, making pregnancy less likely. The device has thin strings that pass through the cervix into the upper vagina; these strings help a clinician check or remove it.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
IUDs are used for long-acting contraception. A copper IUD can also be used as emergency contraception when inserted within the recommended time after unprotected sex. A levonorgestrel IUD may be selected to reduce heavy menstrual bleeding or menstrual cramps. Choice depends on the person's health, bleeding pattern, preferences, and the particular device.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
This is a contraceptive procedure, not a diagnostic test. Insertion may be performed in an office or clinic. The device can usually be removed at any time by a trained clinician if the person wants to stop using it or become pregnant.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician reviews health history and confirms that pregnancy is unlikely. They perform a pelvic examination, place a speculum to see the cervix, clean the cervix, and use a narrow instrument to measure the uterus. The IUD is passed through the cervix in a thin inserter and released in the uterus; the strings are trimmed to an appropriate length. The procedure usually takes a few minutes. Pain, cramping, or a brief faint or lightheaded feeling can occur. The clinician should discuss pain-relief options before insertion and tailor them to the person's preferences and medical circumstances. Depending on the device and timing, backup contraception may be needed for a short period after insertion.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the clinician about possible pregnancy, recent unprotected sex, pelvic pain or infection symptoms, unusual bleeding, allergies, medical conditions, medicines, and prior difficult or painful pelvic procedures. A pregnancy test is not always required: the clinician uses the person's history and timing to determine whether they can be reasonably certain the person is not pregnant. If STI screening is indicated, testing can usually be done on the day of insertion without delaying placement; a known current infection needs treatment before insertion. Ask about pain-management options, what to expect with the selected device, and whether backup contraception is needed. Routine prophylactic antibiotics are not usually needed.

\section*{Contraindications}
Do not insert an IUD during a known pregnancy, with current pelvic inflammatory disease, or with current purulent cervicitis or untreated gonorrhea or chlamydia. Postpartum sepsis is a reason to defer placement. Current breast cancer is a contraindication to a levonorgestrel IUD, but not to a copper IUD; other medical conditions may affect which type is suitable. STI screening alone, without a known active infection, does not usually require delaying insertion. Discuss condom use and STI prevention separately from contraception.

\section*{Precautions}
An IUD may not fit if a uterine abnormality distorts the cavity. Unexplained bleeding should be evaluated when appropriate before insertion.

\section*{Risks}
Pain and cramping during insertion can range from mild to severe; temporary light bleeding, nausea, or a vasovagal fainting reaction can occur. After insertion, spotting or cramping is common. Copper IUDs can make periods heavier or more painful, especially at first. Levonorgestrel IUDs can cause irregular spotting for the first few months and often make periods lighter over time or stop them. Uncommon complications include expulsion, infection, or perforation of the uterus. The risk of pelvic infection after insertion is low and is mainly related to an untreated STI present at the time of insertion. Uterine perforation is rare, but risk is higher with insertion soon after childbirth and during breastfeeding. Pregnancy with an IUD is uncommon; if it occurs, the clinician must promptly assess for ectopic pregnancy.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Mild cramps and spotting may continue for a short time. Most people can return to usual activities when comfortable; bed rest, avoiding exercise, and avoiding tampons are not routinely required. Follow the clinic's instructions about pain relief and backup contraception. Copper IUDs work immediately. A levonorgestrel IUD may require condoms or abstinence for 7 days after insertion, depending on when it was placed and the product; confirm the instructions for the specific device. Use condoms to reduce STI risk.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The clinician may trim and document the strings at insertion. Routine ultrasound or a scheduled string-check visit is not required after uncomplicated placement for every patient. Follow-up can be arranged if there are symptoms, concerns about position, or a specific clinical reason. Some patients choose to feel for the strings occasionally, but this is optional; do not pull on them. If the strings seem much longer or shorter, cannot be felt when previously present, or hard plastic is felt at the cervix, contact a clinician for advice and use backup contraception until placement is clarified.

\section*{Expected Results}
The IUD provides highly effective contraception for the duration approved for that specific device. A levonorgestrel IUD may reduce menstrual bleeding over time; a copper IUD may increase bleeding or cramps. The device does not prevent STIs, and no contraceptive method is completely effective. Fertility generally returns promptly after removal.

\section*{Follow-up}
Continue routine preventive care and follow the device-specific instructions about duration and removal or replacement. Contact a clinician if there is persistent or new pelvic pain, bothersome bleeding, suspected expulsion, or a desire to remove the device. If pregnancy is desired, a trained clinician can remove the IUD; fertility can return promptly.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent care for severe or worsening lower abdominal pain, fever or chills, foul-smelling vaginal discharge, very heavy bleeding, or fainting after insertion. If a pregnancy test is positive while an IUD is in place, contact a clinician promptly. Severe one-sided pelvic pain, shoulder pain, dizziness or fainting, or heavy bleeding with possible pregnancy requires emergency assessment because of the risk of ectopic pregnancy. Contact the clinic if the device is partly expelled or can be felt in the cervix; do not try to push it back in or remove it yourself.
