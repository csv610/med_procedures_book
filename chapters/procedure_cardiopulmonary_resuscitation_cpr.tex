\chapter{Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)}

\section*{Overview}
Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is emergency care for a person in cardiac arrest. A heart attack is caused by blocked blood flow to the heart and is not the same as cardiac arrest, although a heart attack can lead to cardiac arrest. Chest compressions move some blood to the brain and other organs until an automated external defibrillator (AED) or advanced medical care can restore circulation. CPR may include rescue breaths. This chapter summarizes adult basic life support; CPR for infants and children uses age-specific guidance.

\section*{Alternative Names}
CPR; basic life support (BLS); chest-compression-only CPR (when breaths are not given).

\section*{Principle}
Chest compressions provide limited blood flow during cardiac arrest. Rescue breaths add oxygen, and an AED can deliver a shock for certain abnormal rhythms. Early emergency activation, high-quality CPR, and prompt AED use are key links in resuscitation.

\section*{History}
Modern external chest-compression CPR was established in the late 1950s and early 1960s. The technique developed into today's combination of compressions, ventilation, defibrillation, and advanced post-arrest care.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Start CPR when an adult suddenly becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally or is only gasping. Gasping or snoring-like breaths can occur during cardiac arrest and should not be mistaken for normal breathing. A lay rescuer should not delay CPR to check for a pulse. Healthcare professionals check for a pulse for no more than 10 seconds; if no definite pulse is felt, they start CPR.

\section*{Role in Care}
CPR is an immediate emergency treatment, not a diagnostic test. It is performed while emergency responders assess the cause and provide defibrillation or advanced treatment.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
First make sure the scene is safe. Check whether the person responds and whether breathing is normal. Call the local emergency number or ask someone else to call and bring an AED; use a phone speaker so help can guide you. Lay the person on their back on a firm, flat surface when this can be done safely.

For an adult, place the heel of one hand in the center of the chest on the lower half of the breastbone and the other hand on top. Push hard and fast at 100 to 120 compressions per minute, at least 2 inches (5 cm) deep while avoiding depths greater than 2.4 inches (6 cm). Let the chest return fully between pushes and keep pauses as short as possible. If untrained or unwilling to give breaths, continue hands-only chest compressions. If trained and able, give 30 compressions followed by 2 breaths, each over about 1 second and just enough to make the chest rise.

Use the AED as soon as it is available. Turn it on, follow its voice prompts, attach the pads to the bare chest as shown, and make sure no one touches the person during rhythm analysis or a shock. Resume compressions immediately when the AED prompts. Continue until the person shows signs of life and breathes normally, trained help takes over, the scene becomes unsafe, or you cannot continue.

For an infant younger than 1 year (not a newborn at birth) or a child from 1 year until puberty, start CPR if the person is unresponsive, not breathing normally, and shows no signs of life. A lay rescuer should not check for a pulse. Call emergency services and use speakerphone if alone; give breaths as well as compressions if able and willing, because breathing problems are a common cause of cardiac arrest in children. For trained rescuers, compress the center of the chest at 100 to 120 times per minute and at least one-third of the chest depth: about 1.5 inches (4 cm) for an infant and 2 inches (5 cm) for a child. Allow full recoil. Give 30 compressions and 2 breaths when alone, or 15 compressions and 2 breaths when two trained rescuers are present. For an infant, use the heel of one hand or the two-thumb encircling technique; the two-finger technique is no longer recommended. For a child, use one or two hands as needed for the child's size. Attach an AED as soon as available, using pediatric pads or an energy-reducing setting if available; if not, use the AED you have and follow its prompts. For a person with signs of puberty, use adult CPR guidance. Resuscitation of a newborn at birth follows separate neonatal guidance.

Rescue breaths are also especially important in cardiac arrest from drowning or suspected opioid overdose when the rescuer can provide them. If opioid overdose is suspected and naloxone is available, give it without interrupting CPR. Follow dispatcher instructions and training; do not delay compressions or emergency activation while locating naloxone.

\section*{Preparation}
There is no preparation when someone is in cardiac arrest. Scene safety comes first. If available, use a barrier mask or personal protective equipment for breaths, but do not delay chest compressions to find equipment. In a healthcare setting, follow the facility's resuscitation team and equipment protocols.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Do not enter an unsafe scene. A valid do-not-resuscitate (DNR) order or other applicable advance directive may guide clinicians according to local law and policy. Obvious irreversible signs of death may also affect whether resuscitation is started, but a lay rescuer should call emergency services and follow dispatcher instructions when uncertain. Chest compressions can cause rib or other injuries; this risk is outweighed by the need to treat suspected cardiac arrest.

\section*{Risks}
Possible injuries include bruising, rib or breastbone fractures, and, less commonly, injury to internal organs. Rescue breaths given too forcefully or too often can inflate the stomach and increase the risk of vomiting or aspiration. These risks do not justify delaying CPR when cardiac arrest is suspected.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
If circulation returns, emergency clinicians provide post-cardiac-arrest care, investigate and treat the cause, and support breathing and blood pressure. For adults who remain unable to follow verbal commands after return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), AHA guidance recommends a deliberate temperature-control plan; maintaining temperature control for at least 36 hours is considered reasonable. For adults whose arrest is suspected to have a cardiac cause, coronary angiography is recommended before hospital discharge, with timing guided by the clinical situation. It is not automatically required for every survivor. Survivors may need intensive care and rehabilitation.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
ROSC means that the heart has resumed effective circulation. Healthcare professionals assess signs such as a definite pulse, blood pressure, and other evidence of perfusion; an organized electrical rhythm on a monitor alone does not prove ROSC. Survival and recovery vary with the cause, time to CPR and defibrillation, health conditions, and response to treatment. Follow-up may include heart and brain assessment, rehabilitation, and assessment and support for emotional distress in the patient and caregivers.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
For an unresponsive adult who is not breathing normally or is only gasping, call emergency services, start CPR, and use an AED as soon as possible. Continue until help takes over or the person begins breathing normally. If the person wakes or resumes normal breathing, stop compressions, keep them under observation, and follow emergency dispatcher instructions. Anyone who has had a cardiac arrest needs emergency medical evaluation, even if they appear to recover.
