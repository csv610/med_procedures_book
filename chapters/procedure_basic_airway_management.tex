\chapter{Basic Airway Management}

\section*{Overview}
Basic airway management uses positioning and simple equipment to keep air moving into and out of the lungs when a person cannot protect or maintain an open airway. Trained rescuers may use a head-tilt--chin-lift or jaw-thrust maneuver, an oral or nasal airway adjunct, suction, or a bag-mask device to give breaths. Airway adjuncts and bag-mask ventilation require training; bystanders should follow dispatcher instructions and use the first-aid or CPR skills they have learned. The approach depends on the person's age, condition, and the cause of the problem.

This chapter covers basic airway support for infants, children, and adults. Resuscitation immediately after birth follows separate newborn guidelines.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Related terms include airway-opening maneuvers, basic life support airway support, bag-mask ventilation, and oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airway adjuncts.
\section*{Principle}
When a person becomes unresponsive, the tongue and other soft tissues can block airflow through the throat. Repositioning the head and jaw may open the airway. An oral airway adjunct is a device placed in the mouth to help keep the airway open; a nasal airway adjunct is a soft tube placed through the nose. An oral device is used only when a person is deeply unresponsive and has no gag reflex (the automatic response that causes retching). A nasal device may be better tolerated when the gag reflex remains, if nasal injury is not a concern. A bag-mask device can then deliver breaths; each breath should make the chest rise gently. These measures support breathing while the cause is treated or a more advanced airway is considered.
\section*{Why It Is Done}
It is used when a person is unresponsive and the airway is blocked or at risk of becoming blocked, or when breathing is absent or too weak to provide enough oxygen. It is also used during cardiopulmonary resuscitation and while trained clinicians prepare or decide whether an advanced airway is needed. If an opioid overdose is suspected, naloxone may also be given if available, but it must not delay breaths, CPR, or the emergency call. Choking on food or another object requires first aid for foreign-body airway obstruction; a bystander should not insert an airway adjunct into a conscious person.
\section*{Role in Care}
This is an immediate supportive intervention, not a diagnostic test. It can be lifesaving while the team addresses the cause of poor breathing or prepares additional treatment. It does not replace CPR when a person is in cardiac arrest, nor does it remove the need to treat the underlying cause.
\section*{How It Is Performed}
The responder checks responsiveness and breathing and calls for emergency help. Gasping is not normal breathing. If a person is unresponsive but breathing normally, maintain the airway and keep monitoring; for decreased alertness without suspected injury, a trained first aider may place the person on their side in the recovery position. If an unresponsive person is not breathing normally, a bystander should call emergency services, start CPR, and use an automated external defibrillator if available, following dispatcher instructions and their training. Health professionals assess for a pulse; if a person has a pulse but is not breathing or is not breathing effectively, they provide assisted breaths. In an infant or child, if the heart rate remains below 60 beats per minute with signs of poor circulation despite effective oxygenation and ventilation, clinicians start CPR according to the pediatric resuscitation algorithm.

For a conscious person who is choking, encourage coughing if they can cough or make sounds. For severe choking (the person cannot speak, breathe, or cough effectively), promptly call emergency services and give repeated cycles of 5 back blows followed by 5 abdominal thrusts to adults and children. For infants, use 5 back blows followed by 5 chest thrusts; do not give abdominal thrusts. Use chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts for a person in late pregnancy or when the rescuer cannot reach around the abdomen. Keep the emergency call active even if the object comes out; a person who needed back blows or thrusts should be assessed by a medical professional. If the person becomes unresponsive, start CPR with chest compressions. When opening the mouth to give breaths, remove an object only if it is visible; never perform a blind finger sweep.

A head-tilt--chin-lift is generally used when neck injury is not suspected. For an infant, keep the head near neutral with only a slight tilt; older children and adults may need more head tilt. If head or neck injury is possible, trained rescuers try a jaw thrust first; if the jaw thrust and an airway adjunct do not open the airway, a head-tilt--chin-lift may still be needed to allow breathing. Suction is used for fluids. A trained rescuer may use a correctly sized oral or nasal adjunct and a bag-mask device. Bag-mask ventilation is a challenging skill. When two trained rescuers are available, one opens the airway and seals the mask to the face with both hands while the other squeezes the bag. Give breaths at the recommended rate and with just enough force to produce visible chest rise. For a person with a pulse but absent or inadequate breathing, 2025 AHA guidance recommends one breath every 6 seconds for adults and every 2 to 3 seconds for infants and children; CPR ventilation follows a different age- and situation-specific algorithm.
\section*{Preparation}
In an emergency, the team gathers suction, oxygen, a bag-mask device with the correct mask size, and oral and nasal airway adjuncts in several sizes. A pulse oximeter estimates blood oxygen saturation (the percentage of hemoglobin carrying oxygen) through a sensor, usually on a finger. When available, waveform capnography measures exhaled carbon dioxide and helps monitor breathing. If trauma is suspected, trained rescuers limit neck movement while ensuring that the airway is open and breathing is effective.
\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
An oral airway adjunct is generally used only when the person is unresponsive and has no gag or cough reflex; otherwise it may trigger gagging or vomiting. For an unresponsive adult in cardiac arrest with a suspected skull-base fracture or severe blood-clotting problem, 2025 AHA guidance prefers an oral over a nasal airway adjunct. Facial or nasal injury may also make a nasal adjunct unsuitable. Use the size and device recommended by training and local protocol, and check repeatedly that it is helping. If there is a foreign-body obstruction, do not perform a blind finger sweep.
\section*{Risks}
Risks include inadequate ventilation and low oxygen if the airway remains blocked or the mask does not seal. Giving breaths too fast or too forcefully can inflate the stomach, cause vomiting and aspiration, or impair blood flow during CPR. An airway adjunct can injure the mouth, throat, or nose; a nasal adjunct may cause nosebleeding. Airway management can also delay other lifesaving care if it is not coordinated with CPR and emergency response.
\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The care team repeatedly reassesses airway openness, breathing, chest movement, oxygen level, and response to treatment. Capnography may be used to monitor exhaled carbon dioxide when available. Support continues until the person is breathing adequately on their own or another airway plan is in place. A patient who remains unresponsive or needs assisted breathing requires continued emergency care and monitoring.
\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Signs that ventilation is helping include visible chest rise and improving oxygenation. If available, waveform capnography can help assess ventilation and confirm exhaled carbon dioxide. A normal pulse-oximeter reading alone does not prove that ventilation is adequate. If breaths do not produce chest rise, trained rescuers reposition the airway, check mask fit and equipment, suction or adjust the adjunct as appropriate, and escalate care.
\section*{Expected Results}
An open airway with visible chest rise during assisted breaths and improving oxygenation. The target and breathing rate depend on the person's age and situation; one rate is not appropriate for every patient.
\section*{Follow-up}
Patients who do not improve need reassessment and may require a different airway device or advanced airway management by trained clinicians. Once breathing is stable, the team evaluates and treats the underlying cause and watches for complications such as aspiration or injury. Follow-up depends on what caused the problem and how the person recovers.
\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Airway support is used in an emergency. Call emergency services immediately if someone is unresponsive, struggling to breathe, or not breathing normally (including only gasping). If an unresponsive person is breathing normally and has no suspected injury, a trained first aider may place them on their side and monitor their breathing. If they are not breathing normally, begin CPR and use an automated external defibrillator if available, following dispatcher instructions and the training you have. After airway support, urgent reassessment is needed for ongoing breathing difficulty, repeated vomiting, new chest pain, or a suspected aspiration or airway injury.
