\chapter{Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy}

\section*{Overview}
Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) is a type of implanted pacing treatment for selected people with heart failure. It sends timed electrical signals to help the lower heart chambers (ventricles) contract in a more coordinated way. When the ventricles contract out of step, the heart may pump less effectively. CRT can improve symptoms and reduce hospitalizations and death in people who meet specific criteria, but it does not cure heart failure and does not help everyone.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item fluoroscopy and ECG monitoring equipment
  \item device implantation instruments and venous introducer sheaths
  \item pacing leads and CRT pulse generator
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Biventricular pacing; biventricular pacemaker; cardiac resynchronization device. A device with pacing alone is called CRT-P. A device that also can deliver a shock to treat certain dangerous fast heart rhythms is called CRT-D.

\section*{Principle}
The electrical signal that activates the ventricles can be delayed or travel unevenly. This may cause the right and left ventricles, or different parts of the left ventricle, to contract at different times. CRT coordinates electrical activation by pacing both ventricles. The width of the electrical signal on an ECG is called the QRS duration; a wider QRS, particularly with left bundle branch block (LBBB), can indicate delayed activation. The left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is the percentage of blood pushed out of the left ventricle with each beat; a reduced LVEF means weaker pumping.

Most CRT systems have a pulse generator under the skin and thin insulated wires called leads. A lead usually enters the right ventricle, and another is guided through a vein on the heart's surface, reached through the coronary sinus, to pace the left ventricle. A right atrial lead may also be used. The device senses the heart's activity and times the pacing signals. In selected cases, a specialist may discuss pacing that directly stimulates the heart's conduction system, particularly when conventional CRT is not feasible or effective. The evidence and recommendations for these approaches differ.

\section*{History}
Clinical use of CRT developed from observations that delayed electrical activation can make heart contraction less coordinated. Trials in the late 1990s and early 2000s established that biventricular pacing could improve symptoms and heart function in selected people with advanced heart failure. Subsequent studies and guidelines refined which patients are most likely to benefit and how to combine CRT with defibrillator therapy.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
The strongest indication is for a person in sinus rhythm with ongoing heart-failure symptoms, an LVEF of 35\% or less, and a wide QRS with LBBB—especially a QRS duration of at least 150 milliseconds—despite appropriate heart-failure treatment. The 2026 European Society of Cardiology guideline also says CRT should be considered for selected people in sinus rhythm with a non-LBBB pattern and a QRS of at least 150 milliseconds, or LBBB with a QRS of 130--149 milliseconds; the expected benefit is less certain in some of these groups. Eligibility depends on the full ECG, heart imaging, symptoms, rhythm, medicines, other illnesses, and personal goals.

CRT may also be considered when a person with reduced heart function needs frequent ventricular pacing for high-grade atrioventricular block, or when heart failure and reduced LVEF worsen in someone with an existing pacemaker or defibrillator that frequently paces only the right ventricle. For atrial fibrillation, a plan is needed to ensure that the ventricles receive pacing often enough for CRT to work; some people need an additional procedure to control conduction to the ventricles.

CRT is generally not used for heart failure alone when the QRS is narrow and there is no separate need for pacing. The care team weighs potential benefit against procedural risk, overall health, likely prognosis, and the patient's preferences.

\section*{Role in Care}
CRT is one part of heart-failure care. It is usually considered alongside guideline-directed medicines and treatment of the cause of heart failure. CRT-P provides pacing. CRT-D also includes an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD), which can detect and treat certain life-threatening fast rhythms. The decision to add defibrillator function is separate and individualized; not everyone who is eligible for CRT needs a CRT-D. CRT does not replace medicines, follow-up, or other indicated treatments.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The implant is usually performed in a cardiac electrophysiology laboratory. The patient is connected to heart rhythm, blood pressure, and oxygen monitors. Local anesthetic is used to numb the area below the collarbone; sedation is commonly given, and general anesthesia is used in some cases.

The clinician makes a small incision, usually below the left collarbone, and places the leads into a vein. Using X-ray guidance, the leads are advanced to the right side of the heart and into a vein that runs over the outside of the left ventricle. A lead may also be placed in the right atrium. The team checks lead positions and electrical measurements, connects the leads to the pulse generator, and places the generator under the skin. The incision is closed and covered. If a lead cannot be placed through a vein or that route is unsuitable, a surgical lead on the heart's outer surface may sometimes be considered. The procedure often takes a few hours; duration and hospital stay vary.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews the heart-failure history, symptoms, ECG, echocardiogram, medicines, and prior devices. Blood tests or other imaging may be needed. Tell the team about blood thinners and antiplatelet medicines, allergies, kidney disease, bleeding problems, prior infections, and any previous reaction to contrast dye. Do not stop or change medicines unless the treating team gives specific instructions. Follow instructions about fasting, bathing, and arranging a ride home. Discuss whether CRT-P or CRT-D is being considered, expected benefits, alternatives, and what the device can and cannot do.

\section*{Contraindications}
No specific absolute contraindication is identified here; the treating team assesses suitability for the individual.

\section*{Precautions}
There is no single rule that excludes every patient. An active infection, significant bleeding risk, difficult vein access, or a serious illness affecting expected benefit may change the timing, approach, or decision to implant. An unstable heart-failure episode may need treatment before an elective implant. The team considers these factors in context; urgent pacing needs can change the balance of risks and benefits.

\section*{Risks}
Possible complications include bruising, bleeding or a blood collection under the skin, infection, blood clots, or movement or failure of a lead. Placing a lead through a vein can rarely injure the lung, heart, or blood vessel; injury to the heart can cause fluid to collect around it and may require urgent treatment. Abnormal heart rhythms, reaction to contrast dye if it is used, and complications from sedation or anesthesia are also possible. X-ray imaging involves radiation exposure. Sometimes the left-ventricle lead cannot be placed in a useful position, requiring another approach or procedure. A person may have little improvement despite a technically successful implant.

CRT-D has the additional possibility of shocks, including an inappropriate shock, and carries the usual risks of an ICD. Device infection can require removal of the generator and leads and later reimplantation. The individual risk depends on health, anatomy, whether this is a first implant or an upgrade, and the type of device.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After the implant, staff monitor the heart rhythm, wound, and device. An ECG, chest X-ray, or device check may be done before discharge. Some people go home the same day; others stay overnight. Follow the team's instructions for wound care, bathing, pain relief, driving, and activity. Keep the incision clean and dry as directed. Avoid heavy lifting and strenuous or wide shoulder movements on the implant side for the period advised, while following the team's guidance on gentle movement.

Carry the device identification card and tell healthcare professionals that a CRT device is implanted. MRI safety depends on the exact generator and lead system and on the required scanning conditions; have the device checked by the appropriate team before an MRI. Follow the device clinic's advice about household or workplace equipment and strong magnetic fields. Keep scheduled in-person or remote device checks.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The device clinic checks lead function, battery, settings, and how often the heart is being paced. The heart-failure team monitors symptoms and may repeat an echocardiogram to assess changes in heart function. Some people notice improvement over weeks or months; others improve less or not at all. Continued heart-failure treatment remains important. The generator battery lasts for years, but its life varies with settings and pacing needs; replacement requires a planned procedure. Leads may be retained, revised, or replaced depending on their condition and the clinical plan.

In atrial fibrillation, the team may check that a high proportion of heartbeats are effectively paced, because frequent intrinsic beats can reduce CRT benefit. If CRT-D is implanted, the device team explains how to respond to a shock and when to seek emergency help. Shared decisions over time may include device settings, replacement, and—when appropriate—whether defibrillator therapies still fit the person's goals of care.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek emergency care for severe chest pain, fainting, severe shortness of breath, or repeated CRT-D shocks. Contact the device or heart-failure team promptly for any shock, a new fever, increasing redness, warmth, swelling, drainage, or worsening pain at the incision, new swelling of the arm on the implant side, persistent hiccups or muscle twitching, or a sudden return or worsening of heart-failure symptoms. Follow any specific emergency instructions provided by the implanting team.
