\chapter{Brachytherapy}

\section*{Overview}
Brachytherapy is a type of internal radiation treatment. In most forms, a sealed radiation source is placed in or next to a tumor so that a high dose reaches the target while nearby healthy tissue receives less radiation. It is a local treatment; it does not treat the whole body. Radioembolization uses radioactive beads delivered through an artery to the liver and has different procedure and safety instructions; it is not covered in the procedure details below.

The source may be placed in a body cavity using an applicator, inserted into tissue through needles or catheters, or implanted as small seeds. It may stay in place for minutes or days and then be removed, or some seeds may remain in the body permanently. The plan depends on the cancer, its location, and the person's health.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Internal radiation therapy; implant radiation therapy; interstitial (placed in tissue) or intracavitary (placed in a body cavity) radiation therapy.

\section*{Principle}
Radiation damages the DNA of cancer cells so they stop growing or die. Because the source is placed close to the tumor, treatment can deliver radiation to a small area. The treatment plan is calculated by a radiation oncologist and medical physicist to provide the intended dose and limit exposure to nearby organs.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Brachytherapy may be used for selected cancers, including prostate, cervical, uterine, vaginal, breast, head and neck, and eye cancers. It may be used alone or together with external-beam radiation, surgery, or other treatment. Whether it is suitable depends on the cancer type, size and location, stage, previous treatment, and the person's overall health.

\section*{Role in Treatment}
Brachytherapy is a cancer treatment, not a diagnostic test. It may be the main radiation treatment or a planned part of a broader treatment course. The oncology team explains the goal, alternatives, and expected benefits for the individual.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The treatment team uses imaging and a treatment plan to guide placement. The exact steps vary by body site and technique:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Temporary high-dose-rate (HDR) treatment:} An applicator or catheter is placed in or near the target. A small radioactive source travels through it for a planned time and is then removed. Treatment may involve one or more sessions; the applicator may stay in place between sessions or be removed after each one.
  \item \textbf{Temporary low-dose-rate (LDR) or pulsed-dose-rate (PDR) treatment:} With LDR, the source remains in place continuously for a longer period, sometimes requiring a hospital stay, and is removed when the planned treatment is complete. PDR delivers short radiation pulses at intervals while the applicator is in place.
  \item \textbf{Permanent seed implant:} Small radioactive seeds are placed in the target tissue and remain there. Their radiation decreases over time.
\end{itemize}

Applicators or seeds may be placed through a natural opening or through the skin; for some eye cancers, a radioactive plaque is attached to the eye's surface. Some procedures require anesthesia or sedation; others do not. Imaging such as ultrasound, X-ray, CT, or MRI may be used to guide placement and check the plan. The patient may need to lie still while an applicator is in place.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews the treatment plan, medical history, medicines, allergies, pregnancy possibility, and any prior radiation or procedures in the area. Preparation may include blood tests, imaging, bowel preparation or fasting, and anesthesia assessment, depending on the treatment site. The team gives specific instructions about medicines; do not stop blood thinners or other medicines unless the treating team directs it.

Ask the team what to expect during placement, whether an applicator will remain between treatments, how many visits are planned, and whether a support person should accompany you. If pregnancy is possible, tell the team before treatment. If treatment will be near reproductive organs and you may want children in the future, ask about possible effects on fertility and preservation options before treatment begins.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Suitability is decided individually. The team considers the tumor's location and access, nearby organs, prior treatment, infection or bleeding concerns, pregnancy, ability to tolerate the required position or anesthesia, and other health conditions. Some situations may require a different technique or treatment plan rather than ruling out radiation altogether.

Radiation-safety instructions depend on whether the source is temporary or permanent. While a temporary source is in place, the hospital may limit visitors or require other precautions. Staff remove the source before the patient leaves the treatment area; after removal, the patient is generally not radioactive. Permanent implants can require short-term precautions around young children or pregnant people. Follow the radiation oncology team's instructions exactly, since the duration and restrictions vary by implant and treatment plan.

\section*{Risks}
Side effects depend on the treated area, dose, and technique. Placement can cause temporary discomfort, bleeding, or infection. Anesthesia or sedation has its own risks. Radiation can irritate or injure nearby tissue and may cause symptoms such as urinary, bowel, sexual, or skin problems, depending on the treatment site. Treatment near reproductive organs can also affect fertility. Fatigue may occur, especially when brachytherapy is combined with other cancer treatments. Some effects can appear months or years later. The team should explain the side effects most relevant to the planned treatment.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After temporary treatment, the applicator and source are removed as planned. The team monitors recovery from the procedure or anesthesia and gives instructions for wound care, activity, and symptom relief. Many people go home the same day, while some need hospital care during treatment or recovery. Recovery varies with the site and whether other treatments are being given.

For permanent seeds, the seeds stay in place. The team will explain any temporary radiation precautions and what to do if a seed is seen in urine or another body fluid, if this is relevant to the implant used. Do not handle or discard a seed without following the team's instructions.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Follow-up is scheduled by the radiation oncology team. It may include symptom review, examination, imaging, or laboratory tests to assess treatment response and monitor side effects. Radiation does not destroy cancer cells immediately; the response may take weeks or months to become clear. The result depends on the cancer and the full treatment plan, and brachytherapy does not guarantee cure.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Follow the treatment center's contact instructions. Contact the team promptly for fever, increasing pain, heavy or persistent bleeding, worsening urinary or bowel symptoms, inability to pass urine, or signs of infection. Seek emergency care for severe breathing difficulty, fainting, or heavy uncontrolled bleeding. Follow any treatment-site-specific instructions provided by the oncology team.
