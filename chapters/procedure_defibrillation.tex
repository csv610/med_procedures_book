\chapter{Defibrillation}

\section*{Overview}
Defibrillation is an electrical shock used during cardiac arrest when the heart has a shockable rhythm: ventricular fibrillation (VF) or pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT). The shock can stop the disorganized electrical activity long enough for an effective rhythm to return. It does not guarantee that the heart will restart; high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), emergency care, and treatment of the cause are also essential. An automated external defibrillator (AED) can analyze the rhythm and advise whether a shock is needed.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item manual defibrillator or AED with adhesive pads
  \item ECG monitoring leads
  \item bag-mask and oxygen equipment when needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Electrical defibrillation; shock for cardiac arrest. Defibrillation is different from synchronized cardioversion, which is used for certain dangerous fast rhythms when a pulse is present.

\section*{Principle}
Defibrillation delivers an unsynchronized electrical shock through pads or paddles on the chest. It is used for VF and pVT, in which the ventricles do not produce an effective circulation. Asystole and pulseless electrical activity (PEA) are not treated with a shock; CPR and care directed at reversible causes are used instead.

\section*{History}
In 1899, Jean-Louis Prévost and Frédéric Batelli reported that electrical shocks could stop ventricular fibrillation in animals. Claude Beck and colleagues reported successful defibrillation in a human during surgery in 1947.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Defibrillation is given as soon as possible when a person in cardiac arrest has VF or pVT. Early CPR and rapid access to an AED or defibrillator improve the chance of survival. For a public rescuer, follow the AED's spoken and visual instructions and call emergency services.

\section*{Role in Care}
It is an emergency treatment for shockable cardiac arrest, delivered as part of CPR and advanced life support. It is not a diagnostic test or a stand-alone cure.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
Rescuers begin CPR and attach an AED as soon as it is available. The AED analyzes the heart rhythm and tells the rescuer whether to deliver a shock. In hospital or ambulance care, trained clinicians may use a manual defibrillator and select the energy and pad position according to the device, rhythm, and current resuscitation guidance. Pads are placed on the exposed chest, commonly in anterolateral or anteroposterior positions. Everyone must be clear of the patient during rhythm analysis and shock delivery. CPR is resumed immediately after a shock; the device prompts the next rhythm check.

\section*{Preparation}
In an emergency, do not delay CPR or AED use to prepare the skin beyond what is needed for safe pad contact. Follow the AED prompts. If the chest is wet, move the person from standing water when safe and quickly dry the pad sites. Do not place a pad over a medication patch; remove the patch with a gloved hand and wipe the skin before placing the pad if it is in the way. Do not place pads directly over an implanted pacemaker or defibrillator; keep them clear of the implant when possible. Do not touch the person during analysis or shock. Trained teams follow their device and local protocols.

\section*{Contraindications}
Do not deliver a shock for asystole or PEA; continue CPR and treat possible causes. Do not touch the person or allow anyone else to touch them during analysis or shock. Keep pads from touching each other. For an infant or child, use pediatric pads or an energy-reducing setting if available; if not, use the AED that is available rather than delaying defibrillation.

\section*{Precautions}
Follow the AED prompts and pediatric resuscitation guidance.

\section*{Risks}
Possible effects include skin redness or burns beneath the pads, brief muscle soreness, and rare injury to rescuers who touch the person during shock delivery. In cardiac arrest, the immediate danger from the arrest is far greater than these risks.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After a shock, immediately continue CPR for about 2 minutes or until the AED or defibrillator prompts a rhythm check. Do not pause to check a pulse immediately after the shock unless the person shows clear signs of life or the device and clinical protocol direct otherwise. If signs of life return, emergency clinicians assess circulation, breathing, and the cause of the arrest and provide post-arrest care.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The immediate aim is to stop VF or pVT and restore effective circulation. An organized electrical rhythm alone does not prove that circulation has returned; clinicians look for signs such as a pulse and measurable blood pressure. The result and outlook depend on the cause of arrest, how quickly CPR and defibrillation began, and the person's overall condition.

\section*{Expected Results}
The shock may terminate VF or pVT, but more CPR, another shock, medication, or other advanced treatment may be needed. A shock does not ensure a normal rhythm or survival.

\section*{Follow-up}
People who survive cardiac arrest need hospital evaluation and treatment of the cause. Antiarrhythmic medicines and an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator may be considered in selected cases; neither is required for everyone. The care team determines follow-up from the rhythm, underlying disease, recovery, and risk of another event.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Cardiac arrest is an emergency: call the local emergency number, start CPR, and use an AED as soon as one is available. After resuscitation, urgent hospital care is required even if the person appears to have recovered.
