\chapter{Dialysis}

\section*{Overview}
Dialysis is a treatment that removes some waste products and extra fluid from the blood when the kidneys cannot do enough of this work. It can be used for severe acute kidney injury or kidney failure that lasts over time. Dialysis can help control problems caused by kidney failure, but it does not cure the kidneys. Some people need dialysis temporarily; others need ongoing dialysis unless they receive a kidney transplant. The timing and type of treatment are chosen with the kidney care team.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Renal replacement therapy; hemodialysis (HD); peritoneal dialysis (PD). Continuous kidney replacement therapy is a slower form of dialysis used in some critically ill hospital patients.

\section*{Principle}
Dialysis uses a filter to remove waste and extra fluid and to help balance salts in the body. In hemodialysis, blood passes through an external filter, called a dialyzer. In peritoneal dialysis, dialysis fluid flows into the abdomen through a catheter; the lining of the abdomen acts as the filter. Dialysis does not replace every kidney function, so medicines, nutrition care, and monitoring may still be needed.

\section*{History}
Willem Kolff developed an early working hemodialysis machine in the Netherlands during World War II. In 1945, one of his patients survived after treatment with the machine.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Dialysis may be needed when kidney failure causes dangerous fluid buildup, high potassium or acid levels, or symptoms and complications that cannot be controlled adequately with other treatment. A single lab result does not by itself determine when dialysis should start. For long-term kidney failure, options may include hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, kidney transplant, or comprehensive supportive care without dialysis; the choice depends on the person's health and goals.

\section*{Role in Care}
Dialysis is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. The care team uses symptoms, examination, blood tests, fluid status, remaining kidney function, and the person's goals to decide whether to start dialysis and to adjust the prescription.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
\textbf{Hemodialysis:} Blood flows from the body through tubing to a dialyzer and then returns. Most people receive it at a dialysis center or at home. Treatments may be given on different schedules. Blood is accessed through an arteriovenous fistula, an arteriovenous graft, or a dialysis catheter. A fistula or graft is often planned ahead of time, but a temporary catheter may be needed if dialysis must begin urgently.

\textbf{Peritoneal dialysis:} A catheter is placed in the abdomen, usually before treatment begins. Dialysis fluid flows into the abdomen, remains there for a prescribed time, and is then drained along with waste and extra fluid. Exchanges can be done by hand during the day or by a machine overnight. Patients and caregivers receive training in clean technique and catheter care.

\textbf{Continuous kidney replacement therapy:} In some critically ill patients, a machine filters blood slowly and continuously in the hospital, often through a temporary catheter.

\section*{Preparation}
When kidney failure is progressing, early discussion with a nephrologist gives time to compare treatment options and plan access. The team may ask you to protect arm veins for future hemodialysis access; follow its advice about blood draws, IV lines, and blood pressure checks on that arm. A fistula or graft for hemodialysis, or a catheter for peritoneal dialysis, may need to be placed before use. Urgent dialysis can start through a temporary venous catheter if needed. Tell the kidney team about health conditions, medicines, allergies, and treatment goals. Home dialysis requires training, supplies, and a suitable clean space. Food, fluid, and medicine advice is individualized.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
There is no single treatment that suits everyone. Some abdominal conditions or prior surgeries may make peritoneal dialysis difficult or unsuitable; vascular or heart problems may affect the hemodialysis plan. The team considers these issues and may modify the approach. Dialysis is started when its expected benefits fit the person's medical needs and preferences; supportive care without dialysis is also an option for some people with kidney failure.

\section*{Risks}
Hemodialysis can cause low blood pressure, cramps, nausea, tiredness, or problems with the access, including infection, clotting, or bleeding. Peritoneal dialysis can cause infection at the catheter site or peritonitis (infection inside the abdomen), as well as hernia or weight gain. Any dialysis approach can require changes to medicines, food, and fluid intake. The care team monitors for side effects and adjusts the treatment when needed.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After hemodialysis, the team may check blood pressure, weight, symptoms, and the access site. Follow instructions for caring for the access; if you have a fistula or graft, ask the team how to check its vibration (thrill) and protect that arm. Do not let anyone take blood pressure, draw blood, or place an IV in that arm unless your dialysis team specifically advises it. For peritoneal dialysis, follow the training for handwashing, clean exchanges, and catheter exit-site care. Keep scheduled treatments and contact the dialysis team if you cannot complete one. Fluid limits, diet, and medicines are specific to each person; do not change them without advice.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The care team reviews symptoms, fluid status, blood tests, nutrition, and treatment delivery to see whether dialysis is meeting the person's needs. Kt/V or another measure may be used to assess dialysis dose, but the target depends on the treatment schedule, remaining kidney function, and clinical situation. No single lab value describes the whole benefit of dialysis.

\section*{Expected Results}
Dialysis can help control fluid buildup and abnormal levels of waste products and salts. It may improve symptoms such as swelling, nausea, or shortness of breath, but it cannot fully replace healthy kidneys and does not guarantee that all symptoms will resolve.

\section*{Follow-up}
People receiving dialysis need regular kidney-team visits, laboratory monitoring, and access or catheter checks. A dietitian can help tailor nutrition and fluid guidance. Transplant assessment may be appropriate for some people. The dialysis prescription may change as kidney function, health, or personal needs change.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Call the dialysis team promptly for fever, chills, redness, swelling, pain, or drainage at an access or catheter site; persistent bleeding after treatment; or new hand pain, coldness, numbness, or weakness on the access side. Call the dialysis team immediately if the vibration in a fistula or graft changes or cannot be felt. For peritoneal dialysis, contact the PD team urgently if drained fluid becomes cloudy or you develop abdominal pain, fever, nausea, or vomiting. Seek emergency care for severe breathing difficulty, chest pain, fainting, confusion, or uncontrolled bleeding.
