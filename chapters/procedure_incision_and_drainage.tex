\chapter{Incision and Drainage}

\section*{Overview}
Incision and drainage (I\&D) is a procedure to release pus from a collection under the skin, such as an abscess or boil. A clinician makes a small opening so the pus can drain. This often reduces pressure and pain, but the right treatment depends on the size and location of the collection and the person's health. Some lumps that look infected, including inflamed skin cysts, are not caused by bacteria.

\section*{Alternative Names}
I\&D, abscess drainage, surgical drainage

\section*{Principle}
For a drainable skin abscess, a clinician opens the collection and releases the pus. The cavity may be gently explored or rinsed. The opening is usually left to drain and heal; routine packing is not needed for many small, uncomplicated abscesses and can increase pain. A dressing or temporary wick may be used in some cases.
\section*{Why It Is Done}
I\&D may be recommended when examination shows a collection of pus under the skin, especially if it is painful, enlarging, or not improving. Antibiotics alone may not adequately treat a collection that needs drainage. Smaller or early lesions may be managed differently. Abscesses in deeper tissues or near the eye, nose, hand, genitals, or anus may need imaging, specialist care, or a different procedure.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
For many simple skin abscesses, drainage is the main treatment. Antibiotics may also be prescribed depending on the person's health, signs of infection beyond the abscess, location, and response to treatment. For some otherwise uncomplicated abscesses, antibiotics after drainage may modestly improve the chance of cure and reduce recurrence, but can cause side effects; the clinician and patient can weigh these benefits and harms.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician cleans the area and numbs it with local anesthetic; a larger or sensitive abscess may require another pain-control approach or a procedure in an operating room. The clinician opens the collection and lets the pus drain. A sample may be sent to a laboratory, and a dressing or temporary wick may be placed if needed. The approach depends on the abscess and its location.

\section*{Preparation}
Preparation depends on the site and whether sedation or anesthesia is planned. Tell the clinician about allergies, medicines (especially blood thinners), diabetes, immune-system problems, pregnancy, and prior infections or resistant bacteria. Do not stop medicines unless the care team gives you a plan. Follow instructions about eating, drinking, and arranging a ride if sedation is used. The clinician may examine the area or use imaging if it is not clear whether a drainable collection is present.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Abscesses near the eye or nose, on the hand, near the genitals or anus, or close to major blood vessels or nerves need careful assessment and may require a specialist. A large, deep, recurrent, or spreading infection; fever or other signs of illness; diabetes; or a weakened immune system may change the setting and treatment plan. The clinician considers whether antibiotics, a culture, imaging, or additional treatment is needed.

\section*{Risks}
Possible problems include pain, bleeding, scarring, incomplete drainage, recurrence, and infection spreading into nearby skin or, rarely, the bloodstream. Local anesthetic may not fully numb inflamed tissue, so the procedure can still be uncomfortable. Risks vary with the abscess location, size, and the person's health.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Keep the wound clean and covered as instructed, and wash your hands before and after changing a dressing. A small amount of drainage may continue while the cavity heals. If the clinician placed a wick or packing, follow their instructions about when and where it should be removed or changed; it is not routinely needed for every small abscess. The clinician may recommend warm compresses, wound checks, or antibiotics. Do not squeeze or probe the wound yourself.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Drainage often reduces pressure and pain, but tenderness and drainage can continue for a time. Healing may take days to weeks, depending on the size and site. Some abscesses need another assessment or additional drainage, and antibiotics may be needed in selected cases.

\section*{Expected Results}
The collection is opened so pus can drain and the cavity can heal. There is no guarantee of immediate pain relief or that one procedure will fully resolve the infection.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow-up depends on the abscess, its location, and whether a wick, packing, culture, or antibiotics were used. Attend any wound check and review culture results with the clinician if a sample was sent. Complete prescribed antibiotics as directed and contact the clinician if the wound is not improving.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact a clinician promptly if redness or swelling spreads, pain or drainage worsens, the area is not improving, or you develop fever, chills, or feel increasingly unwell. Seek emergency care for confusion, fainting, trouble breathing, rapidly worsening severe pain, or other signs of a serious infection. If the abscess is near the eye, nose, hand, genitals, or anus, follow the clinician's specific instructions for seeking care.
