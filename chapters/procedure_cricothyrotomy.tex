\chapter{Cricothyrotomy}

\section*{Overview}
Cricothyrotomy is an emergency procedure that creates an airway through the front of the neck by opening the cricothyroid membrane between the thyroid and cricoid cartilages. It is used as a last-resort airway when a trained team cannot place a breathing tube or otherwise provide enough oxygen. The adult procedure usually places a cuffed tube through the membrane. This is different from a tracheostomy, which is a separate procedure through the trachea.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Cricothyroidotomy; emergency front-of-neck airway; surgical airway. Needle or cannula cricothyrotomy is a different technique.

\section*{Principle}
The cricothyroid membrane gives emergency access to the airway below the vocal cords. A tube through this opening can connect to a standard breathing circuit. A narrow needle or cannula may provide a temporary route for oxygen in selected situations, but it is not equivalent to a cuffed tube and can make carbon-dioxide removal difficult. High-pressure oxygen through a narrow cannula can cause serious injury if exhalation is obstructed.

\section*{History}
Emergency access through the cricothyroid membrane has a long history in airway care. Modern difficult-airway guidelines include it as emergency front-of-neck access for a cannot-intubate, cannot-oxygenate crisis.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
The procedure is considered when a life-threatening airway emergency persists despite attempts to oxygenate and ventilate using a face mask, a supraglottic airway, or a tracheal tube. Causes may include severe facial or airway trauma, swelling or obstruction, blood or vomit blocking the airway, or an unexpected difficult airway. The immediate goal is to restore oxygen delivery before hypoxic injury occurs.

\section*{Role in Care}
Cricothyrotomy is an emergency rescue procedure performed by trained clinicians. It is not a routine or elective airway procedure. When time permits, the team follows a difficult-airway plan and summons experienced airway and surgical help.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician exposes and stabilizes the front of the neck, identifies the cricothyroid membrane, and creates an opening through the skin and membrane. Adult difficult-airway guidelines generally use a surgical scalpel, guide (bougie), and cuffed breathing tube. When landmarks are difficult to feel, a vertical skin incision and blunt dissection may help locate the membrane before the airway is opened. Technique and equipment depend on the patient's age, anatomy, the emergency, and local protocol.

In infants and children, the anatomy and suitable technique differ from adults. There is no single age cutoff that applies across all protocols; the team follows pediatric difficult-airway guidance and obtains specialist help when possible. A needle or cannula technique may be used in selected pediatric emergencies, but ventilation can be limited and complications are possible. This procedure must only be performed by trained clinicians.

\section*{Preparation}
There is usually no time for routine preparation during a cannot-intubate, cannot-oxygenate emergency. The team calls for help, continues any effective oxygen delivery, declares the need for emergency front-of-neck access, and uses the locally selected technique and equipment. When the patient cannot consent, clinicians act under applicable emergency consent laws and respect any valid advance directive.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
In a life-threatening cannot-oxygenate emergency, there may be no safer alternative; anatomic injury or bleeding does not automatically make the procedure impossible. Neck trauma, swelling, obesity, prior surgery, or poor landmarks can make access difficult and increase the risk of misplacement, so the team uses an approach suited to the anatomy and seeks expert help if this will not delay oxygenation. Pediatric airway access requires age-appropriate equipment and protocols.

\section*{Risks}
The main danger is failure to establish a working airway, with ongoing low oxygen. Other complications include bleeding, injury to the larynx or trachea, tube misplacement, damage to nearby structures, infection, air under the skin, pneumothorax, and later narrowing or scarring below the vocal cords. Narrow-cannula oxygenation can cause pressure-related lung or chest injury, especially when exhalation is impaired. The emergency itself carries a high risk of death or brain injury if oxygenation is not restored.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After tube placement, clinicians confirm ventilation using waveform capnography when available, chest movement, oxygen levels, and clinical assessment. They secure the tube and assess for complications such as tube obstruction, endobronchial placement, bleeding, or pneumothorax. The patient needs continuous monitoring and critical-care support. Once the patient is stable, an airway and surgical specialist plans ongoing airway management; conversion to a tracheostomy or another airway is based on the patient's needs and is not governed by one universal timeline.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The immediate goal is effective oxygenation and ventilation. Follow-up includes evaluation for airway injury or narrowing, documentation of the difficult airway, and a clear plan for future anesthesia or airway procedures. Patients who recover consciousness should be told what happened and given written information about the airway used. Psychological support and a team debrief may also be appropriate.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
This procedure is performed during a medical emergency, not by patients or bystanders. For a person who cannot breathe, call emergency services immediately and follow dispatcher instructions; do not attempt a neck incision or puncture. After hospital discharge, seek urgent care for new or worsening difficulty breathing, noisy breathing, bleeding, fever, increasing neck swelling, or discharge from the neck wound.
