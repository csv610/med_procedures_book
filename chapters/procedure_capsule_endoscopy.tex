\chapter{Capsule Endoscopy}

\section*{Overview}
Capsule endoscopy uses a swallowable camera to take pictures of the digestive tract as it moves through the body. This chapter focuses on small-bowel capsule endoscopy, which helps examine parts of the small intestine that standard upper endoscopy and colonoscopy may not reach. Other capsule systems are designed for the esophagus or colon.

The capsule sends images to a recorder worn on a belt or shoulder strap. Keep the recorder and sensors in place until the study is complete or the care team tells you to remove them; disconnecting them early may result in missing images. The capsule is usually passed naturally in a bowel movement. Unlike conventional endoscopy, capsule endoscopy cannot take tissue samples, remove polyps, or treat bleeding.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item swallowable camera capsule
  \item wearable sensor belt or recorder
  \item workstation for downloading and reviewing images
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Video capsule endoscopy; small-bowel capsule endoscopy; wireless capsule endoscopy; PillCam (a brand name).

\section*{Principle}
The capsule contains a small camera, light, battery, and transmitter. Muscles and normal movement carry it through the digestive tract while it takes and transmits images. A clinician reviews the recording after the capsule study is complete. The capsule does not steer, pause on command, or provide a tissue diagnosis.

\section*{History}
Wireless capsule endoscopy was developed in the late 1990s and became a clinical imaging method in the early 2000s.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Small-bowel capsule endoscopy is commonly used to look for a cause of suspected bleeding from the small intestine after upper endoscopy and colonoscopy have not found the source. When there is visible bleeding and small-bowel bleeding is suspected, testing is ideally done as soon as possible, within 48 hours of the bleeding episode, because this can improve the chance of finding the cause. The test may also be used for selected cases of suspected or known Crohn's disease, unexplained iron-deficiency anemia, or a suspected small-bowel lesion. In people with uncomplicated, asymptomatic iron-deficiency anemia and normal upper endoscopy and colonoscopy, a trial of iron treatment is generally considered before routine capsule testing. The best test depends on symptoms, prior test results, and the chance of a narrowed or blocked area.

\section*{Role in Diagnosis}
This is a diagnostic imaging test. It is often used after other tests, but capsule endoscopy may be the first-line small-bowel examination for suspected small-bowel bleeding after appropriate initial evaluation. It does not replace upper endoscopy or colonoscopy when those tests are needed.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
Adhesive sensors or a belt recorder are placed on the body. The patient swallows the capsule with water. If swallowing is difficult or the capsule does not move from the stomach as expected, a clinician may place it into the small intestine using an endoscope. The patient can usually go home and continue light daily activities while the capsule records, following the center's instructions. The recorder and sensors are returned or removed at the scheduled time, and a specialist reviews the images.

The capsule is disposable and usually leaves the body in stool. You usually do not need to retrieve it; follow the center's instructions about confirming that it has passed. The capsule typically records for several hours; the battery life and study length vary by device. Some patients do not have a complete examination if the capsule battery runs out before it reaches the end of the small bowel.

\section*{Preparation}
Follow the facility's specific instructions about diet, fasting, bowel preparation, and when to take medicines. Preparation differs by capsule type and reason for testing. Tell the care team about swallowing problems, prior abdominal or pelvic surgery or radiation, Crohn's disease, bowel obstruction or narrowing, abdominal pain or distention, implanted cardiac devices, pregnancy or possible pregnancy, and all medicines. Do not stop prescribed medicines unless the team instructs you to do so.

\section*{Contraindications}
Because safety data are limited, capsule endoscopy is usually postponed during pregnancy and considered only when clinically necessary after specialist discussion. Pacemakers and defibrillators are not generally considered a reason to avoid capsule endoscopy, but tell the care team about any implanted device so it can check the planned system's instructions; image quality may be affected with some devices, such as left ventricular assist devices. Do not have an MRI while the capsule may still be inside the body; wait until passage is confirmed or the care team confirms it is safe.

\section*{Precautions}
The main concern is capsule retention: the capsule may become stuck in a narrowed area of bowel. Risk is higher with known or suspected strictures, prior obstruction or small-bowel surgery, some tumors, prior abdominal or pelvic radiation, or Crohn's disease affecting the small bowel. When narrowing is a concern, the clinician may choose imaging first or use a dissolvable patency capsule to check that a capsule-sized object can pass. ESGE recommends a patency capsule before small-bowel capsule endoscopy for people with established Crohn's disease to reduce the risk of retention. Severe swallowing difficulty may require endoscopic placement or another test.

\section*{Risks}
The test is generally well tolerated and does not usually require sedation. Risks include difficulty swallowing the capsule, incomplete images, technical failure, or capsule retention. Very rarely, the capsule can enter the airway, especially in someone with a swallowing problem; the team may place it into the small intestine with an endoscope to reduce this risk. Retention rates vary with the reason for testing and individual risk, so one rate does not apply to everyone. A retained capsule may pass after medical treatment or require removal by device-assisted endoscopy; surgery is uncommon and may be needed if there is an obstruction or an underlying problem requiring surgery.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the center's instructions for when to drink, eat, and take medicines while the capsule is recording. These instructions vary; many centers allow clear fluids after a short wait and a light meal later. Continue to avoid MRI until the capsule has passed or the care team has confirmed it is safe. Normal activity is usually allowed unless the center advises otherwise. No special action is needed when the disposable capsule passes.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The clinician reviews the images for bleeding, ulcers, inflammation, vascular lesions, or other abnormalities. Image review and final results may take days or weeks. A normal or incomplete study does not rule out every small-bowel problem. Capsule images can suggest a diagnosis, but they cannot provide a tissue sample or treatment.

Next steps depend on the findings. They may include observation, medication, CT or MR enterography, repeat testing, or device-assisted enteroscopy to biopsy or treat an area found on the capsule study. If the capsule has not been seen to pass, follow the center's instructions about whether and when imaging is needed to confirm passage.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek emergency care for choking, new breathing difficulty, or persistent coughing after swallowing the capsule, as these can be signs that it entered the airway. Contact the care team promptly for new difficulty swallowing, chest discomfort, or fever after swallowing the capsule. Also contact the care team for new or worsening abdominal pain, abdominal swelling, nausea or vomiting, or inability to pass stool or gas after the test, as these may indicate capsule retention or blockage. Seek urgent care for severe pain, persistent vomiting, breathing difficulty, or other rapidly worsening symptoms. If the capsule has not passed by the time specified by the center, contact the capsule service for advice. Do not undergo MRI until passage is confirmed or the care team says it is safe.
