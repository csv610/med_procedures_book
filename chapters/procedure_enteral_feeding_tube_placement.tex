\chapter{Enteral Feeding Tube Placement}

\section*{Overview}
An enteral feeding tube carries liquid nutrition, water, or some medicines into the stomach or small intestine when a person cannot safely eat or drink enough. A tube may pass through the nose for short-term use or enter through the abdominal wall for longer-term access. Some tubes are also used to drain stomach contents. The route and procedure depend on the person's needs, digestive tract function, expected duration, and goals of care.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Nasogastric tube (NG tube); nasoenteric tube; nasoduodenal tube; nasojejunal tube (NJ tube); gastrostomy tube (G-tube); percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube; radiologically inserted gastrostomy (RIG); jejunostomy tube (J-tube).

\section*{Principle}
Enteral nutrition delivers formula into the digestive tract, where it can be digested and absorbed. An NG tube passes through the nose and ends in the stomach; a post-pyloric tube ends farther along in the small intestine. A gastrostomy or jejunostomy tube enters through the abdominal wall into the stomach or small bowel. These access routes do not guarantee protection from aspiration, and tube feeding may not be appropriate when the digestive tract cannot be used safely.

\section*{History}
Nasal feeding tubes have been used for many decades. Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy was introduced in 1980 and provided a way to place feeding access through the abdominal wall without open surgery. Endoscopic, radiologic, and surgical methods are now used according to a person's anatomy and clinical needs.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Tube feeding may be considered when oral intake is unsafe or too low to meet nutritional needs and the gastrointestinal tract is usable. Reasons can include swallowing problems after a stroke or neurologic disease, certain head or neck conditions, or prolonged illness. A tube may also be used to give water or selected medicines, or to drain the stomach. The team considers the likely benefit, burdens, alternatives, the expected duration, and the person's wishes. A feeding tube does not necessarily prevent aspiration pneumonia.

\section*{Role in Care}
Tube placement is a treatment or access procedure, not a diagnostic test. It is part of a nutrition plan prepared by clinicians with appropriate training, often including a dietitian. A nasal tube may be used when support is expected to be short term. NICE advises considering gastrostomy when enteral feeding is likely to be needed for 4 weeks or longer in adults; the timing and route are individualized, and recommendations may vary by situation and setting.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
An NG tube is passed through the nose, down the throat and esophagus, and into the stomach. Some small-bowel tubes are advanced beyond the stomach, sometimes with endoscopic, radiologic, or other guidance. A PEG tube is placed through the abdominal wall into the stomach using an endoscope; radiologic or surgical methods are alternatives. A jejunostomy tube is placed into the small intestine using an appropriate endoscopic, radiologic, or surgical approach. The method may involve local anesthetic, sedation, or anesthesia. The clinician confirms the location using the method required for that tube before it is used.

\section*{Preparation}
Preparation depends on the type of tube and urgency. The care team explains the reason, expected benefits, alternatives, and risks and obtains consent when possible. For planned gastrostomy or jejunostomy, fasting and medication instructions are provided; antibiotics may be given for some procedures. Tell the team about blood thinners, diabetes medicines, allergies, bleeding problems, prior abdominal surgery, and any nasal, facial, or esophageal injury. Do not stop or change prescribed medicines without instructions.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Enteral feeding may not be safe when the gut is obstructed, perforated, severely inflamed, or otherwise not functioning adequately. These conditions do not all rule out every type of tube, and a tube may sometimes be placed for drainage instead of feeding. Facial or skull-base injury, nasal obstruction, or esophageal disease may make nasal placement unsafe. Abdominal anatomy, ascites, infection, or bleeding risk may affect whether and how a gastrostomy or jejunostomy can be placed. A specialist assesses the person and selects the safest route.

\section*{Risks}
Nasal tubes can cause discomfort, nosebleeds, sinus or throat irritation, and accidental entry into the airway. If a misplaced tube is used, feeding or medicine can cause serious lung injury. Gastrostomy and jejunostomy placement can cause bleeding, infection, leakage, injury to nearby organs, or inflammation of the abdominal lining; sedation or anesthesia has its own risks. Any tube can become blocked, move out of place, or be pulled out. Tube feeding can cause nausea, diarrhea, constipation, fluid or blood-sugar problems, and aspiration. In a severely malnourished person, starting nutrition too quickly can cause refeeding syndrome, a dangerous shift in body salts; feeding may need to begin gradually with close monitoring.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Do not use a newly placed tube for feeding, water, or medicines until its position and readiness for use have been confirmed by the care team. For a nasal tube, follow the facility's protocol for confirming placement after insertion and checking it again before use; pH testing of aspirate and/or X-ray may be used. Listening for a "whoosh" over the stomach is not a reliable way to confirm placement. After a gastrostomy or jejunostomy, the team gives specific instructions about when feeding can start and how to clean and care for the site. The formula, rate, and schedule are advanced as advised. Flush the tube as instructed, and use only medicines and preparation methods approved for the tube. Care instructions differ by device and procedure.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The care team monitors whether the person tolerates the feeding plan and receives enough fluid and nutrition. They may follow weight, hydration, blood tests, digestive symptoms, and signs of tube-site problems. Formula and feeding schedule can be adjusted. Tube feeding may be reduced or stopped if eating and drinking become safe and adequate, or the tube may be exchanged if it is damaged, displaced, or no longer suitable.

\section*{Expected Results}
The goal is to provide needed nutrition, fluids, or medicines through a usable route while minimizing complications. Weight gain is not always the goal; maintaining weight, preventing further loss, or meeting specific nutrition needs may be appropriate. A tube does not treat the underlying cause of swallowing difficulty and does not guarantee prevention of aspiration.

\section*{Follow-up}
Keep appointments with the nutrition and medical team. Follow instructions for checking the external tube marking or site, routine flushing, and tube replacement. Contact the care team if the tube becomes blocked, moves, falls out, leaks, or causes persistent discomfort. A new or recently placed gastrostomy tract can close quickly if the tube comes out; seek prompt clinical advice and do not try to replace it unless specifically trained and instructed.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Get urgent medical advice for a tube that has moved or come out, new trouble breathing or coughing during feeding, repeated vomiting, a swollen or painful abdomen, significant leakage, bleeding, fever, or worsening redness or drainage around the site. Stop using the tube and follow the care team's emergency instructions if placement is uncertain. Seek emergency care for severe pain, breathing difficulty, heavy bleeding, or rapidly worsening symptoms.
