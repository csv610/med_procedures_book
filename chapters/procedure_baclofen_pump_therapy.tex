\chapter{Intrathecal Baclofen Pump Therapy}

\section*{Overview}
Intrathecal baclofen (ITB) therapy delivers baclofen into the fluid around the spinal cord through a catheter connected to a programmable pump implanted under the skin, usually in the abdomen. It may reduce severe spasticity with a smaller drug dose than oral treatment, but it requires surgery, dose adjustments, regular refills, and long-term device follow-up. It is not a cure for the condition causing spasticity.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item implantable programmable pump and intrathecal catheter
  \item needle and syringe for refill or access
  \item pump programmer and imaging equipment when needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Intrathecal baclofen therapy; ITB therapy; baclofen pump; spinal baclofen pump

\section*{Principle}
Baclofen reduces overactive nerve signals that cause muscle stiffness and spasms. Delivery into the intrathecal space places the medicine near the spinal cord. The pump releases a programmed amount through a catheter; the dose and schedule are adjusted to balance spasticity control with unwanted weakness or other side effects. Because treatment depends on continuous delivery, an empty reservoir, pump problem, catheter problem, or programming error can interrupt treatment or deliver too much medicine.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
ITB may be considered for severe spasticity caused by injury or disease of the brain or spinal cord when oral medicines and other treatments have not provided enough benefit or cause unacceptable side effects. Spasticity may interfere with comfort, sleep, positioning, mobility, or daily care. Before treatment, the patient, caregivers, and clinical team should agree on specific goals, such as fewer painful spasms or easier transfers. The therapy is not appropriate for every cause or pattern of muscle stiffness.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
This is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. A screening dose is usually given into the intrathecal space before implantation to assess whether baclofen helps the person's agreed goals and whether side effects are acceptable. A positive trial does not guarantee the same response to ongoing pump therapy.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
First, a specialist team reviews the person's spasticity, health, goals, medication history, ability to attend follow-up, and available support. If appropriate, a test dose is given by lumbar puncture or through a temporary catheter, and the person is monitored for benefit and side effects. If the trial supports proceeding, surgery is performed under anesthesia: the pump is placed under the skin, usually in the abdomen, and a catheter is tunneled to the intrathecal space. The pump is programmed, and the dose is adjusted gradually with clinical monitoring. Refills are performed at planned clinic visits using a needle inserted through the skin into the pump's reservoir; only trained clinicians should refill or program the pump.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the treatment team about allergies, infection, bleeding problems, prior spine or abdominal surgery, seizures, breathing problems, pregnancy, and all medicines, including oral baclofen and other sedating drugs. Discuss how spasticity supports standing, transfers, or walking, since reducing muscle tone can sometimes make these activities harder. Before implantation, make a practical plan for refills, transportation, caregivers, emergency contacts, and device alarms. The pump clinic should provide an identification card and written instructions for urgent problems and imaging such as MRI.

\section*{Contraindications}
An active infection or a serious allergy to baclofen or pump materials may prevent or delay treatment. Do not stop oral baclofen or change any pump dose without clinical direction.

\section*{Precautions}
A trial that does not meet the agreed treatment goals, or causes unacceptable adverse effects, generally argues against implantation. The team should consider whether the patient can attend refills and monitoring, with caregiver support where needed. If ITB must be stopped, the dose is usually reduced gradually under specialist supervision; sudden interruption can cause life-threatening withdrawal. MRI safety depends on the exact pump model and conditions. Always tell the imaging team about the implanted pump and follow the pump clinic's pre- and post-scan instructions; some devices require a check after MRI to confirm therapy has resumed.

\section*{Risks}
Surgery can cause pain, bleeding, infection, spinal fluid leak, or anesthesia-related complications. The catheter may move, kink, break, or become blocked, and the pump may malfunction or need surgical replacement. Dose or refill errors can cause overdose or withdrawal. Baclofen may cause drowsiness, dizziness, nausea, low blood pressure, weakness, or excessive loss of muscle tone; serious overdose can slow or stop breathing, cause seizures, or lead to coma. Abrupt interruption can cause increased stiffness or spasms, itching, fever, confusion, severe rigidity, seizures, organ failure, or death. These emergencies can occur even when a patient has previously been stable.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After implantation or a dose change, the team monitors the person for benefit and side effects and adjusts the dose gradually. Recovery instructions cover wound care, activity, and signs of infection or spinal fluid leak. Keep every refill appointment and respond to pump alarms according to the written plan. Record the next refill date and low-reservoir alarm information; do not rely on hearing an alarm as the only reminder. The pump battery eventually reaches end of service, so planned replacement surgery is needed. The timing depends on the pump model and settings and is reviewed at clinic visits.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The team checks whether treatment meets the patient's goals and whether it causes weakness, sedation, or other unwanted effects. Increased spasticity may have other causes, such as infection, constipation, skin injury, or a full bladder, as well as a device or delivery problem. Do not assume that a dose increase is the answer; contact the pump team for assessment.

\section*{Expected Results}
The goal is a meaningful reduction in spasticity or spasms while preserving useful strength and function. The amount of improvement varies, and the best dose may take weeks or months to find. Some people continue other medicines or rehabilitation. The trial response, long-term benefit, and side effects differ between individuals.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend pump clinic visits for dose review and refills on schedule. At visits, the team checks pump settings, the remaining reservoir, and alarm status, and assesses the catheter or pump if symptom control changes unexpectedly. Contact the clinic before travel or hospitalization if a refill may become due, and carry the pump identification card and emergency instructions. Tell all health professionals about the pump before procedures or imaging. If the pump is no longer needed, baclofen should generally be tapered under specialist care rather than stopped suddenly, except when an emergency overdose plan requires urgent specialist treatment.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Get emergency medical help for severe sleepiness, difficulty waking, slow or difficult breathing, fainting, seizure, or sudden severe weakness; these may signal overdose. Sudden return or worsening of spasms, itching, tingling, or low blood pressure needs immediate contact with the pump team. High fever, severe stiffness, confusion, seizure, or rapidly worsening symptoms may indicate life-threatening withdrawal and require emergency care. Also contact the pump clinic urgently for a pump alarm, missed or threatened refill, redness or drainage at the implant site, fluid leaking from the wound, or a sudden unexplained change in spasticity. Do not attempt to adjust, refill, or repair the pump yourself.
