\chapter{Injections}

\section*{Overview}
An injection delivers a medicine through a needle into a specific tissue. This chapter focuses on intramuscular (IM) injections, which place medicine in a muscle, and subcutaneous (SC) injections, which place it in the fatty tissue just beneath the skin. Other routes, including intravenous and intradermal injections, require different procedures. The correct route is determined by the prescribed medicine; the routes are not interchangeable.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Shot; injection; intramuscular injection; subcutaneous injection; parenteral medication administration

\section*{Principle}
The medicine is absorbed from the tissue where it is placed. The speed and amount of absorption depend on the medicine, formulation, injection site, and the person's circulation and tissue. Muscle injections do not always act faster than injections under the skin; follow the product-specific instructions.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Injections are used when a medicine must be delivered by a particular route, when swallowing is not suitable, or when a medicine cannot be absorbed reliably by mouth. Examples include vaccines, insulin, some blood thinners, hormones, and certain antibiotics and biologic medicines. Some injections are given by a health professional; others may be self-administered after the person has been taught the exact medicine-specific technique.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
An injection is a method of delivering treatment, not a diagnostic test. Its purpose and effect depend on the medicine being given.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician or trained patient confirms the person's identity, medicine, dose, route, and timing; checks the label and expiry; and selects the site and equipment specified for that medicine. After hand hygiene and clean preparation, the skin is cleaned when appropriate and allowed to dry. An IM needle enters the selected muscle at 90 degrees; an SC needle enters the fatty layer at an angle guided by the device, needle length, and the person's tissue. The medicine is administered according to its instructions, then the needle is removed and the site is gently pressed if needed. Site selection, needle length, skin handling, and injection speed vary by medicine and person. For vaccines at recommended sites, aspiration (pulling back on the plunger before injecting) is not recommended; follow medicine-specific instructions for other injections.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the clinician about medicine or latex allergies, bleeding problems, medicines that affect clotting, prior reactions to injections, and relevant skin problems. Confirm the prescribed medicine, dose, route, and timing, and ask for teaching if giving it yourself. Use a new sterile needle and syringe for each injection; never reuse them or share injection devices. Follow the medicine's storage, preparation, and disposal instructions, and use an approved sharps container. Do not inject through infected, inflamed, scarred, or damaged skin unless specifically directed by a clinician.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Whether an injection can be given depends on the medicine and the person's health. A previous severe allergic reaction to the medicine or one of its ingredients may prevent its use. Bleeding disorders or anticoagulant treatment can increase bruising or bleeding with IM injections and should be discussed with the prescriber. The clinician chooses an appropriate site and needle for body size, age, medicine volume, and route. Do not change the prescribed route or site on your own. Routine aspiration is not required for vaccination at recommended sites; other products may have their own instructions.

\section*{Risks}
Common effects include brief pain, tenderness, or a small amount of bleeding or bruising. Less common complications include infection, persistent swelling or a lump, tissue injury, or injury to a nearby nerve or blood vessel. The wrong medicine, dose, route, site, or technique can reduce benefit or cause harm. Any injected medicine can cause an allergic reaction, including rare, life-threatening anaphylaxis. Repeated insulin injections into the same small area can cause lumps or changes in the fat under the skin, which may affect absorption.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the instructions for the specific medicine. Mild soreness can occur; avoid rubbing or massaging the site unless instructed. If vaccination causes dizziness or faintness, sit or lie down and tell staff; vaccine recipients are commonly observed briefly to reduce injury from fainting. Put used needles and syringes directly into an approved sharps container without recapping. For repeated medicines, rotate sites as directed; insulin users should avoid injecting into lumps or thickened areas and follow a consistent rotation plan.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The expected benefit and monitoring depend on the medicine: for example, glucose monitoring for insulin or a planned symptom check after a pain medicine. If the medicine seems ineffective, or an injection was missed, leaked, or given by the wrong route, contact the prescriber or pharmacist for advice rather than repeating or changing the dose independently. Some medicines require laboratory tests or follow-up visits.

\section*{Expected Results}
The medicine reaches the intended tissue and produces its expected effect. The time to benefit varies by medicine; an injection does not guarantee that treatment will work or prevent side effects.

\section*{Follow-up}
Keep scheduled visits and monitoring appointments. Ask a clinician or pharmacist to review self-injection technique if doses are difficult, sites become painful or lumpy, or medicine appears to leak. Follow local instructions for sharps disposal.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Call emergency services for trouble breathing, swelling of the face or throat, widespread hives, collapse, or another severe allergic reaction after an injection. Contact a clinician promptly for increasing redness, warmth, swelling, pus, fever, worsening pain, new numbness or weakness near the site, or bleeding that does not stop with gentle pressure. Seek advice promptly if the wrong medicine, dose, or route was used.
