\chapter{Acupuncture}

\section*{Overview}
Acupuncture is a treatment in which a trained practitioner places thin needles through the skin at selected points. It originated in traditional Chinese medicine and is now used in many countries, most often for pain. Research suggests it may help some pain conditions and nausea related to cancer treatment. For cancer-treatment-related nausea and vomiting, however, much of the evidence predates current anti-nausea regimens, and its additional benefit alongside current standard treatment is uncertain. Benefits vary by condition and person.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Needling therapy; auricular acupuncture (needling at points on the ear)

\section*{Principle}
In traditional Chinese medicine, acupuncture is understood as affecting the flow of qi along pathways called meridians. Scientific research has found that acupuncture may affect the nervous system and tissues near the needles, but how it works is not fully understood. Some benefit may also come from factors such as expectations and the interaction between patient and practitioner.

\section*{History}
Acupuncture has been practiced in China and other parts of Asia for more than 2,500 years and later spread worldwide.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Acupuncture may help some people with back or neck pain, knee pain from osteoarthritis, postoperative pain, and migraine. It may also help nausea and vomiting related to cancer treatment, although the added benefit with current standard anti-nausea treatment is uncertain. Evidence varies by condition, and effects compared with sham acupuncture are generally smaller than effects compared with no treatment. It should not delay assessment or replace treatments with established benefit.

\section*{Role in Care}
A complementary treatment that may be used alongside standard care. Whether it is appropriate depends on the condition, evidence, patient preference, and practitioner expertise.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The practitioner reviews the patient's health, explains the expected benefits and possible risks, and obtains informed consent. The patient is positioned comfortably. The practitioner performs hand hygiene, prepares the skin according to infection-prevention practice, and inserts sterile, single-use needles at selected points. Safe needle depth depends on the body area and the patient's anatomy; the practitioner may gently move the needles or use electrical stimulation. The needles may remain in place for several minutes, often about 10 to 30 minutes. The practitioner removes all needles, checks that none remain, and disposes of them in an appropriate sharps container. The exact technique and session length vary.

\section*{Preparation}
No special preparation is usually needed. Wear clothing that allows access to the treatment area. Tell the practitioner about pregnancy, bleeding problems, blood-thinning medicines, an implanted electrical device, immune-system problems, skin conditions, or a history of fainting. Do not stop prescribed medicine for acupuncture unless the prescribing clinician directs you to do so.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Needles should not be placed through infected, ulcerated, or otherwise damaged skin. A bleeding disorder or anticoagulant use calls for individualized assessment of bleeding risk and needling technique; patients should not stop prescribed anticoagulants or antiplatelet medicines for acupuncture without direction from the prescriber. Pregnancy should be disclosed so a practitioner experienced in treating pregnant patients can adapt or avoid needling in sensitive areas, including the lower abdomen and lumbosacral region. People with reduced immunity or sensation may also need modified care. Those with pacemakers or other implanted electrical devices should tell the practitioner because electroacupuncture may not be suitable. The patient can decline or stop treatment at any time.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary soreness, minor bleeding, bruising, fatigue, or lightheadedness may occur; some people faint. Serious complications are uncommon but can include infection or injury to an organ, lung, or nerve, especially if the procedure is performed improperly or nonsterile needles are used. Sterile, single-use needles, appropriate training, and knowledge of anatomy reduce risk.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people return to usual activities after treatment. If you feel faint or unusually drowsy, wait until you feel fully alert before driving or doing anything that requires full attention. Follow the practitioner's advice about the treated area and report unexpected or persistent symptoms.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The patient and practitioner can agree on a treatment goal and track symptoms or daily function over time. Some people improve; others do not. Studies often find a larger benefit compared with no treatment than compared with simulated (sham) acupuncture. Reassess the plan if there is no meaningful improvement, and seek evaluation for persistent or worsening symptoms.

\section*{Expected Results}
The result varies. Some patients have less pain or nausea, while others notice little or no change. Acupuncture does not identify or cure the underlying cause of every symptom.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow-up depends on the reason for treatment and the patient's response. A symptom diary or simple rating scale may help track change. Persistent, new, or worsening symptoms need appropriate medical evaluation rather than repeated acupuncture alone.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact a healthcare professional for persistent bleeding, increasing redness or swelling, pus, or worsening pain at a needle site. Seek emergency care for sudden chest pain or trouble breathing after needling of the chest, upper back, or shoulder, or for any severe or rapidly worsening symptom.
