\chapter{Biliary Drainage}

\section*{Overview}
Biliary drainage is treatment to restore the flow of bile when a bile duct is blocked or narrowed. Bile drains from the liver through ducts into the small intestine and helps digest fats. Depending on the location and cause of the blockage, a clinician may place an internal stent through an endoscope, a drain through the skin and liver, or use a specialist endoscopic ultrasound-guided approach. The procedure can relieve blockage, but the cause may need separate treatment.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Biliary decompression; bile-duct drainage; biliary stenting; percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage (PTBD). Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided biliary drainage are endoscopic ways to restore bile flow. Gallbladder drainage is a different procedure.

\section*{Principle}
A blockage can prevent bile from reaching the intestine and cause bile to build up. A stent holds a narrowed duct open, while a catheter can carry bile past the blockage. A catheter may drain bile into an external bag, into the intestine, or both. The exact plan depends on where the blockage is, its cause, the patient's anatomy and condition, and the treatments planned next.

\section*{History}
Endoscopic and percutaneous methods allow many bile-duct blockages to be treated without open surgery. Equipment and techniques have advanced, and endoscopic ultrasound-guided drainage is now another specialist option when standard ERCP cannot reach or drain the duct.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Drainage may be needed when a blockage causes jaundice (yellow skin or eyes), itching, or infection of the bile ducts (cholangitis). Cholangitis is a medical emergency that usually requires antibiotics and prompt drainage to relieve the blockage. The American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy suggests endoscopic drainage within 48 hours of admission for cholangitis. For a patient in septic shock whose condition does not improve with fluid treatment, drainage in less than 24 hours may be considered. Blockages can be caused by gallstones, scar tissue or a narrowing, an injury from previous surgery, or a tumor. Drainage may also be part of a treatment plan before surgery or other treatment for a tumor, but it is not needed before every operation. The care team decides whether drainage is needed and how quickly, based on symptoms, infection, test results, and the overall treatment plan.

\section*{Role in Care}
Biliary drainage is a treatment, not a screening test. Blood tests and imaging, such as ultrasound, CT, or MRI, are usually used to find the blockage and plan treatment. ERCP is commonly used when the duct can be reached through the digestive tract and a stent, stone removal, or other treatment is needed. If ERCP fails, the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) conditionally suggests endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided drainage over PTBD. Its guidance notes that PTBD may be preferred for a patient who is hemodynamically unstable, cannot tolerate general anesthesia, has suspected malignant blockage, or is being treated where EUS expertise is unavailable; it also addresses EUS-guided options for distal malignant blockages after failed ERCP. The 2026 European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) guideline recommends EUS-guided drainage over PTBD for malignant bile-duct narrowing after failed or unfeasible ERCP, when EUS-guided drainage is feasible. These recommendations differ in scope, so the care team considers the blockage, anatomy, urgency, anesthesia tolerance, local expertise, and treatment plans when choosing a route. No single approach is best for everyone.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The method depends on the planned route:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{ERCP:} The clinician passes a flexible endoscope through the mouth into the first part of the small intestine. A small tube is passed through the opening of the bile duct. The clinician may remove a stone, widen a narrowed area, take a sample, or place a stent to keep bile flowing. The stent is internal.
  \item \textbf{PTBD:} An interventional radiologist uses ultrasound and X-ray imaging to guide a needle through the skin and liver into a bile duct. A catheter is placed to drain bile. Depending on the situation, the catheter may lead to an external bag, pass across the blockage into the intestine, or later be replaced by an internal stent.
  \item \textbf{EUS-guided drainage:} A specialist uses an endoscope with ultrasound to guide a stent that creates an internal path for bile to flow from the bile duct into the stomach or small intestine. This may be considered when ERCP cannot reach or drain the duct.
\end{itemize}
Sedation or anesthesia is used for endoscopic procedures. PTBD is usually performed with local anesthetic and may also involve sedation. The team monitors the patient during and after the procedure.

\section*{Preparation}
Preparation depends on the approach. The team may order blood tests and review imaging, liver function, blood clotting, and signs of infection. Follow instructions about fasting and medicines. Tell the team about allergies, kidney problems, possible pregnancy, prior abdominal surgery, and all medicines, especially blood thinners. Do not stop or change a prescribed medicine unless the treating team gives you a plan. Antibiotics are used to treat cholangitis; preventive antibiotics may be given in selected situations, such as when incomplete drainage is expected, depending on the procedure and patient. Ask whether you will need someone to take you home or an overnight stay.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
There is no single set of conditions that rules out all forms of biliary drainage. Bleeding risk, infection, fluid in the abdomen, altered anatomy, the level and cause of the blockage, and whether sedation or anesthesia is safe may affect the choice or timing. A serious infection of the bile ducts can make drainage urgent; it is not by itself a reason to avoid drainage. If one approach is unsuitable, another route or a different treatment may be considered by the care team.

\section*{Risks}
Risks vary by approach and by whether a stone is removed or another treatment is performed. ERCP can cause pancreatitis (inflammation of the pancreas), bleeding, infection, or a tear in the digestive tract or bile duct. PTBD can cause bleeding, infection, bile leakage, or injury to nearby tissue; an external catheter can become blocked, leak, or move out of place. Stents can also block or move. Sedation or anesthesia has its own risks. With an external drain, loss of bile and fluid may require monitoring and replacement. The team will explain the risks that apply to the planned procedure.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The care team monitors for pain, fever, bleeding, infection, and whether bile is draining as expected. After PTBD, staff teach the patient or caregiver how to secure and empty the drainage bag and care for the skin and dressing. Do not flush, cap, disconnect, or remove the catheter unless the care team has instructed you to do so. Follow the written instructions for bathing, activity, food, and medicines. A stent or catheter may need a planned exchange or removal; keep those appointments even if symptoms improve.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Successful drainage lets bile flow past or through the blockage. Jaundice, itching, or infection may improve, but improvement can take time and blood tests may be repeated. Drainage does not always remove the cause: a stone may need removal, a narrowing may need further treatment, and a tumor may need surgery, medicine, or other care. A stent or catheter can block or move, so the team will arrange monitoring and explain what to do if drainage changes.

\section*{Expected Results}
The procedure report describes the location of the blockage, how drainage was established, and whether a stent or external catheter was left in place. The amount of bile draining into a bag and follow-up blood tests can help the team assess the result. There is no single expected laboratory value or output for every patient.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow-up depends on the cause of the blockage and the type of drain or stent. The team may repeat blood tests or imaging and will tell the patient when the catheter or stent should be checked, exchanged, or removed. Some stents are temporary, so the patient should not assume that a stent will stay in place permanently. Continue follow-up for the underlying condition even if drainage relieves symptoms.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the treating team urgently for fever or chills, worsening jaundice, increasing abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, or feeling very unwell. After PTBD, also seek prompt advice if the catheter is pulled out, the dressing is soaked with bile or blood, the skin around it becomes red or painful, bile leaks around the catheter, or an external drainage bag that is meant to be collecting bile suddenly stops filling or fills much faster than usual. Seek emergency care for severe pain, heavy bleeding, fainting, confusion, or trouble breathing. Follow the specific emergency instructions provided by the care team.
