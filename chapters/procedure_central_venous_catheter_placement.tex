\chapter{Central Venous Catheter Placement}

\section*{Overview}
A central venous catheter (CVC), often called a central line, is a thin tube placed into a large vein. Its tip sits in a large vein near the heart so medicines or fluids can be given through it. Some lines also allow blood sampling or pressure monitoring. This chapter focuses on short-term central lines placed through a vein in the neck, upper chest, or groin. A PICC, tunneled catheter, implanted port, or dialysis catheter is a different type of central access and may have different placement and care instructions.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Central line; central venous line; central venous access catheter; CVC.

\section*{Principle}
The catheter provides access to a large vein. The high blood flow can help dilute some medicines that may irritate smaller veins. A CVC may also be used when reliable access is needed for certain treatments, frequent blood sampling, or selected forms of pressure monitoring. A CVC is not needed for every patient receiving urgent treatment; a peripheral IV may be started first when appropriate.

\section*{History}
Werner Forssmann performed an early human heart catheterization in 1929. Sven-Ivar Seldinger introduced the guidewire method for placing catheters in 1953.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A CVC may be used for medicines that need delivery into a large vein, such as some concentrated or vein-irritating medicines; for certain fluids or nutrition; or when repeated blood sampling or central venous monitoring is needed. Some patients need a special large-bore catheter for temporary dialysis. The care team chooses the type of access based on the treatment, how long it is needed, the person's veins and health, and the risks of each option.

\section*{Role in Care}
This is a form of vascular access, not a diagnostic test. A CVC may be placed as part of emergency or planned care. Less invasive access, such as a peripheral IV, may be suitable for some treatments. A central line should be used only when its expected benefit justifies the risks.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The team monitors the patient and uses maximal sterile barrier precautions: the clinician wears a cap, mask, sterile gown, and sterile gloves, and the patient is covered with a sterile full-body drape. The skin is usually cleaned with a chlorhexidine preparation containing more than 0.5\% chlorhexidine in alcohol and allowed to dry; another antiseptic is used if chlorhexidine cannot be used. The clinician numbs the skin with local anesthetic. Real-time ultrasound is used to guide needle placement for most internal jugular vein lines and is also used for other sites when appropriate. A thin guidewire is passed into the vein. Before a dilator or catheter is advanced over it, the clinician confirms that the wire is inside the vein, often using ultrasound. The catheter is then placed over the wire, which is removed before the catheter is secured and covered with a sterile dressing. The chosen vein may be in the neck, upper chest, or groin; the best site depends on the reason for the line, anatomy, infection and clot risks, and the clinician's judgment.

\section*{Preparation}
The team explains why the line is needed, discusses alternatives and risks, and obtains consent when the situation allows. Tell the team about blood-thinning medicines, bleeding problems, allergies to antiseptics or dressings, prior central lines, and any infection near a possible insertion site. Blood tests to assess bleeding risk may be considered based on the person's history and the planned procedure; they are not automatically required for everyone. Sedation is not always needed.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Infection or a blood clot in a proposed insertion area may lead the team to use another site or another form of access. Bleeding risk, altered anatomy, prior surgery or radiation, and lung disease may affect the choice of vein and precautions. These factors require an individualized plan and do not necessarily rule out central access.

\section*{Risks}
Possible problems during placement include bleeding or bruising, puncture of an artery, injury to nearby structures, an abnormal heart rhythm if the wire or catheter irritates the heart, air entering the bloodstream, and air leaking around the lung (pneumothorax), especially with some upper-chest approaches. The catheter can be misplaced, become blocked, or cause a blood clot in the vein, which may rarely travel to the lungs. Infection can develop at the insertion site or in the bloodstream while the line is in place. Serious complications are uncommon but may need urgent treatment.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Before the catheter is used, the team confirms that it is in a vein and that its tip is in an appropriate position, and checks for important complications. The method depends on the line and how it was placed; it may include a chest X-ray, imaging during placement, an ECG-based method, or echocardiography. A chest X-ray is not the only possible method, and the need for one depends on the placement approach and local practice.

Keep the dressing clean, dry, and in place. Trained staff use sterile technique when accessing or changing the line, disinfect the connector before each access, and allow it to dry. Tell the care team if the dressing becomes wet, loose, or dirty, or if the line is pulled, cracked, leaking, or no longer working. Do not push a displaced catheter back in. The team checks the site and reviews each day whether the line is still needed; it is removed when it is no longer necessary.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The line is ready for use when its position and function have been confirmed. While it remains in place, the team checks the insertion site, keeps the line clean, and monitors for blockage, a blood clot, or infection. The catheter is not routinely replaced on a fixed schedule just to prevent infection. How long it stays in depends on the treatment and whether it remains useful and safe.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Tell the care team promptly about fever or chills, redness, warmth, pain, swelling, or drainage near the line, or swelling of the neck, arm, or leg on the side of the catheter. Seek emergency care for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, fainting, or severe bleeding. If the line is being used at home, follow the supplied instructions for a leak, break, disconnection, or displacement and contact the line-care team immediately.
