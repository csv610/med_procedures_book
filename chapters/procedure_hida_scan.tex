\chapter{HIDA Scan}

\section*{Overview}
A hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scan, also called hepatobiliary scintigraphy or cholescintigraphy, is a nuclear medicine test. A small amount of radioactive tracer is injected into a vein. A special camera follows the tracer as the liver takes it up and sends it into bile, showing how bile moves through the bile ducts, gallbladder, and into the small intestine. It mainly shows how these organs and ducts function; other imaging, such as ultrasound, gives more detail about their structure.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item gamma camera and imaging workstation
  \item radiotracer injection supplies
  \item ECG or vital-sign monitoring when clinically indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Hepatobiliary scan, HIDA scan, cholescintigraphy, biliary scan

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A HIDA scan may help evaluate suspected gallbladder inflammation, a blockage or narrowing in bile flow, a leak after surgery, or selected problems with gallbladder emptying. In infants, a specialized study may help evaluate bile flow when biliary atresia is a concern, but the scan alone may not establish the diagnosis. The test is chosen based on symptoms and other findings, often including ultrasound and blood tests.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
HIDA is an imaging test, not a treatment. It may be used when examination, blood tests, or other imaging have not answered a specific question about bile flow or gallbladder function. Not every HIDA scan measures how well the gallbladder empties; that requires an additional step in the imaging protocol.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
You lie on a table while a technologist places a small IV in your hand or arm and injects the tracer. You then lie still as a gamma camera takes a series of pictures. The tracer normally moves from the blood into the liver and then through the bile pathways. Depending on the reason for the test, imaging may take about an hour or longer, and delayed images may be needed. Sometimes a medicine called sincalide or a fatty drink is given to make the gallbladder contract so its emptying can be measured. You may feel temporary cramping or nausea with this step. The camera detects the tracer; it does not give off radiation.

\section*{Preparation}
Follow the imaging center's instructions. Adults are commonly asked to fast for at least four hours before the scan, but usually should not fast for more than 24 hours because prolonged fasting can affect gallbladder filling and scan results. Tell the team if you have already fasted that long or are receiving nutrition through a vein or feeding tube; the team may adjust preparation or the scan. Tell the team about all medicines, especially opioid pain medicines, which can affect bile flow, and do not stop or delay a medicine unless they instruct you to. Tell them if you may be pregnant or are breastfeeding, and about allergies or a previous reaction to a radiotracer. Do not skip needed food or fluids for longer than instructed, especially if you have diabetes or another condition affected by fasting.

\section*{Contraindications}
The team may adjust or postpone the scan based on these factors.

\section*{Precautions}
Pregnancy or possible pregnancy should be discussed because the test uses a radioactive tracer; the clinician weighs the expected benefit against radiation exposure and may consider another test. If breastfeeding, ask the nuclear medicine team whether a temporary pause is needed for the specific tracer and protocol. Recent meals, prolonged fasting, certain medicines, severe illness, and liver dysfunction may affect image interpretation or preparation.

\section*{Risks}
The scan uses a small amount of radioactive tracer, so it involves a low level of radiation exposure. The IV may cause brief pain, redness, or bruising. Allergic reactions to the tracer are very rare. Sincalide or another medicine used to stimulate gallbladder emptying can cause brief nausea, cramping, or discomfort. The imaging team can discuss risks specific to your health and the planned protocol.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people can return to usual activities after the scan. Follow any instructions about fluids, breastfeeding, or contact with young children provided by the imaging center; these depend on the tracer used. The tracer loses radioactivity over time and leaves the body, in part through urine or stool. Drink fluids as instructed unless you have been told to restrict them.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
A radiologist or nuclear medicine physician reviews the images and sends a report to the ordering clinician. The report describes tracer movement through the liver and bile pathways. If gallbladder emptying was measured, it may also include an ejection fraction. Results need to be interpreted with symptoms, examination, laboratory tests, and other imaging; a scan finding alone may not establish the cause of symptoms. Additional tests or treatment depend on the clinical picture.

\section*{Expected Results}
The expected appearance depends on why the scan was ordered. In general, the tracer should be taken up by the liver and move through the biliary system, but the timing and pattern are interpreted by the specialist. If gallbladder ejection fraction is measured, the result is compared with the method and reference range used by that imaging center. There is no single result that applies to every HIDA study.

\section*{Follow-up}
The ordering clinician explains the report and whether further evaluation is needed. Follow-up may include no additional testing, other imaging, laboratory tests, or treatment directed at the underlying problem.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
The scan itself usually does not require recovery care. Contact the imaging center or your clinician if you develop a new rash, swelling, or other possible reaction after the tracer or medicine. Seek urgent care for difficulty breathing or swelling of the face or throat. Severe or worsening abdominal pain, fever, repeated vomiting, or yellowing of the skin or eyes should be assessed promptly because they may reflect the condition being investigated.
