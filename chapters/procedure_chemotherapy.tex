\chapter{Chemotherapy}

\section*{Overview}
Chemotherapy uses medicines to kill cancer cells or slow their growth. Many chemotherapy drugs affect cells throughout the body, including some healthy cells that grow and divide quickly. Chemotherapy may be used alone or with surgery, radiation therapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, or other treatments. The drug, dose, route, and schedule depend on the cancer and the person's health.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item infusion pump and medication-administration tubing
  \item peripheral IV, implanted port, or central catheter
  \item vital-signs monitoring and protective handling supplies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Chemo; anticancer drug treatment; cytotoxic chemotherapy (for medicines that damage or kill cells).

\section*{Principle}
Different chemotherapy medicines work in different ways. Many damage cancer cells or interfere with their growth and division. They can also affect healthy cells, which can cause side effects. Treatment plans include timing and supportive care to balance cancer control with recovery and safety. Chemotherapy is different from targeted therapy, hormone therapy, and immunotherapy, although treatments may be combined.

\section*{History}
Modern chemotherapy began in the 1940s, when nitrogen mustard was used to treat lymphoma. Sidney Farber's work with antifolate medicines in childhood leukemia helped establish drug treatment as a cancer therapy.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Chemotherapy may be used to cure a cancer, lower the chance that it will return, shrink a tumor before surgery or radiation, treat cancer that remains afterward, slow cancer growth, or relieve symptoms. The expected goal varies by cancer and treatment plan and should be discussed with the oncology team.

\section*{Role in Care}
Chemotherapy is a treatment, not a test. It may be the main treatment for some cancers or one part of a combined treatment plan. The treatment goal and how benefit will be measured are specific to the cancer and medicines used.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
Chemotherapy can be given through a vein, into an artery supplying the cancer, as a pill or liquid, as a shot under the skin or into a muscle, as a cream on the skin, or by injection into the spinal fluid, abdominal cavity, or bladder. Some patients receive an infusion through a temporary IV, a port, or another catheter; others take treatment at home. Many plans use cycles, with treatment followed by a break, but schedules vary. A portable pump may deliver some medicines over several hours or days.

\section*{Preparation}
Before treatment, the team reviews the cancer diagnosis, the treatment goal, other health conditions, medicines and supplements, allergies, and possible pregnancy. Blood tests are commonly used to check blood counts and organ function before treatment and at intervals during treatment. Heart tests are needed only for certain medicines or when a person's history or symptoms make them appropriate. Ask about fertility preservation before treatment if future pregnancy or having children is important to you; some treatments can affect fertility. Follow the team's instructions about food, fluids, medicines, and handling oral chemotherapy at home. Ask what to do if you miss a dose or vomit after taking a dose; do not take an extra dose unless the team tells you to.

\section*{Contraindications}
Low blood counts, infection, changes in liver or kidney function, pregnancy, or other health problems may lead the oncology team to delay treatment, adjust the dose, choose another medicine, or add supportive care. Do not skip, delay, or change treatment on your own; contact the oncology team if you are unwell or cannot take a dose.

\section*{Precautions}
There are no single blood-count or organ-function cutoffs that apply to every chemotherapy plan. Some medicines can harm a developing pregnancy or pass into breast milk. Tell the team if you are pregnant, might be pregnant, or are breastfeeding, and ask whether you or a partner should use contraception during or after treatment.

\section*{Risks}
Side effects depend on the medicine, dose, and person. They may include tiredness, nausea or vomiting, appetite changes, mouth sores, diarrhea or constipation, hair loss, nerve symptoms such as numbness or tingling, and low blood counts. Low white blood cells can increase infection risk; low platelets can increase bleeding; and low red blood cells can cause anemia. Some medicines can affect the heart, kidneys, liver, lungs, hearing, fertility, or nerves. Infusion reactions, leakage of medicine into tissue around an IV, and later health effects can occur with certain treatments. Not everyone has the same effects, and severe side effects do not mean the treatment is working better.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Take anti-nausea medicines and other supportive medicines as prescribed, and follow the plan for blood tests and clinic visits. Ask the oncology team which side effects are expected and how to manage them. Report new or worsening symptoms rather than waiting for the next appointment. Infection precautions, activity advice, and care of an IV, port, or pump depend on the treatment plan. Some cancer medicines can remain in body fluids for a time after treatment. Ask how long special precautions are needed and how to protect household members when handling medicine, body fluids, laundry, or spills. For oral chemotherapy, follow the drug-specific directions for storage, handling, and disposal; do not crush, split, or open a dose unless the oncology pharmacist says it is safe.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The oncology team assesses response using the methods appropriate for the cancer, which may include symptoms, examination, blood tests, imaging, or bone marrow or other tissue tests. Tumor markers are used only for some cancers. Treatment may be continued, changed, delayed, or stopped according to the results and side effects. The amount of nausea, fatigue, or hair loss does not show whether chemotherapy is controlling the cancer.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
During chemotherapy, call the oncology team immediately for a temperature of 100.4\,$^{\circ}$F (38\,$^{\circ}$C) or higher, or for the fever threshold in your treatment instructions. Fever can be a sign of serious infection even before a low white blood cell count is known. Call promptly for other possible infection signs, such as a new cough or sore throat, pain when urinating, mouth sores, or redness or swelling around a catheter. Do not take fever-reducing medicine before speaking with the team unless they have told you to. During an infusion, tell the nurse immediately about pain, burning, tingling, redness, or swelling at the IV site; these can signal that medicine is leaking into nearby tissue. Seek urgent help for shaking chills, trouble breathing, chest pain, confusion, uncontrolled bleeding, severe vomiting or diarrhea, or signs of an infusion reaction such as swelling of the face or throat. Follow the emergency contact plan provided by the oncology service.
