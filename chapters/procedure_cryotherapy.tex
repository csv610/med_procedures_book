\chapter{Cryotherapy}

\section*{Overview}
This chapter describes liquid-nitrogen cryotherapy for selected skin lesions. The clinician applies extreme cold to freeze and destroy the target tissue. Uses include some common warts, actinic keratoses, and benign lesions such as seborrheic keratoses or skin tags when treatment is appropriate. Cryotherapy is not one treatment for every skin growth; the diagnosis, location, size, and person's skin and health affect the choice and expected result. Internal cryoablation and whole-body cryotherapy are different procedures and are not covered here.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item cryotherapy unit or cryoprobe/applicator
  \item cryogen supply and protective eyewear
  \item speculum or positioning equipment for site-specific treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Cutaneous cryotherapy; cryosurgery; liquid-nitrogen treatment; skin freezing.

\section*{Principle}
Liquid nitrogen, at about $-196^\circ$C, rapidly freezes the lesion and nearby tissue. The freezing and thawing injure the targeted cells, and the treated tissue later blisters, crusts, or sheds as the skin heals. The treatment destroys tissue rather than preserving it for laboratory examination.

\section*{History}
The controlled use of extreme cold in medicine dates to the 19th century. Liquid nitrogen later became a widely used cryogen for treating selected skin lesions.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Cryotherapy may be used for a clinically diagnosed wart, actinic keratosis, seborrheic keratosis, or another appropriate superficial lesion. A clinician may consider freezing a confirmed low-risk basal cell carcinoma only in selected circumstances, such as when surgery is impractical or unsuitable; surgery is generally preferred when it can be performed. If a lesion is changing, bleeding, ulcerated, irregular, or otherwise suspicious for melanoma or another skin cancer, it should be assessed and may need a biopsy before any destructive treatment.

\section*{Role in Care}
Cryotherapy is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. It is a lesion-directed option for selected conditions. It does not show what cells a lesion contains, so an uncertain or suspicious lesion needs diagnostic assessment rather than empiric freezing.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician confirms the target and explains expected healing, pigment changes, and alternatives. The skin is exposed and cleaned if needed. Liquid nitrogen is applied with a spray device, a probe, or a cotton-tipped applicator. The freezing time and whether one or more freeze-thaw cycles are used depend on the diagnosis, thickness, size, and body site. The treatment usually takes a few seconds per lesion and often does not require an injection of anesthetic, although it can sting or burn.

\section*{Preparation}
There is usually no special preparation. Tell the clinician about cold urticaria (hives triggered by cold), cryoglobulinemia, Raynaud phenomenon, poor circulation, reduced sensation, immune problems, impaired wound healing, allergies, or prior problems with freezing. Mention if the lesion has changed or bled. Follow any site-specific instructions, especially for lesions near the eyes, nails, or areas with reduced blood flow or sensation.

\section*{Contraindications}
Do not freeze a lesion before its diagnosis is clear if skin cancer is suspected, because treatment destroys tissue needed for pathology.

\section*{Precautions}
A history of cold-triggered hives or cryoglobulinemia may make cryotherapy unsafe. Poor circulation, neuropathy, impaired healing, and treatment near sensitive structures require individual assessment. Freezing can cause a lighter or darker mark and may be less suitable for some cosmetic sites or skin tones; discuss this before treatment.

\section*{Risks}
Brief pain, stinging, redness, swelling, and a blister are common. The blister may contain clear fluid or blood and can be followed by a crust or scab. Pigment changes, often a lighter spot, may last for months or be permanent; scarring is possible. Infection, delayed healing, nerve injury, or damage to nearby tissue are less common. The lesion may not clear after one treatment and can recur.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Keep the treated area clean and gently wash it as directed. A dressing may be used if clothing rubs the site or if it is likely to be injured. A blister can form within hours; leave its roof intact unless the clinician gives different instructions. Do not pick or peel the scab. Healing often takes 1 to 3 weeks, but may take longer depending on the site and depth of freezing. Ask the clinician before using an ointment or covering the area.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The treated lesion may blister, crust, and shed as the area heals. This response does not by itself confirm that a lesion has been completely cleared or that it was benign. The need and timing for re-examination or repeat freezing depend on the diagnosis: warts may need a series of treatments, while actinic keratoses and other lesions have different follow-up plans. If a treated lesion persists, returns, changes, or does not heal, contact the clinician rather than repeatedly freezing it without reassessment.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the clinician for increasing pain, spreading redness or warmth, pus, fever, ongoing bleeding, or healing that is much slower than expected. Seek prompt assessment if the original lesion persists, grows, changes, or returns. For treatment near an eye, seek urgent care for eye pain or changes in vision.
