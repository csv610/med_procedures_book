\chapter{Carotid Angioplasty and Stenting}

\section*{Overview}
Carotid angioplasty and stenting (CAS) is a procedure to widen a narrowed carotid artery in the neck and support it with a small mesh tube (stent). The carotid arteries carry blood to the brain. A stent may be placed through a catheter from an artery in the groin or, using a different approach called transcarotid artery revascularization (TCAR), through a short incision at the base of the neck. A protection system is used to reduce the chance that debris will travel to the brain.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Carotid artery stenting; carotid angioplasty with stenting; CAS; transfemoral carotid artery stenting; transcarotid artery revascularization (TCAR).

\section*{Principle}
A catheter carries a balloon and stent to the narrowed segment. The balloon may be inflated to widen the artery, and the stent remains in place to support it. In transfemoral CAS, a filter or other embolic-protection device may catch debris during the procedure. In TCAR, blood flow is temporarily directed away from the brain and through a filter while the stent is placed. These approaches use different access routes and protection methods.

\section*{History}
Carotid stenting developed as an alternative to open carotid endarterectomy for selected patients. Improvements in stents, embolic protection, and access techniques have expanded the options, but selection still depends on individual risks and local expertise.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
The procedure may be considered for selected people with significant narrowing of a carotid artery, especially after a recent stroke or transient ischemic attack in the area supplied by that artery. For people without recent symptoms and with at least 70\% narrowing, the 2025 CREST-2 trial found that a four-year combined outcome occurred in 2.8\% of those treated with transfemoral stenting plus intensive medical treatment and 6.0\% of those receiving intensive medical treatment alone. The outcome counted any stroke or death in the first 44 days and later stroke on the treated side. There were 8 early strokes or deaths in the stenting group and none in the medical-treatment group. CREST-2 also found no statistically significant benefit for endarterectomy plus intensive medical treatment compared with medical treatment alone (3.7\% versus 5.3\%). These were separate parallel comparisons, not a direct comparison of stenting with surgery. CREST-2 used credentialed operators and selected participants, so its results may not apply to every patient or setting. The results support considering transfemoral stenting for some people, but do not mean everyone with asymptomatic narrowing should have a procedure; CREST-2 did not test TCAR, and the choice depends on individual risk, life expectancy, anatomy, and preferences.

Stenting is not the preferred treatment for every carotid narrowing. Best medical treatment is important for everyone and may include antiplatelet medicine, cholesterol-lowering treatment, blood-pressure and diabetes management, and stopping smoking. For many people with recent symptoms, carotid endarterectomy (open surgery to remove plaque) is preferred over transfemoral stenting, especially for people aged 70 or older and when treatment is needed within 14 days after symptoms begin. Stenting may be considered for selected people depending on age, timing, anatomy, surgical risk, and local expertise. A vascular or stroke team compares expected stroke-prevention benefit with procedural risk and discusses stenting, surgery, and medical treatment.

In CREST-2, intensive medical treatment included close management of stroke risk factors and lifestyle support. The trial aimed for systolic blood pressure below 130 mm Hg and LDL ("bad") cholesterol below 70 mg/dL; personal treatment targets may differ.

\section*{Role in Care}
CAS is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. Imaging such as carotid ultrasound, CT angiography, or MR angiography is used to confirm and measure the narrowing before deciding whether any procedure is appropriate. CAS is an alternative to endarterectomy in selected cases; it is not simply a follow-up procedure after endarterectomy.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The patient receives local anesthetic and may receive sedation; general anesthesia is used in some cases. For transfemoral CAS, a sheath is placed into an artery, usually in the groin. Catheters and wires are guided under X-ray to the carotid artery. A protection device is positioned, the narrowing may be widened with a balloon, and the stent is deployed. Contrast dye and X-ray images help the clinician check blood flow and stent position.

For TCAR, a small incision is made near the collarbone to access the carotid artery directly. A temporary circuit reverses blood flow away from the brain through a filter while the stent is placed. The route chosen depends on anatomy, medical risks, and clinician expertise. The procedure team monitors heart rhythm and blood pressure, which may change when the carotid artery is stretched.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews carotid imaging, prior stroke or TIA, heart and kidney health, allergies, and medicines. Blood tests and an ECG may be performed. Tell the team about kidney disease, a prior contrast reaction, bleeding problems, all medicines, and possible pregnancy.

Most patients receive two antiplatelet medicines, usually aspirin and clopidogrel, before or at the time of stenting and for at least 4 weeks afterward; longer-term treatment usually continues with one antiplatelet medicine. The regimen may differ for TCAR or when a person also takes an anticoagulant or has bleeding risks. Do not start, stop, or change aspirin, clopidogrel, anticoagulants, or other medicines without instructions from the treating team. Follow the team's fasting and diabetes-medicine instructions. Statin treatment and other stroke-risk reduction are also reviewed.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
The team considers whether the carotid narrowing caused the symptoms, the timing and size of any recent stroke, the shape of the arteries, age, other health conditions, and the risks of stenting compared with surgery or medical treatment. Severe vessel twisting, heavy calcification, or difficult arch anatomy can make transfemoral access less suitable. A recent large stroke or bleeding in the brain may affect when revascularization can safely occur. These factors require specialist assessment; they are not a universal checklist for excluding every approach.

\section*{Risks}
The main serious risk is stroke, which can occur if plaque or a blood clot blocks an artery in the brain; rarely, a patient may die during or shortly after the procedure. Other risks include transient ischemic attack, heart attack, bleeding or a bruise at the access site, damage to the artery, a blood clot in the stent, restenosis (re-narrowing), contrast reaction, or kidney injury. With TCAR, bleeding or swelling in the neck wound and injury to a nearby nerve are additional possible complications. Balloon inflation can temporarily slow the heart rate or lower blood pressure. Rarely, sudden changes in blood flow after treatment can cause brain swelling or bleeding (cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome).

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The patient is monitored in hospital for neurologic symptoms, heart rate, blood pressure, and access-site bleeding. Length of stay and activity limits depend on the access route, the patient's condition, and whether complications occurred. Continue prescribed antiplatelet and cholesterol-lowering medicines as directed. Do not stop antiplatelet treatment without contacting the vascular team because a clot in the stent can cause a stroke.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The team checks the treated artery and stent with imaging, usually carotid duplex ultrasound. A common schedule is a baseline scan within 3 months, scans every 6 months for the first 2 years, and then yearly until the results are stable; less frequent ongoing scans may still be advised. The schedule may be adjusted for the findings and local protocol. The team also reviews blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, smoking, and other stroke risks. Stenting reduces narrowing but does not remove the underlying tendency to develop atherosclerosis or eliminate all stroke risk.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Call emergency services immediately for sudden facial droop, weakness or numbness on one side, trouble speaking or understanding, sudden vision loss, severe imbalance, a sudden severe headache, seizure, or new confusion. These can signal a stroke or a rare complication from a sudden increase in blood flow to the brain after treatment. Also seek emergency help for chest pain, fainting, severe breathing difficulty, or rapidly increasing neck swelling, which can threaten the airway after TCAR. Contact the vascular team for bleeding or an enlarging lump at the access site, increasing neck swelling that is not rapidly worsening, fever, or other new symptoms.
