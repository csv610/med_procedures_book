\chapter{Electrocardiogram}

\section*{Overview}
An electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG) records the heart's electrical signals through small sensors attached to the skin. A resting ECG records a short sample while the person lies still. Other tests record heart rhythm over a longer period or while the heart is working harder. An ECG does not send electricity into the body.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item electrocardiograph with ECG leads and electrodes
  \item skin-preparation supplies
  \item recording paper or digital display
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
ECG; EKG; electrocardiography. A portable recording is called ambulatory ECG monitoring or Holter monitoring. An exercise ECG may be called a stress test.

\section*{Principle}
Each heartbeat creates electrical signals that spread through the heart muscle. Sensors (electrodes) detect these signals, and the ECG machine displays them as lines on a screen or paper. The pattern can show heart rate, rhythm, and the timing and direction of electrical activity.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
An ECG may be used to assess chest pain, palpitations, fainting, shortness of breath, a suspected abnormal rhythm, or possible heart attack. It can also help monitor known heart conditions, implanted pacemakers, and the effects of certain medicines or electrolyte changes. The clinician chooses a resting, ambulatory, or stress recording based on the symptoms and question being investigated.

\section*{Role in Care}
An ECG is a quick, noninvasive test that can contribute to diagnosis and monitoring. It does not show the heart's structure or pumping directly. A normal ECG, including one recorded when a heart attack is suspected, does not rule out every heart problem; clinicians interpret it with symptoms, examination, and, when needed, repeat ECGs or other tests.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
\textbf{Resting ECG:} A common resting test is a 12-lead ECG: 10 adhesive electrodes on the chest and limbs create 12 views of the heart's electrical activity. The patient lies down while a technician may clean the skin and attach the electrodes. They connect by wires to the machine, which records the pattern for a few seconds while the patient stays still. The recording itself usually takes only a few minutes.

\textbf{Ambulatory ECG:} A small wearable recorder or patch records heart rhythm during usual activities for a set period, from hours to days or longer. The patient may be asked to keep a diary of symptoms and activities and to follow instructions about keeping the device dry.

\textbf{Exercise or stress ECG:} Electrodes record the ECG while the patient walks on a treadmill or pedals a stationary bicycle. If exercise is not possible, medicine may be used to increase the heart's workload. A stress test may also include heart imaging, such as ultrasound or nuclear imaging; this is separate from the ECG recording. Heart rate, blood pressure, and symptoms are monitored.

\section*{Preparation}
A resting ECG usually needs no special preparation. Wear a top that is easy to remove and avoid lotions, oils, or powder on the chest so the electrodes stick well. Tell the technician about medicines, supplements, symptoms, and any implanted heart device. Do not stop medicines unless the clinician tells you to. For a stress test, follow the testing center's specific instructions about meals, caffeine, exercise, and medicines; these instructions vary.

\section*{Contraindications}
A resting ECG has no major medical contraindications. Exercise stress testing may not be suitable for some people, so the clinical team assesses safety and may choose a different test. An ECG needed for possible emergency heart symptoms should not be delayed for routine preparation.

\section*{Precautions}
Skin irritation, wounds, dressings, or body hair may make electrode placement harder, but the technician can often adjust placement or prepare the skin.

\section*{Risks}
A resting ECG is painless and has very little risk. Removing the stickers may cause brief discomfort or mild skin irritation. A stress ECG involves exercise or medicine that increases the heart's workload and can cause symptoms; trained staff monitor the patient throughout and stop the test if needed.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
There is no recovery period after a resting ECG, and normal activities can usually resume right away. Follow the monitoring team's instructions while wearing an ambulatory device, including when to remove it and whether it can get wet. After a stress test, staff may observe the patient briefly until symptoms and heart rate return toward baseline.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
A clinician reviews the tracing for rate, rhythm, conduction, and patterns that may suggest reduced blood flow, heart muscle injury, or other problems. An abnormal result does not always mean heart disease, and a normal result does not exclude all conditions. Depending on the findings, the clinician may recommend blood tests, imaging, a longer recording, or other assessment.

\section*{Expected Results}
The test produces a tracing of the heart's electrical activity during the recording period. A brief resting tracing can miss an abnormality that comes and goes; longer monitoring may be more useful when symptoms are intermittent.

\section*{Follow-up}
Ask the clinician who ordered the ECG how and when results will be explained. Follow-up depends on the reason for testing and the result. If wearing a monitor, return or transmit it as instructed and include symptom notes if requested.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
An ECG itself does not usually cause symptoms. Seek emergency care for new or severe chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting, or other sudden serious symptoms, even if a recent ECG was normal.
