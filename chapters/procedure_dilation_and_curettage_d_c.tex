\chapter{Dilation and Curettage}

\section*{Overview}
Dilation and curettage (D\&C) is a procedure in which the cervix, the opening to the uterus, is gently widened so tissue can be removed from inside the uterus. Tissue may be collected for testing or removed to treat a condition. Removal is often done with suction; a curette may also be used. The method, anesthesia, and recovery depend on why the procedure is being done.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item speculum, tenaculum, and cervical dilators
  \item uterine curette or suction cannula
  \item ultrasound equipment when used for guidance or confirmation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
D\&C; uterine evacuation; suction curettage. When suction is used, the procedure may be called vacuum aspiration.

\section*{Principle}
After the cervix is opened enough to pass instruments, the clinician removes a sample or contents from the uterine cavity. D\&C may be done alone or with hysteroscopy, which uses a small camera to view the inside of the uterus. The tissue may be sent to a laboratory for examination.

\section*{History}
D\&C developed as a way to investigate and treat uterine bleeding and remove pregnancy tissue. Modern practice often uses suction and may use hysteroscopy to guide diagnosis or treatment.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A D\&C may be used to obtain uterine tissue when evaluating abnormal bleeding or a possible problem such as endometrial overgrowth, or to remove pregnancy tissue after a miscarriage or during some abortion care. It may also be used for retained tissue after pregnancy. For some early pregnancy losses, waiting for the tissue to pass or using medicine may be alternatives; the options depend on the person's health, the clinical situation, and their preferences. For abnormal bleeding, other evaluation or treatments may be more appropriate, such as an office endometrial biopsy, hysteroscopy, or medicine. The clinician recommends the approach based on the cause and the patient's circumstances.

\section*{Role in Care}
D\&C can be diagnostic, therapeutic, or both. It is not the only way to investigate abnormal uterine bleeding, and it is not a routine first treatment for all heavy bleeding.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure may take place in a clinic, outpatient unit, or operating room. For miscarriage care, manual vacuum aspiration under local anesthetic in a clinic may be an option; surgery in an operating room under general anesthesia is another option when clinically appropriate. The clinician places a speculum in the vagina, gently widens the cervix, and removes tissue with suction, a curette, or both. Hysteroscopy or ultrasound may be used in some cases to guide the procedure. Many patients go home the same day.

\section*{Preparation}
The care team reviews health conditions, medicines, allergies, and the reason for the procedure. A pregnancy test may be done when relevant to the situation. Fasting is needed only if the planned anesthesia or sedation requires it; follow the instructions from the treating team. Ask whether a driver or adult support person is needed after sedation or general anesthesia. Do not stop prescribed medicines unless the clinician managing them advises you to.

\section*{Contraindications}
No specific absolute contraindication is identified here; the treating team assesses suitability for the individual.

\section*{Precautions}
The team assesses the cause of bleeding or pregnancy-related symptoms before choosing a D\&C. Pregnancy tissue removal can be an indication for the procedure; however, D\&C does not treat an ectopic pregnancy, so the pregnancy location must be considered when relevant. Infection, bleeding risk, and other health conditions may affect timing, precautions, or the method used. The clinician should discuss alternatives and obtain informed consent.

\section*{Risks}
Most procedures are completed without serious problems. Possible complications include bleeding, infection, injury to the cervix, or a hole in the uterine wall (perforation). Occasionally, some tissue remains and further treatment is needed. Anesthesia or sedation can have side effects. Rarely, scar tissue can form inside the uterus, especially after procedures involving pregnancy tissue; this is called intrauterine adhesions or Asherman syndrome. Risk depends on the reason for the procedure and the technique used.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Mild cramping and light bleeding or spotting are common for several days; spotting may continue for a few weeks, depending on the reason for the procedure. The next menstrual period may come earlier or later than usual. Rest as needed and use pain relief only as advised. If sedation or general anesthesia was used, follow the team's instructions about driving, work, alcohol, and having an adult with you. The team may advise using pads instead of tampons and avoiding vaginal sex or swimming while bleeding; follow the instructions given for your procedure. If tissue was removed for testing, ask when and how results will be shared.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The outcome depends on the reason for the procedure. A laboratory may examine the tissue, but results do not always provide a complete diagnosis or end the need for follow-up. After treatment for miscarriage or retained pregnancy tissue, the care team may recommend a pregnancy test, blood test, or other follow-up based on the situation. Contact the clinic if results or a follow-up plan are not provided.

\section*{Expected Results}
The procedure may remove the intended tissue or provide a sample for diagnosis. It may reduce bleeding when bleeding is caused by tissue that can be removed, but it does not treat every cause of heavy bleeding. Follow-up treatment may be needed depending on the findings.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend follow-up if recommended to review pathology results, symptoms, or pregnancy-related care. If periods do not return within about three months after a D\&C, contact a health professional, especially if pregnancy is possible or symptoms continue.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the treating team urgently for very heavy bleeding (such as soaking a large pad each hour for two hours), worsening or severe abdominal pain, fever or chills, foul-smelling vaginal discharge, fainting, or feeling seriously unwell. Seek emergency care for collapse or severe weakness.
