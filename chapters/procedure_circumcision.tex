\chapter{Circumcision}

\section*{Overview}
Circumcision is surgery to remove some or all of the foreskin, the skin covering the tip of the penis. It may be performed in a newborn, child, or adult for religious, cultural, personal, or medical reasons. The setting, technique, anesthesia, recovery, and risks depend strongly on the person's age and health. Circumcision is permanent and is not medically necessary for every person with a foreskin.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Male circumcision; foreskin removal.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Some families choose newborn circumcision for cultural, religious, or personal reasons. Medical reasons can include repeated foreskin or glans inflammation, scarring that causes pathologic phimosis, or other selected conditions. A tight foreskin is normal in many babies and young children and usually loosens naturally; it should not be forced back. For some foreskin conditions, treatment may include a prescribed steroid cream or a foreskin-preserving operation. The clinician discusses likely benefits, limits, and alternatives for the specific reason for surgery.

Families may also weigh potential preventive health benefits. These vary with age and setting and can include a lower risk of urinary tract infection in infancy and, in high HIV-burden settings, a lower risk of a male acquiring HIV through heterosexual sex. Circumcision does not eliminate these risks or the risk of other sexually transmitted infections, and it does not replace condoms or other prevention.

\section*{Role in Care}
Circumcision is a surgical procedure, not a diagnostic test. In a newborn it is usually elective. When it is being considered to treat a medical problem, the need and alternatives depend on the diagnosis.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is done with sterile instruments. For a newborn, a clinician uses an age-appropriate clamp or ring technique and provides effective pain relief, usually a local anesthetic injection around the penile nerves or under the skin. Comfort measures such as swaddling or sucrose may help but should not replace anesthetic pain relief. For an older child or adult, the clinician removes the foreskin under local, regional, or general anesthesia and usually closes the edges with dissolving stitches. The glans and urinary opening are checked, and a dressing or protective ointment may be applied. The procedure often takes less than an hour, but timing varies by age, technique, and setting.

\section*{Preparation}
The care team reviews the reason for surgery, medical history, allergies, medicines, bleeding problems, and anesthesia plan. Follow instructions about eating, drinking, and medicines; fasting may be needed if sedation or general anesthesia is planned. For newborns, clinicians confirm that the baby is stable, has received recommended vitamin K, and has no genital difference that could affect the operation. Parents or guardians should have time to discuss the choice, benefits, risks, alternatives, pain relief, and aftercare. An older child or adult should be involved in the decision and provide consent when legally able.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Newborn circumcision is usually postponed if the baby is unwell, premature or medically unstable, has a bleeding disorder or a relevant family history, or has not received recommended vitamin K. A penile or urinary opening difference, such as hypospadias, curvature, or a buried penis, needs specialist assessment because foreskin tissue may be needed for repair. In older patients, active infection, bleeding risk, medicines that affect clotting, or health conditions affecting anesthesia or wound healing may change the timing or plan. The team should assess these factors rather than applying one rule to everyone.

\section*{Risks}
Pain, swelling, bruising, and a small amount of bleeding can occur. Other risks include infection, bleeding that needs treatment, adhesions or scarring, removal of too much or too little skin, injury to the glans or urinary opening, and a result that needs further surgery. Anesthesia has its own risks. Complications are uncommon when the procedure is performed by a trained clinician with appropriate pain relief and sterile technique, but the chance and type of complications vary with age, setting, and medical condition. The clinician can discuss possible changes in appearance or sensation and the uncertainty of predicting an individual's experience.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the clinician's instructions for cleaning, dressing, ointment, pain relief, bathing, and activity. Keep the area clean and do not pull back or manipulate healing skin unless instructed. For newborns, check the penis and diaper at each change for bleeding and urine. Mild redness and a yellowish coating can be part of normal healing. Use petroleum ointment only as directed, and let a plastic ring fall off on its own; ask the care team when to call if it does not. Newborns usually heal in about 7 to 10 days. Older children and adults may take several weeks; healing after an adult procedure often takes 4 to 6 weeks. Avoid activities that could injure the wound, including sexual activity, until it has healed and the clinician says it is safe.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The foreskin is permanently removed. Follow-up may be arranged to check healing or address the original medical problem. If circumcision was done for a tight foreskin or recurrent inflammation, the clinician explains what symptoms should improve and whether more treatment may be needed.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek prompt medical advice for bleeding that does not stop with gentle, continuous pressure, increasing redness or swelling, pus, fever, worsening pain, or a wound that opens. Inability to urinate needs urgent assessment. For a newborn, seek urgent advice if the baby does not urinate within the timeframe given by the clinician, appears very unwell, or the penis becomes dark or markedly swollen. Follow the discharge instructions for the correct contact number and emergency care.
