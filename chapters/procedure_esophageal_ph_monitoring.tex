\chapter{Esophageal pH Monitoring}

\section*{Overview}
Esophageal pH monitoring records how much acid reaches the esophagus and how long it stays there. A small catheter sensor or a temporary wireless capsule sends measurements to a recorder while the person goes about usual daily activities. The test can help assess gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) when the diagnosis is uncertain or symptoms continue despite treatment. The type of test and whether acid medicines are continued depend on the clinical question.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item pH catheter with recording unit or wireless pH capsule
  \item endoscope for wireless capsule placement when used
  \item monitoring recorder and data-review software
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Esophageal pH test; ambulatory reflux monitoring; 24-hour pH monitoring; wireless pH monitoring; Bravo pH test (a brand name). If combined with impedance, the test may be called pH-impedance monitoring.

\section*{Principle}
A sensor measures acidity in the esophagus over time. The recorder tracks periods when acid levels fall and can compare them with symptoms recorded by the patient. A standard pH test detects acid reflux. A pH-impedance study also detects movement of liquid or gas and can identify weakly acidic or non-acid reflux.

\section*{History}
Ambulatory pH testing developed from catheter-based recordings that monitored esophageal acidity for about a day. Wireless capsules later allowed longer recordings without leaving a catheter through the nose. These tests provide objective information about acid exposure, but results must be interpreted with symptoms and other findings.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Testing may be recommended when reflux symptoms need objective evaluation, when endoscopy has not answered the question, when symptoms continue despite treatment, or before selected anti-reflux procedures. For people without previously proven GERD, testing is commonly done off acid-suppressing medicine to determine whether abnormal reflux is present. For people with established GERD whose symptoms continue despite treatment, a clinician may choose pH-impedance monitoring while medicine is continued. The appropriate approach depends on the person's history and purpose of testing.

\section*{Role in Care}
Ambulatory pH monitoring is a diagnostic test that measures reflux over time. It is different from esophageal manometry, which measures muscle contractions, and from endoscopy, which examines the lining. A normal endoscopy does not by itself rule out GERD. Testing can help determine whether acid exposure is abnormal and whether recorded symptoms occur around reflux events, but it does not prove that every symptom is caused by reflux.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
There are two common approaches. For catheter-based testing, a thin tube with a pH sensor is passed through the nose into the esophagus, usually while the person is awake. It connects to a small recorder worn for about 24 hours, after which the tube and recorder are returned. For wireless testing, a clinician attaches a small pH capsule to the esophageal lining during an upper endoscopy. The capsule sends readings to a recorder for about 48 to 96 hours, depending on the system. It later detaches and passes naturally in a bowel movement. During either test, the person records symptoms, meals, sleep, and relevant activities using a diary or recorder buttons. Some centers offer combined pH-impedance testing instead of pH-only monitoring.

\section*{Preparation}
Follow the testing center's instructions about fasting and medicines. Whether proton-pump inhibitors or other acid-suppressing medicines should be stopped or continued depends on whether the test is intended to establish GERD or assess symptoms despite treatment. When testing off treatment, proton-pump inhibitors are commonly stopped about 7 days beforehand, but the center will give you an individual plan; do not stop medicines on your own. Tell the team about swallowing difficulty, prior esophageal surgery, bleeding problems, pregnancy or possible pregnancy, and any implanted electronic device. Wireless capsule placement usually involves endoscopy and its preparation and sedation instructions; catheter testing usually does not require sedation.

\section*{Contraindications}
For the Medtronic Bravo system, pacemakers and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators are listed as contraindications; a catheter-based test may be considered instead. If a Bravo wireless capsule is used, do not have magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) within 30 days after placement; follow the device-specific instructions you receive.

\section*{Precautions}
The clinician considers whether a catheter can be passed safely and whether a wireless capsule can be attached safely. A narrowing or blockage of the esophagus, severe swallowing difficulty, or certain prior operations may affect the choice of test. The team also considers bleeding risk and conditions that affect endoscopy or sedation.

\section*{Risks}
Catheter testing may cause temporary nasal or throat discomfort, gagging, or a minor nosebleed. A wireless capsule may cause temporary chest discomfort or a sensation when swallowing. Rare complications of capsule placement include bleeding, capsule detachment, or injury to the esophagus. Risks related to endoscopy or sedation apply when these are used. Neither method measures every type of reflux unless the study also includes impedance.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After catheter placement, most people continue usual activities while wearing the recorder and return it as instructed. After wireless capsule placement, follow the clinic's eating and activity instructions and keep the recorder near the body as directed. The capsule usually detaches on its own and passes in stool; do not try to retrieve it. If a Bravo capsule was used, do not have MRI within 30 days after placement. If sedated for capsule placement, follow the usual escort and activity restrictions for sedation.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The report may include total acid exposure time, number and timing of acid reflux events, and how closely reported symptoms occurred with reflux. For pH-impedance testing, it may also describe non-acid reflux. Interpretation depends on test type, medicine status, recording quality, symptoms, and other findings. The clinician reviews the report and explains whether it supports GERD, makes it less likely, or leaves the question uncertain.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no single expected result for everyone. Testing may show normal acid exposure, abnormal acid exposure, or an inconclusive pattern. A normal pH study makes excessive acid exposure less likely during the recording period but does not rule out every cause of symptoms or every type of reflux. Day-to-day variation can occur, which is one reason a longer wireless study may be chosen for some people.

\section*{Follow-up}
Review the results with the ordering clinician before changing treatment. Depending on the findings, the clinician may recommend continuing, adjusting, or reducing acid-suppressing medicine, additional tests, or another treatment. Results are considered with symptom history and may be used in planning an anti-reflux procedure, but pH monitoring alone does not determine whether surgery is appropriate.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the testing center for persistent or worsening chest pain, difficulty swallowing, repeated vomiting, or symptoms that concern you after catheter or capsule placement. Seek urgent care for severe chest pain, trouble breathing, inability to swallow, vomiting blood, heavy bleeding, or rapidly worsening symptoms. Follow the center's instructions about the recorder and wireless capsule.
