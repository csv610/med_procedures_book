\chapter{Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)}

\section*{Overview}
Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) combines upper endoscopy with X-ray imaging to examine and treat selected problems in the bile ducts and pancreatic duct. It is now used mainly to treat a problem, such as removing a bile-duct stone, opening a narrowing, draining an infected or blocked duct, or placing a stent. Less invasive tests such as magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) or endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) are often used first when the goal is diagnosis alone.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item duodenoscope and video processor
  \item cannula/sphincterotome, guidewire, and contrast system
  \item fluoroscopy and biliary stents or extraction accessories as needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
ERCP; endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography; biliary endoscopy.

\section*{Principle}
A flexible endoscope is passed through the mouth into the first part of the small intestine. The clinician guides a small catheter through the opening where the bile and pancreatic ducts drain. Contrast dye is injected into the ducts and X-ray images help guide treatment. Instruments passed through the endoscope can remove stones, widen a narrowed area, collect a sample, cut the muscle around the duct opening (sphincterotomy), or place a drainage tube called a stent.

\section*{History}
ERCP was developed in the late 1960s as a way to image the bile and pancreatic ducts. As MRCP and EUS became available, ERCP shifted toward treatment because it can cause complications, including pancreatitis.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
ERCP may be used to remove stones from the common bile duct, drain a blocked or infected bile duct, treat a bile leak, or open a benign or cancer-related narrowing. It can also treat selected pancreatic duct problems, such as a blockage or leak. A stent may be placed to keep a duct draining; some stents are temporary and must be removed or exchanged as scheduled. ERCP is not usually used to drain a pancreatic pseudocyst, which is generally evaluated for other treatments.

\section*{Role in Care}
ERCP is a treatment procedure rather than a first-choice diagnostic test in most cases. If a duct problem is suspected but treatment is not yet needed, MRCP, EUS, or other imaging may provide the needed information with less risk. ERCP is selected when the expected benefit of treating or sampling a duct problem outweighs its risks.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
ERCP is performed in a hospital or endoscopy center by a specially trained clinician. The patient receives deep sedation or general anesthesia and is monitored throughout. The endoscope passes through the mouth, esophagus, and stomach into the duodenum. The clinician identifies the duct opening, places a catheter, and uses contrast dye and X-rays to view the ducts. Depending on the problem, treatment may include stone removal, sphincterotomy, dilation, tissue sampling, or stent placement. The procedure can take longer when treatment is complex.

\section*{Preparation}
Follow the endoscopy team's instructions about fasting and medicines. Tell the team about allergies (including to contrast or medicines), blood thinners, diabetes medicines, pregnancy or possible pregnancy, heart or lung conditions, and prior problems with anesthesia. Some medicines may need adjustment, especially if a sphincterotomy or other treatment is planned; do not stop medicines without instructions. Antibiotics are used to treat an existing infection and may be given before ERCP when incomplete drainage is expected or for certain higher-risk situations; they are not needed for everyone. The team may also use medicines or fluids to lower the risk of pancreatitis when appropriate.

\section*{Contraindications}
ERCP may be delayed or avoided if the risks of sedation or the procedure are too high, or if a safer test or treatment is available. Active pancreatitis alone does not rule out ERCP; urgent ERCP may be needed when pancreatitis is caused by ongoing bile-duct blockage or cholangitis.

\section*{Precautions}
Bleeding risk, infection, pregnancy, and other health conditions affect planning. The clinician weighs these factors against the urgency of treating the duct problem.

\section*{Risks}
ERCP can cause pancreatitis, infection of the bile ducts or gallbladder, bleeding (especially after sphincterotomy), or a tear in the intestine or duct. Sedation or anesthesia may affect breathing, heart rate, or blood pressure. Pancreatitis is the most common serious complication. Its risk varies with the person's health and what is done. Guidelines recommend a rectal anti-inflammatory medicine for most patients if it is safe; the team may also use intravenous fluids or a temporary pancreatic stent for selected higher-risk procedures. These steps lower risk but cannot prevent every complication.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After ERCP, staff monitor the patient while sedation wears off and check for symptoms of complications. A sore throat or mild bloating may occur. Some people go home the same day; others stay longer depending on the procedure and their condition. Blood tests or imaging are obtained when clinically needed, not automatically for every patient. Follow instructions about eating, activity, and restarting medicines. If a temporary stent was placed, make sure you know the plan and date for its removal or exchange.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The endoscopist may explain initial findings after the procedure, but pathology or other test results can take longer. The report describes whether a stone was removed, a narrowing was treated, or drainage was restored. Success depends on the original problem and may require additional treatment. A stent may need removal or replacement even if symptoms improve.

\section*{Expected Results}
The goal is to treat the duct problem, such as restoring bile flow or removing a stone. There is no single normal result. Some conditions need more than one procedure, and ERCP does not treat every cause of abdominal pain or jaundice.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend follow-up to review findings, biopsy results, and the treatment plan. Keep any appointment for temporary stent removal or exchange; a stent left in place longer than intended can cause blockage or infection. Depending on the condition, follow-up may include blood tests, ultrasound, CT, or MRCP. Repeat ERCP is used when another duct treatment is needed, not simply as routine imaging.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
After ERCP, seek urgent medical care for severe or worsening abdominal or chest pain, repeated vomiting, fever or chills, vomiting blood, black or bloody stools, yellowing of the skin or eyes that worsens, difficulty swallowing that worsens, or trouble breathing. These can be signs of pancreatitis, infection, bleeding, or injury. Follow the endoscopy unit's instructions and tell other clinicians that you recently had ERCP.
