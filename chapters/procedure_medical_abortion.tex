\chapter{Medical Abortion}

\section*{Overview}
Medical abortion (medication abortion) uses medicines to end an intrauterine pregnancy. The commonly used combination is mifepristone followed by misoprostol. The medicines cause the pregnancy to stop developing and the uterus to contract and empty. Depending on gestational age, clinical circumstances, local rules, and the person's preference, care may take place at home, in a clinic, or in another health-care setting. This chapter covers induced abortion; medication management of miscarriage is a related but separate clinical situation.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Medication abortion, abortion with pills, mifepristone--misoprostol abortion

\section*{Principle}
Mifepristone blocks progesterone, a hormone needed to maintain pregnancy. Misoprostol causes the uterus to contract and pass the pregnancy tissue. A common regimen through 70 days of pregnancy is 200 mg mifepristone by mouth, followed 24--48 hours later by 800 micrograms of misoprostol in the cheek pouches. Other routes or protocols may be used under current clinical guidance, particularly at different gestational ages or where mifepristone is unavailable. The exact plan should come from a qualified source and follow local clinical and regulatory requirements.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Medication abortion is one option for a person who decides to end a pregnancy and is eligible for the regimen. Other options include procedural abortion and continuing the pregnancy. Counseling should be voluntary, private, and nonjudgmental, and should include expected effects, alternatives, follow-up, and how to obtain urgent care.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Medication abortion is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. Pregnancy duration is usually estimated from the first day of the last menstrual period and clinical history. Ultrasound is not required for every uncomplicated early pregnancy when dates are reliable and there are no symptoms or risk factors for ectopic pregnancy. Examination, ultrasound, or laboratory tests may be needed when dates are uncertain, symptoms suggest ectopic pregnancy, or other clinical concerns are present.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The person takes mifepristone and then misoprostol according to the treatment plan. Cramping and bleeding, often heavier than a period and sometimes with clots, commonly begin after misoprostol; the timing and amount vary. Nausea, diarrhea, chills, a short-lived fever, headache, and fatigue can also occur. Pain medicine and anti-nausea medicine may be offered. The process can take hours or longer, and spotting may continue for days or weeks. A misoprostol-only regimen is another evidence-based option in some settings, but the regimen and need for repeat doses differ.

\section*{Preparation}
Before treatment, the care team confirms pregnancy and estimates gestational age, reviews symptoms and risk factors for ectopic pregnancy, medical history, allergies, and medicines. The team should know about an intrauterine device (IUD), bleeding disorder, anticoagulant use, chronic adrenal failure, long-term systemic corticosteroid use, inherited porphyria, or a prior ectopic pregnancy or tubal surgery. The person receives instructions for pain relief, expected bleeding, follow-up, and a 24-hour contact or emergency-care plan. Routine Rh testing and Rh immune globulin are not recommended by ACOG for abortion before 12 weeks; recommendations may differ by jurisdiction or clinical circumstance, especially at or beyond 12 weeks.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Mifepristone is not appropriate for ectopic pregnancy, chronic adrenal failure, long-term systemic corticosteroid therapy, inherited porphyria, allergy to either medicine, or certain bleeding disorders or anticoagulant use. An IUD in place is removed before treatment. Some of these conditions may require a different treatment plan rather than ruling out all abortion care. Medication abortion does not treat ectopic pregnancy; urgent evaluation is important for symptoms or risk factors that raise concern. The clinician discusses alternatives if the pregnancy is beyond the gestational duration supported by the planned regimen or if another condition changes safety.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
Cramping and bleeding are expected. Possible complications include incomplete abortion, ongoing pregnancy, or bleeding that requires urgent treatment; infection is uncommon but can be serious. Some people need an additional misoprostol dose, aspiration, or another treatment. If a pregnancy continues after exposure to misoprostol, prompt follow-up is important to discuss the findings and available options. Serious complications are uncommon, but anyone using the medicines needs clear instructions and access to medical care.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Use pain relief and other medicines as directed. Rest or resume ordinary activities according to comfort; there is no general need to avoid tampons or sex for a fixed period, but follow individualized instructions and avoid anything that worsens pain or bleeding. A person can become pregnant again soon after an abortion, before the next menstrual period; contraception can be discussed if desired. Bleeding often lasts longer than a typical period, and the next period commonly returns within several weeks.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Completion is confirmed by an appropriate follow-up plan, which may use symptoms and medical history, a pregnancy test at the instructed time, blood human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) tests, or ultrasound. A routine in-person visit or ultrasound is not necessary after every uncomplicated abortion. Bleeding alone does not prove that the abortion is complete, and ultrasound findings such as uterine lining thickness alone do not mean that a procedure is needed. Little or no bleeding after misoprostol, or continuing pregnancy symptoms, should prompt contact with the care team.

\section*{Expected Results}
The pregnancy ends and the uterus passes the pregnancy tissue. The amount and duration of bleeding vary. A positive pregnancy test can persist for a time after a successful abortion, so testing should be done at the time and in the way specified by the care team.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow the testing or contact plan provided by the clinician or service. Follow-up is especially important if symptoms do not occur as expected, pregnancy symptoms continue, or a test remains positive at the advised time. The care team should explain how results will be reviewed and what additional treatment may be offered if the abortion is incomplete or the pregnancy is ongoing.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent care for very heavy bleeding (such as soaking 2 full-size pads per hour for 2 consecutive hours), fainting, severe or worsening abdominal pain, or signs of ectopic pregnancy such as one-sided pain, shoulder pain, dizziness, or weakness. Contact the care team promptly for fever that continues more than 24 hours after misoprostol, foul-smelling discharge, feeling very unwell, or little or no bleeding after misoprostol. If emergency symptoms occur or the care team cannot be reached, use local emergency services.
