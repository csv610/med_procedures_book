\chapter{Electroconvulsive Therapy}

\section*{Overview}
Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is a treatment in which a brief, controlled electrical stimulus is given to the brain while the person is under general anesthesia. The stimulus causes a short, monitored seizure. ECT can improve some severe mental health conditions, particularly when symptoms are life-threatening, other treatments have not helped, or a person has previously responded well to ECT. The decision is made with the person whenever possible after discussing expected benefits, risks, and alternatives.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item ECT device and treatment electrodes
  \item EEG/ECG monitor and blood-pressure monitor
  \item anesthesia airway and oxygen equipment with bite block
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
ECT; electroconvulsive treatment. “Electroshock therapy” is an older term and is generally avoided because it can be misleading.

\section*{Principle}
ECT uses a carefully controlled electrical pulse to produce a brief seizure under anesthesia and muscle relaxation. The exact way ECT improves symptoms is not fully understood. Electrode placement, pulse settings, and treatment frequency are chosen to balance the chance of benefit with side effects, including effects on memory.

\section*{History}
Ugo Cerletti and Lucio Bini introduced ECT in 1938. Modern ECT is given under general anesthesia with a muscle-relaxing medicine and continuous monitoring, unlike early forms of the treatment.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
ECT may be considered for severe depression, including depression with psychotic symptoms, when a rapid response is needed (for example, if the person is not eating or drinking), other treatments have not worked, or the person prefers ECT based on a previous good response. It may also be used for catatonia or a prolonged or severe manic episode in selected cases. A high risk of self-harm may make rapid treatment important, but the full clinical situation guides the decision. ECT is not a routine treatment for mild or moderate depression.

\section*{Role in Care}
ECT is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. Whether it is offered early or after other treatments depends on symptoms, urgency, prior response, preferences, medical risks, and local clinical guidance.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
Before treatment, the team confirms the plan and reviews the person's health and consent. An anesthetist gives a short-acting general anesthetic and a muscle relaxant through a vein. The person receives oxygen and is monitored with an electroencephalogram (EEG), electrocardiogram (ECG), blood pressure, and oxygen measurements. Electrodes are placed on one side or both sides of the head, depending on the treatment plan. A brief electrical pulse causes a seizure that is monitored by the team while the muscle relaxant limits body movement. The treatment itself usually lasts only a few minutes; the person then wakes in a recovery area. ECT is often given twice a week during an initial course, though some schedules use three treatments per week. The number and schedule are individualized, and treatment is reviewed throughout.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews medical and anesthesia history, medicines, allergies, prior ECT response, and memory or cognitive concerns. Tell the team about heart or lung conditions, pregnancy, dental problems, implanted devices, or recent illness. Fasting and medicine instructions depend on the anesthesia plan and local protocol. Do not stop psychiatric or other medicines unless the ECT or prescribing team tells you to; some medicines may be adjusted to improve safety or treatment response. Arrange an escort and support after treatment if required by the clinic.

\section*{Contraindications}
There is no single health condition that automatically rules out ECT for every person. The team weighs the risks of anesthesia and ECT against the risks of delaying or not receiving treatment.

\section*{Precautions}
General anesthesia and the brief changes in heart rate and blood pressure require extra assessment for people with significant heart, brain, lung, or other medical conditions. If the person has capacity to decide, the team should explain likely benefits, memory and other risks, and alternatives so they can make an informed, voluntary choice. If the person cannot give consent, specific legal safeguards apply and vary by jurisdiction; the team should explain which rules apply and involve the person as much as possible.

\section*{Risks}
Common short-term effects include headache, muscle soreness, nausea, tiredness, and confusion on waking. Memory and thinking can be affected: people may have trouble forming new memories around the treatment period or lose some personal memories from before treatment. These effects often improve after the course, but memory gaps can sometimes last longer or remain. Memory effects are more likely with some treatment approaches, including placing electrodes on both sides of the head rather than one side. General anesthesia and temporary changes in heart rate or blood pressure carry additional risks. The likelihood and degree of side effects vary with the person and the treatment settings; the team should discuss and monitor them during the course.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The person is monitored in a recovery area while the effects of anesthesia wear off. A short period of confusion, headache, nausea, or muscle aches may occur. The care team checks that the person is awake and medically stable before discharge or return to the ward. After outpatient treatment, follow instructions about eating, medicines, driving, work, and having someone accompany you. Do not drive or make important decisions until the anesthesia team says it is safe.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The team checks symptoms, daily function, safety, and memory during the treatment course. Some people improve after several sessions; others may need a different plan or may not respond. ECT is stopped when the intended improvement is reached or when risks or side effects outweigh likely benefit. Improvement does not prevent symptoms from returning, so ongoing treatment planning is important.

\section*{Expected Results}
ECT can lead to substantial improvement in some severe conditions, sometimes more quickly than other treatments. The response is individual, and no result is guaranteed. The care team discusses progress and side effects with the person throughout the course.

\section*{Follow-up}
After an acute course, ongoing care may include antidepressant or mood-stabilizing medicine, psychological treatment, and regular mental health follow-up. Some people benefit from continuation or maintenance ECT at intervals. The plan is tailored to symptoms, prior response, side effects, and preferences.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
After discharge, follow the ECT team's instructions and contact them for confusion that is severe or not improving, a significant memory concern, persistent vomiting, severe headache, or any other worrying effect. Seek emergency care for chest pain, difficulty breathing, fainting, a seizure after returning home, or a serious injury. If mental health symptoms worsen or there is immediate risk of self-harm, contact the treating team or local emergency service right away.
