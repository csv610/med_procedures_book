\chapter{Dermabrasion}

\section*{Overview}
Dermabrasion is a medical skin-resurfacing procedure. A clinician uses a rapidly moving abrasive instrument to remove controlled layers of skin. The treated area heals with new surface skin. Dermabrasion is more invasive than microdermabrasion, which removes only the outermost skin layer and usually does not create an open wound. A dermatologist assesses the skin and discusses expected benefits, risks, and alternatives before treatment.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Mechanical dermabrasion; surgical skin resurfacing. Microdermabrasion is a separate, much more superficial procedure.

\section*{Principle}
An abrasive wheel or brush removes the surface of the skin to a planned depth. The wound then heals as new skin grows from the edges and remaining skin structures. The depth and area treated affect the result, healing time, and risk of scarring or pigment changes.

\section*{History}
Dermabrasion was developed in the early 20th century and became more widely used for acne scars after World War II. It remains an option for selected problems, although other treatments, including lasers and less invasive procedures, may be preferred depending on the skin and treatment goal.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Dermabrasion may be used to soften selected acne scars, surgical or traumatic scars, certain wrinkles, and some sun-damaged areas or skin growths. A dermatologist first checks that a spot is not suspicious for skin cancer; changing, bleeding, or unusual spots may need examination or biopsy instead. Dermabrasion is not suitable for every scar or skin type and may not remove a problem completely.

\section*{Role in Care}
It is a therapeutic skin-resurfacing procedure, not a diagnostic test. The clinician examines the skin and may recommend another treatment if the diagnosis is uncertain or if another approach is safer or more effective.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The treatment area is cleaned and numbed, usually with local anesthetic; freezing spray or other methods may also be used. A motorized abrasive instrument is moved across the skin to remove the planned surface layers. The clinician may treat a small area or a larger area, depending on the goal. The treated skin is then covered with an ointment or dressing, or managed with a specific open-wound care plan. The setting and anesthesia depend on the size and depth of treatment.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the dermatologist about medicines, allergies, cold sores, skin conditions, past scarring, and any tendency to develop dark marks after skin injury. Mention current or recent isotretinoin use. Current acne guidance advises against full-face dermabrasion and rotary mechanical dermabrasion during isotretinoin treatment and within six months after treatment. The safety and timing of other procedures depend on their type and depth, so discuss the specific plan with the dermatologist. Do not stop medicine unless the prescribing clinician advises you to. The clinician may prescribe antiviral medicine for people at risk of herpes outbreaks, especially when the area around the mouth is treated.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Treatment may be postponed or avoided with an active skin infection, cold sore in the treatment area, uncontrolled inflammatory skin disease, or a health condition that interferes with healing. A personal tendency to keloids or raised scars, recent isotretinoin use, prior procedures in the area, and darker skin tones require careful individualized assessment because scarring or lasting light or dark patches may occur. The clinician should discuss alternatives and likely results for the person's skin.

\section*{Risks}
Expected short-term effects include redness, swelling, tenderness, crusting, and temporary sensitivity. Complications can include infection, herpes flare, acne-like bumps or milia, prolonged redness, changes in skin color, uneven texture, and scarring. Deeper or larger treatments generally need longer healing and can carry greater risks. Sun exposure during healing can worsen discoloration.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the clinician's wound-care instructions closely. This may include gently cleansing the area, applying an ointment or changing a dressing, and avoiding products that irritate healing skin. Do not pick crusts. New surface skin often forms in about 7 to 10 days, but deeper or larger treatment areas may take longer, and redness can last for weeks. Avoid sun exposure while the skin heals; after it has closed, use sun protection as directed. Attend planned follow-up visits so the clinician can check for infection, delayed healing, or pigment changes.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Improvement becomes clearer as the skin heals and remodels over weeks to months. Results vary; complete removal of scars or wrinkles is not guaranteed, and some people need another treatment or choose a different approach. The clinician monitors healing and discusses further treatment only after the skin has recovered.

\section*{Expected Results}
The aim is to make selected scars or uneven areas less noticeable and improve skin texture. The treated skin may look different in color or texture from surrounding skin, and the final appearance depends on the original condition, treatment depth, skin type, and healing.

\section*{Follow-up}
Keep all wound checks and follow-up appointments. Contact the treating clinician if healing is slower than expected or a new treatment is being considered before recovery is complete. Protect healed skin from the sun to help reduce the risk of pigment changes.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the treating clinician promptly for increasing pain, spreading redness or warmth, pus, fever, new blisters or cold sores, or delayed wound healing. Seek urgent medical care for rapidly spreading swelling, severe illness, or trouble breathing.
