\chapter{Mechanical Ventilation}

\section*{Overview}
Mechanical ventilation is life-support treatment that helps move air into and out of the lungs when a person cannot breathe adequately on their own. This chapter focuses on invasive ventilation, which connects a ventilator to a breathing tube in the windpipe or to a tracheostomy tube. Noninvasive ventilation delivers support through a fitted mask and is covered separately. Ventilation supports breathing while clinicians treat the underlying problem; it does not cure that problem by itself.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item ventilator, breathing circuit, and humidifier
  \item endotracheal or tracheostomy tube and suction catheter
  \item oxygen, pressure, and gas-exchange monitors
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Invasive mechanical ventilation, ventilator support, breathing-machine support

\section*{Principle}
The ventilator delivers a controlled mixture of oxygen and air under pressure and helps remove carbon dioxide. Clinicians choose settings such as breath size, breathing rate, oxygen concentration, and pressure to match the person's condition. Some modes deliver most or all breaths; others assist breaths the patient starts. For acute respiratory distress syndrome, guidelines recommend lower breath volumes and limits on airway pressure to reduce additional lung injury. The ventilator and settings are adjusted in response to examination, oxygen and carbon dioxide levels, and the person's comfort and lung mechanics.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Ventilation may be used for severe breathing failure, when a person cannot protect their airway, during some operations under general anesthesia, or in other critical situations. It can support oxygen levels, remove carbon dioxide, reduce the work of breathing, and provide time for treatment or recovery. The reason and expected goals should be explained to the patient or, when needed, their representative.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Mechanical ventilation is supportive treatment, not a diagnostic test. It may be life-saving but also involves burdens and risks. The care team should discuss the likely benefits, risks, alternatives, and how the treatment fits the person's goals and preferences. A do-not-resuscitate (DNR) order addresses cardiopulmonary resuscitation and does not by itself mean that a person has declined ventilation; a do-not-intubate order or other treatment preferences should be considered separately.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
For invasive ventilation, a clinician places a breathing tube through the mouth (or, less often, the nose) into the windpipe, usually after giving medicines for the procedure. In some people who need longer-term support, a tracheostomy tube is used instead. The tube is connected to the ventilator, and its position is checked and secured. Staff provide humidification, suction secretions when needed, and adjust the ventilator to the patient's response. Sedative and pain-relief medicines may be used, but the amount depends on the person's condition and comfort.

\section*{Preparation}
In an emergency there may be little time for preparation. When possible, the team reviews the cause of breathing failure, medical history, allergies, medicines, prior wishes, and advance directives. The clinician explains why a breathing tube is recommended and what other options are appropriate. If the person cannot communicate, the team works with the legally appropriate decision-maker and follows known preferences and local law. For planned surgery, the anesthesia team discusses airway management and recovery from ventilation.

\section*{Contraindications}
There is no single medical condition that automatically rules out ventilation.

\section*{Precautions}
A person's decision to decline intubation should be respected. Whether it is offered depends on the expected benefit, the cause and reversibility of illness, the person's goals, and available alternatives. Some problems, such as a pneumothorax, may need prompt treatment alongside ventilation because positive pressure can worsen an untreated air leak; this requires urgent clinical judgment rather than a blanket rule. Patients with a DNR order may still receive ventilation if consistent with their separate preferences and care plan.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
Possible complications include ventilator-associated pneumonia, injury to the lungs from pressure or overexpansion, low blood pressure, and damage or irritation to the mouth, vocal cords, windpipe, or lungs from the breathing tube. Sedating medicines can cause sleepiness or delirium and may contribute to weakness, especially during a prolonged intensive-care stay. The tube can make speaking and eating by mouth difficult while it is in place. Risk depends on the illness, length of ventilation, and treatment required.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Ventilated patients are cared for by an intensive-care team. Staff repeatedly assess comfort, consciousness, oxygenation, carbon dioxide removal, blood pressure, secretions, tube position, and ventilator settings. Care may include mouth care, repositioning, nutrition, infection prevention, and movement or rehabilitation as the patient can tolerate. Sedation is managed to provide comfort and safety while avoiding unnecessary deep sedation when appropriate. The plan is reviewed regularly with the patient or decision-maker.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The goal may be to support the patient until the lungs or other illness improve, to provide support during anesthesia, or to provide longer-term breathing assistance. Readiness to reduce support is assessed regularly. When appropriate, a spontaneous breathing trial checks whether the patient can breathe with less help; passing it is one part of deciding whether the breathing tube can be removed. The team also considers alertness, airway protection, cough, and ability to manage secretions. Some people need a gradual process, a tracheostomy, noninvasive support after extubation, or ongoing ventilation.

\section*{Expected Results}
Ventilator settings and targets are individualized. Oxygen level, carbon dioxide level, comfort, lung protection, and the underlying illness are considered together; one blood-gas value or target does not fit every patient. Ventilation may improve gas exchange while the cause is treated, but recovery and the length of support cannot be predicted exactly.

\section*{Follow-up}
The care team explains progress, test results, and changes to the treatment plan. After the breathing tube is removed, some people have temporary hoarseness, sore throat, swallowing difficulty, weakness, or confusion and may need respiratory therapy, speech and swallowing assessment, or rehabilitation. Follow-up depends on the illness and duration of ventilation.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Mechanical ventilation is delivered in a monitored clinical setting. Patients, family members, and visitors should alert staff immediately if the breathing tube becomes loose, disconnected, or appears displaced; if the patient has sudden trouble breathing, blue or gray color, new severe agitation or unresponsiveness, or a sudden change in the ventilator alarm; or if there is bleeding, swelling, or fluid around the tube. Do not silence alarms or adjust the ventilator unless trained staff instruct you to do so.
