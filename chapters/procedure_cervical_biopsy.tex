\chapter{Cervical Biopsy}

\section*{Overview}
A cervical biopsy removes a small sample of tissue from the cervix, the lower part of the uterus that opens into the vagina. The sample is examined under a microscope for abnormal or cancerous cells. A biopsy is often taken during colposcopy, an examination using a magnifying instrument. An abnormal screening result does not by itself mean that a person has cancer.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item speculum and colposcope
  \item biopsy forceps or endocervical curette
  \item local hemostatic supplies and specimen container
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Colposcopy-directed biopsy; cervical punch biopsy. Endocervical sampling is a separate way to collect cells or tissue from inside the cervical canal. LEEP and cone biopsy are excisional procedures that remove a larger area and are not synonyms for a small punch biopsy.

\section*{Principle}
The clinician uses magnification and, often, a mild vinegar-like solution to identify areas that may need sampling. Small tissue pieces are taken from selected areas and sent to a laboratory. A pathologist examines the tissue and reports whether abnormal cells are present and, if so, their type and grade.

\section*{History}
Colposcopy became an important way to examine the cervix after Hans Hinselmann introduced the instrument in the 1920s. The methods used to sample cervical tissue have developed alongside cervical screening.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A biopsy may be recommended after an abnormal cervical screening result, when a clinician sees an area that needs closer examination, or when symptoms such as unexplained bleeding need investigation. Screening results and medical history help determine whether colposcopy, biopsy, or follow-up testing is appropriate.

\section*{Role in Care}
This is a diagnostic procedure, usually done after screening or examination raises a concern. A biopsy can identify changes in the sampled tissue, but it does not replace routine screening or evaluate every cause of bleeding or pelvic symptoms.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The patient lies on an examination table, and a speculum is placed in the vagina so the cervix can be seen. The clinician looks through the colposcope, which remains outside the body, and may apply a solution to highlight areas. Small samples may be taken with biopsy forceps; this can cause a brief pinch or cramp. If needed, a separate sample may be taken from the cervical canal. Pressure or a medicine may be used to reduce bleeding. LEEP or cone biopsy removes more tissue and involves a different procedure and recovery.

\section*{Preparation}
Usually no special preparation is needed. Tell the clinician if you may be pregnant, take blood-thinning medicine, have a bleeding problem, have an allergy to medicines used to stop bleeding, or have had cervical treatment before. Do not stop a prescribed medicine unless the treating clinician tells you to. Ask about pain-relief options and whether you may bring a support person. The appointment may be rescheduled if heavy menstrual bleeding would make the examination difficult, but menstruation is not always a reason to cancel.

\section*{Contraindications}
Endocervical curettage (scraping tissue from inside the cervical canal) is not done during pregnancy, and treatment of precancerous changes is generally deferred until after birth unless cancer is suspected.

\section*{Precautions}
Pregnancy-related changes can make the cervix harder to interpret, so an experienced colposcopist is preferred. The clinician considers pregnancy, active infection, bleeding risk, and prior cervical procedures when planning sampling. A colposcopy-directed biopsy may be performed during pregnancy when needed to assess a concerning area; available evidence indicates that it is safe and is not associated with adverse obstetric outcomes. The clinician explains the reason for sampling and any alternatives.

\section*{Risks}
Mild cramping, spotting, or dark discharge can occur for several days. Less common risks include heavier bleeding, infection, or injury to the cervix. These risks differ for a small punch biopsy and for procedures such as LEEP or cone biopsy. Removing a larger amount of cervical tissue can increase the risk of cervical narrowing and may affect a future pregnancy; those risks are discussed separately before an excisional procedure.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people return to usual light activities soon after a punch biopsy. Follow the clinic's instructions about sex, tampons, menstrual cups, and swimming; temporary restrictions may be advised while the cervix heals or if bleeding continues. Use only recommended pain relief. A small amount of spotting or dark discharge may be expected. After LEEP or cone biopsy, bleeding and restrictions may last longer, and separate instructions apply.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The pathology result may be normal, show a precancerous change, or show cancer. The clinician explains the result and the next step, which may be routine screening, repeat HPV or cell testing, further colposcopy, or treatment. A small biopsy samples only selected areas, so follow-up depends on the result, screening history, and whether the examination was complete. If precancer is treated, longer-term surveillance is important because abnormal cells can recur.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the clinic promptly for bleeding heavier than a usual period, such as soaking more than one sanitary pad in an hour, bleeding that is increasing or will not slow, foul-smelling discharge, fever, or pain that is severe or worsening. Seek emergency care for very heavy bleeding, fainting, or severe weakness.
