\chapter{Cardiac Catheterization}

\section*{Overview}
Cardiac catheterization uses a thin flexible tube (catheter) passed through a blood vessel to the heart. It can help diagnose heart and blood-vessel problems, measure pressures, or guide treatment. The exact procedure depends on the question being answered. This chapter describes common diagnostic catheterization; angioplasty, stenting, valve procedures, and other treatments may be performed during the same visit but add their own steps and risks.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Heart catheterization; cardiac cath; coronary angiography (when the coronary arteries are imaged); right-heart catheterization; left-heart catheterization.

\section*{Principle}
X-ray imaging guides the catheter through blood vessels to the heart. Depending on the procedure, clinicians can measure pressure and oxygen levels in heart chambers, assess valves and blood flow, take a small tissue sample, or inject contrast material (a liquid that shows up on X-rays) to show the coronary arteries or heart chambers. If a narrowed coronary artery is found, balloon angioplasty or stenting may sometimes be performed during the same procedure.

\section*{History}
Werner Forssmann performed an early human heart catheterization in 1929. André Cournand and Dickinson Richards developed its clinical use, and they shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Forssmann.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Cardiac catheterization may be used to investigate possible coronary artery disease, heart valve problems, heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, congenital heart disease, or unexplained symptoms. It can measure pressures or oxygen levels, check how well the heart pumps, image coronary arteries, or obtain a heart tissue sample. The care team chooses it when the information is likely to guide diagnosis or treatment.

\section*{Role in Care}
This is an invasive diagnostic procedure and may also guide treatment. It is often done after other tests, but urgent catheterization may be used when a heart attack or another serious condition is suspected. The benefits and alternatives depend on the indication.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The patient lies on an X-ray table and is connected to heart and blood-pressure monitors. The clinician numbs the skin with local anesthetic; medicine to relax the patient may also be given. A small tube called a sheath is placed into a blood vessel, and the catheter is guided to the heart under X-ray imaging.

For coronary angiography or many left-heart procedures, access is usually through an artery in the wrist or groin. Contrast material is injected to show the coronary arteries or heart chambers. For right-heart catheterization, access is through a vein, often in the neck or groin, and the catheter is guided through the right side of the heart into the pulmonary artery to measure pressures and blood flow. Some procedures use both routes or additional tools. The catheter and sheath are removed when the procedure is complete, and pressure or a closure device is used to stop bleeding.

\section*{Preparation}
Follow the facility's instructions about fasting and medicines; these differ by the planned procedure and whether sedation is used. Tell the team about all medicines, especially anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs, diabetes medicines, and medicines for heart failure; do not stop or change them unless the treating team provides a plan. Also report kidney disease, bleeding problems, allergies or a prior reaction to contrast material, and possible pregnancy.

The team may review blood tests, an ECG, and other heart imaging. If contrast will be used, kidney function and hydration needs may be considered. Do not increase fluids without advice if you have heart failure, kidney disease, or a fluid restriction.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
The decision is individualized and depends on urgency and the information needed. Active infection at a proposed access site, a serious bleeding problem, or an unstable medical condition may require treatment first or a change in plan. Severe kidney disease, a prior severe contrast reaction, or pregnancy may require additional precautions or a different approach. These factors do not automatically rule out or justify delaying catheterization when it is clinically needed.

\section*{Risks}
Bruising, soreness, and minor bleeding at the access site are common. Less common complications include significant bleeding, a blood clot or blockage in the accessed vessel, abnormal heart rhythm, infection, or damage to the heart, valve, or blood vessel. Rare serious complications include heart attack, stroke, a tear in a coronary artery or heart chamber, or a need for emergency treatment.

Contrast material can cause an allergic-like reaction. Kidney function can worsen around the time of catheterization, especially in people with kidney disease, heart failure, or an unstable illness. This is called contrast-associated acute kidney injury; the procedure, illness, blood pressure changes, and contrast may all contribute, so contrast is not always the sole cause. The team considers kidney function, contrast amount, and whether fluids or other precautions are appropriate. X-ray exposure is kept as low as practical, although prolonged or repeated exposure can rarely injure the skin. The risks also depend on whether angioplasty, stenting, biopsy, or another intervention is performed.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The patient is monitored while the access site seals and heart rate and blood pressure are checked. Recovery differs by access route and procedure. After wrist access, a compression band may be used and the patient can often sit up sooner. After groin access, the patient may need to lie flat and keep the leg straight for a period. The team determines when it is safe to stand and go home.

Follow the discharge instructions for wound care, activity, bathing, and medicines. Avoid strenuous activity and heavy lifting for the period advised. Drink fluids as directed if contrast was used and if there is no fluid restriction. An overnight stay may be needed depending on the findings, treatment, and health status.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The clinician may explain the initial findings during or soon after the procedure. The results can include coronary artery images, heart pressures, oxygen measurements, valve assessment, or tissue results. The next step may be medicines, further testing, angioplasty and stenting, heart surgery, or no additional procedure. A finding does not determine treatment by itself; the cardiologist considers symptoms, other tests, and the patient's preferences.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
If the access site bleeds, apply firm, continuous pressure directly over it and follow the discharge instructions; lie flat if the site is in the groin. Call emergency services if bleeding is heavy or does not stop with firm pressure. Seek emergency care for severe lower-abdominal or back pain after groin access, which can signal internal bleeding, or for chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting, or new stroke symptoms. Seek urgent care for a rapidly enlarging lump or a cold, pale, numb, or weak hand or leg. Contact the care team for increasing redness, warmth, swelling, drainage, fever, or worsening pain at the access site.
