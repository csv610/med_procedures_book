\chapter{Epidural Blood Patch}

\section*{Overview}
An epidural blood patch (EBP) is a procedure in which a clinician injects a person's own blood into the epidural space around the spinal cord. It is most often used to treat a post-dural-puncture headache (PDPH), which can happen after a spinal tap, spinal anesthetic, or an accidental puncture during an epidural. A blood patch can also be used in specialist care for some spontaneous cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks. It often helps, but does not always provide complete or lasting relief.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Epidural blood patch; autologous epidural blood patch; EBP.

\section*{Principle}
The injected blood may form a clot over a small hole in the dura, the membrane around the spinal fluid, and may also temporarily raise pressure around the brain. This can reduce CSF leakage and relieve the headache. The exact mechanism is not fully understood, and symptoms may recur if the leak continues.

\section*{History}
Epidural blood patching was introduced in the mid-20th century and became an established treatment for persistent or disabling headache after a dural puncture. Its use and technique have since been adapted for different causes of spinal CSF leakage.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
An EBP may be offered for a headache that began after a known or suspected dural puncture, especially when it is severe, persists despite initial treatment, or interferes with daily activities. A typical PDPH is worse when upright and improves when lying down, but clinicians assess for other causes and warning signs before treatment. A blood patch may also be considered for spontaneous intracranial hypotension caused by a spinal CSF leak; this requires a specialist plan and may involve imaging or targeted treatment.

\section*{Role in Care}
An EBP is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. Initial management of PDPH may include usual fluids, pain medicines, and sometimes short-term caffeine. Lying flat may ease symptoms temporarily but routine prolonged bed rest does not prevent PDPH and is not a substitute for assessment. A blood patch is considered when symptoms are not improving or are limiting daily activities. Imaging or additional evaluation may be needed when the headache is unusual, changes, or does not respond as expected.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is performed by a clinician trained in epidural techniques, using sterile precautions. A small amount of blood is drawn from a vein and injected through an epidural needle into the space around the spinal cord. For a typical PDPH, the injection is often made at the same spinal level as the puncture or one level below. The blood is injected slowly; the clinician stops if the person develops significant back pressure, pain, or leg symptoms. For PDPH, 15 to 20 mL is commonly used when tolerated, but the amount and approach vary, especially for spontaneous CSF leaks. Imaging guidance may be used when anatomy or the leak location makes it helpful.

\section*{Preparation}
The clinician reviews the headache, previous procedures, medicines, allergies, and risks, and explains alternatives and possible outcomes. Tell the team if you take anticoagulants or antiplatelet medicines, have a bleeding disorder, fever, infection, or new neurologic symptoms. Medicines that affect blood clotting may need a carefully timed plan; do not stop them without medical instructions. The procedure may be postponed if infection or an unsafe bleeding risk is present.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
An infection at the injection site, bloodstream infection, or a significant untreated bleeding risk generally prevents an elective patch until assessed or treated. Anticoagulant use, low platelet count, altered spinal anatomy, and certain neurologic symptoms require individualized assessment. If the headache is not typical, has changed, or includes neurologic signs, the clinician may investigate other causes before doing a patch.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary back soreness or pressure during injection is common. The headache may not improve or may return, and a second patch may be considered after reassessment. Less common risks include another dural puncture, infection, bleeding around the spinal cord, nerve irritation or injury, and temporary leg discomfort. Rarely, symptoms can change because pressure becomes too high after sealing a leak. Serious complications are uncommon but need urgent evaluation.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The care team observes the patient for a period after the procedure and provides instructions about activity and follow-up. Some centers advise a brief period lying down, but routine prolonged bed rest is not established as necessary. Mild back soreness can occur for a few days. Arrange a ride home if sedation or other medicines affect alertness. Headache relief may begin quickly, but the response can be partial, delayed, or temporary.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The aim is meaningful improvement in headache and the ability to sit or stand more comfortably. One patch does not guarantee full or lasting relief. The treating team should arrange follow-up until symptoms resolve or another plan is made. If relief is incomplete or the headache returns, the clinician reassesses the diagnosis and may consider another patch, imaging, or consultation with neurology or another specialist.

\section*{Expected Results}
Many people improve after a blood patch, but complete relief varies. Symptoms may recur, and the original leak may need further evaluation or treatment. Recovery depends on the cause and size of the leak and the person's overall condition.

\section*{Follow-up}
Keep the follow-up appointment and contact the treating team if the headache persists, returns, or changes. A second patch may be appropriate for persistent typical PDPH after other causes have been considered. Repeated unsuccessful patches or symptoms suggesting spontaneous CSF leak call for specialist evaluation rather than assuming that another routine patch is the answer.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent medical assessment for a severe or changing headache, new weakness or numbness, difficulty walking, confusion, seizure, vision changes, fever, worsening back pain, or loss of bladder or bowel control. Contact the treating team if the original headache returns or does not improve as expected. These symptoms may need evaluation for causes other than a persistent CSF leak.
