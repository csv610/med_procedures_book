\chapter{Lithotripsy}

\section*{Overview}
This chapter describes extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL or SWL), a treatment that uses focused sound waves from outside the body to break kidney or ureteral stones into smaller pieces. The fragments may then pass in the urine. Other procedures also use the word lithotripsy, including laser treatment during ureteroscopy; those are different procedures. SWL does not require an incision, but it may not remove every fragment or avoid the need for another treatment.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item shock-wave lithotripter and coupling cushion/gel
  \item ultrasound or X-ray targeting system
  \item ECG and vital-signs monitor when indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy, shock wave lithotripsy, ESWL, SWL

\section*{Principle}
Imaging is used to locate the stone. A machine sends focused shock waves through the skin and surrounding tissue to the stone, breaking it into fragments. The body then passes the fragments through the urinary tract. The degree of fragmentation and passage varies.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
SWL may be offered for selected kidney or ureteral stones when treatment is needed. The choice depends on stone size, location, composition and density, the anatomy of the urinary tract, body habitus, symptoms, kidney function, and patient preference. Small stones that are likely to pass may be observed; larger or difficult stones may be better treated with ureteroscopy or percutaneous nephrolithotomy. SWL is not the right treatment for every stone.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
SWL is a non-incisional treatment option for some stones, not a diagnostic test. Imaging and clinical assessment are used to decide whether a stone needs treatment and which method is most suitable. The clinician should discuss the chance of needing repeat SWL or another procedure.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
Treatment is usually performed in a hospital or outpatient unit. The patient lies on a treatment table, often with part of the body positioned against a water cushion or gel pad. X-ray imaging or ultrasound guides the shock waves toward the stone. Pain medicine is given; sedation or anesthesia may be used depending on the patient, equipment, and local practice. The treatment often takes about an hour, but duration varies. No skin incision is made.

\section*{Preparation}
The team may arrange urine testing or a culture and imaging before treatment. Follow the facility's instructions about food and drink, since these depend on whether sedation or anesthesia is planned. Tell the urology team about pregnancy or possible pregnancy, infection symptoms, allergies, bleeding problems, and all medicines, especially blood thinners and antiplatelet drugs. Do not stop or restart these medicines without a specific plan from the prescribing and procedural teams. Ask whether a ride home is needed.

\section*{Contraindications}
SWL is generally avoided during pregnancy and when there is an uncontrolled urinary infection. Uncorrected bleeding risk, an aneurysm near the stone, blockage below the stone, or body shape or size that prevents accurate targeting may make SWL unsafe or ineffective.

\section*{Precautions}
Uncontrolled high blood pressure and some heart devices may also require treatment or special planning. A clinician should review individual risks; another treatment may be preferred.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
Temporary blood in the urine, bruising or soreness where the shock waves entered, and cramping as fragments pass can occur. Fragments may block the ureter (steinstrasse), causing pain or obstruction; some people need another procedure. Other uncommon risks include infection, kidney or nearby-organ injury, and bleeding around the kidney. SWL may fail to break the stone sufficiently, or fragments may remain or grow later. The risks depend on the stone and the person's health.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people return home the same day. Follow the team's instructions about pain relief, activity, and fluid intake. Drink enough to avoid dehydration unless a clinician has restricted fluids for another health reason; forcing excessive water is not necessary. Straining urine may help collect fragments for analysis if the team requests it. An alpha-blocker or other medicine should be taken only if prescribed. Some blood in the urine and discomfort while fragments pass may occur for a short time.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Imaging at a time chosen by the urology team checks whether the stone has broken up and whether fragments remain or are blocking urine flow. A follow-up interval is not the same for everyone. A stone may need another SWL session, ureteroscopy, or another procedure. Any recovered fragment may be analyzed, and the care team may recommend prevention measures based on stone type and risk factors.

\section*{Expected Results}
The intended result is sufficient fragmentation for the pieces to pass without causing obstruction or infection. Complete clearance is not guaranteed, and treatment may need to be repeated or changed.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend the planned imaging and urology follow-up even if pain improves. Discuss stone analysis, prevention, and any remaining fragments with the care team. If you have a ureteral stent, follow the specific plan for its removal or exchange.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent medical advice for fever or chills, worsening or uncontrolled flank or abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, inability to urinate, or heavy or persistent blood in the urine. A stone blocking urine flow with infection or very little/no urine is an emergency. Call emergency services for fainting, confusion, severe weakness, or rapidly worsening symptoms.
