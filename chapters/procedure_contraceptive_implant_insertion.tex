\chapter{Contraceptive Implant Insertion}

\section*{Overview}
A contraceptive implant is a small, flexible rod placed just under the skin of the upper arm. The single-rod etonogestrel implant (Nexplanon) prevents pregnancy for up to 5 years under current U.S. labeling; approved duration varies by product and country. It can be removed at any time by a trained clinician, and fertility usually returns quickly after removal. The implant does not protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs).

\section*{Alternative Names}
Etonogestrel implant; Nexplanon; subdermal contraceptive implant.

\section*{Principle}
The implant releases a progestin hormone, mainly preventing ovulation and thickening cervical mucus. It is highly effective and does not require daily action. Bleeding may become irregular, lighter, more frequent, or stop; this pattern does not by itself show whether the implant is working.

\section*{History}
The single-rod etonogestrel implant followed earlier multi-rod contraceptive implants. The current radiopaque version can be seen on X-ray, which helps clinicians locate it if it cannot be felt. In the United States, its approved duration was extended to 5 years in 2026.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
People may choose the implant for highly effective, long-acting, reversible pregnancy prevention. It may be suitable for people who cannot or prefer not to use estrogen-containing contraception. Some users have lighter bleeding or no periods, while others have unpredictable bleeding. It is not a reliable treatment for heavy menstrual bleeding for everyone.

\section*{Role in Care}
Insertion is a minor procedure to start a contraceptive method. Removal is a separate minor procedure and can be requested whenever the patient wants to stop or change methods.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
After reviewing consent and checking the planned arm, the clinician cleans the skin and injects local anesthetic. A preloaded applicator places one rod just under the skin of the usually nondominant upper arm, over the triceps muscle and behind the groove between the upper-arm muscles. This location helps avoid nearby nerves and blood vessels. The clinician and patient feel for the implant immediately afterward. A pressure bandage and a smaller adhesive bandage are applied. In the United States, NEXPLANON insertion and removal must be performed by a clinician trained and certified through the product's restricted safety program. The visit usually takes only a few minutes.

\section*{Preparation}
The clinician asks about pregnancy possibility, current contraception, medicines and supplements, bleeding patterns, allergies, breast cancer, liver disease, and prior blood clots. A pregnancy test is not required for every patient if the clinician can be reasonably certain the patient is not pregnant; testing may be used based on the situation. The implant can be started at different times, including when switching from another method, with backup contraception or follow-up testing when needed. In general, if it is inserted more than 5 days after menstrual bleeding began, use condoms or avoid sex for the next 7 days unless the clinician gives different instructions. Medicines such as rifampin, some seizure medicines, some HIV medicines, and St. John's wort can reduce effectiveness; review all medicines with the clinician.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Contraindications depend on the product label and local medical eligibility guidance. U.S. labeling lists pregnancy, current or past blood clots, active liver disease or liver tumors, unexplained uterine bleeding, current or past breast cancer or another progestin-sensitive cancer, and allergy to an implant component. The clinician also checks for medicines that can reduce effectiveness and discusses other methods if needed. The implant is not appropriate protection from STIs.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary pain, bruising, swelling, or a small scar at the insertion site can occur. Irregular bleeding is common; other possible effects include headache, acne, breast tenderness, weight change, or mood changes. Infection, numbness, injury to nearby nerves or blood vessels, a bent or broken implant, or deep placement that makes removal difficult are uncommon. Rarely, a deeply inserted implant can move into a blood vessel. Insertion and removal should be done by a trained clinician.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Keep the insertion site clean and dry. The pressure bandage is usually removed after 24 hours; keep the smaller bandage in place for 3 to 5 days or as instructed. Mild tenderness or bruising should improve. If the implant was not placed within the first 5 days of menstrual bleeding, use backup contraception for 7 days unless the clinician gives different instructions. If you cannot feel the rod after insertion, contact the clinic promptly and use backup contraception until its presence is confirmed.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The implant's presence should be confirmed by touch immediately after insertion. If it cannot be felt or its location is uncertain, contact the clinic promptly and use a non-hormonal backup method until its presence is confirmed. Imaging may be needed to find it. The implant must not be removed until its exact location has been confirmed; deeply placed or migrated implants may need specialist removal. Routine follow-up is not required for everyone, but contact the clinic if you have concerns, troublesome bleeding, or side effects. The implant can be removed at any time. Under current U.S. labeling, it must be removed by the end of the fifth year; local product instructions may differ. Fertility can return soon after removal, so arrange another method promptly if pregnancy is not desired.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the clinician for increasing redness, warmth, drainage, swelling, or pain at the insertion site; new numbness or weakness in the arm; troublesome bleeding; or possible pregnancy. A positive pregnancy test or lower abdominal pain with possible pregnancy needs prompt medical assessment, because pregnancy with an implant can rarely be ectopic. Get emergency medical care for severe chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, coughing blood, or one-sided leg swelling or pain. If the implant cannot be felt, contact the inserting clinic promptly for help locating it.
