\chapter{Gastric Emptying Study}

\section*{Overview}
A gastric emptying study measures how quickly food leaves the stomach. In the most common type, you eat a small meal containing a tiny amount of radioactive tracer, and a special camera takes pictures over time. Other protocols use a liquid meal. The study helps assess stomach movement; it does not by itself explain the cause of symptoms.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item gamma camera and imaging workstation
  \item standardized test meal containing a radiotracer
  \item timing and patient-positioning equipment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Gastric emptying scan, gastric emptying scintigraphy, gastric emptying test

\section*{Why It Is Done}
The test may be used when symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, feeling full soon after starting a meal, bloating, or upper abdominal discomfort suggest that the stomach empties too slowly or too quickly. It is often used to evaluate suspected gastroparesis, in which the stomach empties slowly without a blockage. Other tests may be needed to check for a physical blockage or another cause of symptoms.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Gastric emptying scintigraphy is the standard test for measuring how a solid meal moves through the stomach when gastroparesis is suspected. It is usually ordered after a clinician reviews symptoms and considers other possible causes, including a physical blockage. The meal and imaging schedule vary with the clinical question, age, and ability to eat the standard meal.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
For the standard adult solid-meal study, the imaging center provides a low-fat meal, commonly egg whites, toast, jam, and water, with a small amount of radioactive tracer. You are usually asked to finish it within about 10 minutes. Tell the team if you have a food allergy or cannot eat or finish the meal; they may use an alternative meal or protocol with different reference ranges. A camera takes pictures after the meal, usually right away and at set times through four hours. You may sit or stand between images, but follow instructions about eating, drinking, smoking, and activity. Some studies use a liquid meal or a different schedule, especially for children or people unable to eat the standard meal.

\section*{Preparation}
Follow the imaging center's fasting instructions; adults are commonly asked to avoid food and drink, and tube feeding, for about four to six hours before the test. Tell the team about all medicines, supplements, cannabis use, diabetes, and recent blood-glucose readings. Medicines that speed or slow stomach emptying can affect the result. Examples include opioid pain medicines, some medicines with anticholinergic effects, medicines that speed stomach movement, and GLP-1 medicines used for diabetes or weight management, such as semaglutide. The ordering clinician may ask you to pause certain medicines, often for about 48 hours, and others for longer. The appropriate interval depends on the medicine, how long it stays in the body, and your health; long-acting medicines may affect the test for longer than 48 hours. Do not stop medicines or change insulin on your own. High blood sugar can slow stomach emptying, so the team may check your glucose and adjust timing or reschedule if needed. Tell them if you may be pregnant or are breastfeeding so they can advise you about the tracer and any temporary precautions.

\section*{Contraindications}
No specific absolute contraindication is identified here; the treating team assesses suitability for the individual.

\section*{Precautions}
There is no single preparation plan for everyone. Pregnancy, diabetes, medicines that affect stomach movement, recent stomach or esophageal surgery, and inability to eat or keep down the test meal may change how the study is done or interpreted. The team may select a liquid or alternative meal, use another test, or reschedule. Tell staff if you vomit or cannot finish the meal, since this can affect the result.

\section*{Risks and Limitations}
The test uses a small amount of radioactive tracer, so radiation exposure is low. The main limitations are that the result can be affected by the meal, incomplete meal consumption, vomiting, blood sugar, medicines, and the timing or length of imaging. Results are most useful when a standardized meal and schedule are used and the patient's preparation is documented.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people can return to usual activities after the study. The team will tell you when to restart any medicines that were paused. Eat and drink according to their instructions, especially if you have diabetes or take medicines with meals.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The report describes how much of the meal remains in the stomach at the imaging time points. The clinician interprets the result alongside symptoms, blood sugar, medicines, and other test findings. A delayed result supports a diagnosis of gastroparesis only when compatible symptoms are present and a physical blockage has been excluded. With the standard low-fat egg-white meal, more than 10% of the meal remaining at four hours is commonly considered delayed; different meals and protocols use different reference ranges. Faster-than-expected emptying can also be clinically relevant, but its interpretation depends on the protocol and clinical context. The scan does not identify every cause of nausea or fullness, and additional testing may be needed.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no single normal range for all gastric emptying studies: results depend on the meal and protocol. For the common standardized solid-meal study, imaging is usually continued through four hours because a shorter study can miss delayed emptying. With the standard low-fat egg-white meal, no more than 10% of the meal should remain at four hours; the interpreting clinician applies the validated range for the meal and imaging method actually used.

\section*{Follow-up}
The ordering clinician reviews the result and decides whether more testing or treatment is appropriate. If the test is abnormal, next steps depend on the symptoms, the degree and type of delay or rapid emptying, possible causes, and the person's overall health.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
The scan itself usually does not cause symptoms that need urgent care. Contact your clinician if you have concerns about restarting medicines or managing blood glucose after the test. Seek prompt care for ongoing vomiting, inability to keep fluids down, severe abdominal pain, signs of dehydration, or very high or low blood glucose, whether or not these symptoms began around the time of the study.
