\chapter{Laparoscopy and Dye Test}

\section*{Overview}
Laparoscopy and dye test (chromopertubation) combines keyhole surgery with a check of whether fluid can pass through the fallopian tubes. A camera is used to examine the pelvis while colored fluid is gently passed through the cervix and uterus. The surgeon looks for fluid spilling from the ends of the tubes. The test can assess tubal patency and visible pelvic conditions, but it does not by itself explain every cause of infertility or prove that an egg and sperm can meet and result in pregnancy.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item laparoscope, camera, and insufflator
  \item uterine cannula and dye syringe
  \item trocars, graspers, and monitoring equipment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Laparoscopy with chromopertubation, laparoscopic tubal patency test, laparoscopy and dye

\section*{Why It Is Done}
The procedure may be offered during an infertility evaluation when there is a higher chance of pelvic or tubal disease, such as a history of pelvic inflammatory disease, ectopic pregnancy, endometriosis, or pelvic surgery. It lets the clinician assess the tubes and look for other pelvic conditions, such as endometriosis or adhesions, at the same operation. It may also be done when laparoscopy is needed for another reason. For people without known risk factors for pelvic disease, a less invasive test such as hysterosalpingography (HSG) or, where available, ultrasound with contrast is usually used first. Laparoscopy and dye is not a routine first test for everyone with fertility concerns.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
This is a surgical procedure that can provide direct views of the pelvis and show whether dye passes through the tubes. It is generally selected when laparoscopy is otherwise appropriate or when risk factors make assessment of pelvic disease important. It is not a routine screening test and does not replace assessment of ovulation, sperm, the uterus, or other factors that may affect fertility.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is usually done under general anesthesia. After a small abdominal incision is made, carbon dioxide gas is used to expand the abdomen, and a camera is inserted. A thin instrument is passed through the vagina and cervix into the uterus. The surgeon injects a small amount of colored fluid through it while watching the fallopian tubes with the camera. Fluid spilling from a tube's open end supports patency. The surgeon also inspects the pelvic organs and may take a biopsy or treat a suitable finding if this was discussed and consented to beforehand. The cuts are closed at the end. The exact steps depend on the clinical plan.

\section*{Preparation}
The surgical team will explain fasting, medicines, and other preparation for general anesthesia. Do not stop prescribed medicines unless the team instructs you to do so. Tell the team about allergies, medical conditions, prior abdominal or pelvic surgery, medicines and supplements, and any possibility of pregnancy. The test is often scheduled after menstrual bleeding has ended and before ovulation to reduce the chance of operating during an early pregnancy; timing and pregnancy testing follow local practice. Follow advice about avoiding unprotected sex before the procedure. Arrange for someone to take you home after anesthesia.

\section*{Contraindications}
The procedure is usually postponed if pregnancy is known or suspected or if there is an active pelvic infection.

\section*{Precautions}
The clinician should know about allergies to any planned medicines or dye, bleeding problems, and conditions that may increase the risks of anesthesia or laparoscopy. Timing may need to change if menstrual bleeding is heavy. Suitability is decided individually; the care team can discuss a less invasive alternative when appropriate.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
Temporary effects may include abdominal cramping, bloating, shoulder-tip pain from the gas, nausea or sore throat after anesthesia, and light vaginal spotting or colored discharge. Less common but serious risks include bleeding, infection, injury to the uterus, bowel, bladder, blood vessels, or other organs, blood clots, and complications of anesthesia. The operation may need to be changed to open surgery if safe access is not possible or a complication occurs. The treating team can explain the risks that apply to the planned procedure and personal health.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people having an uncomplicated diagnostic procedure go home the same day after recovering from anesthesia, but an overnight stay may be needed. Staff check recovery, pain, nausea, and the small wounds. Mild cramping, abdominal soreness, shoulder discomfort, and light spotting may occur for a few days. Follow the team's instructions about wound care, bathing, activity, driving, sex, pain relief, and return to work. Recovery depends on whether another procedure was performed at the same time.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
If dye spills from a tube, it shows that fluid passed through that tube during the test; it does not guarantee normal tube function or future pregnancy. If no spill is seen, blockage is possible, but temporary tubal spasm, the position of the uterine instrument, or other technical factors can affect the result. The finding should be interpreted with the operative view, symptoms, and other fertility tests. The clinician will discuss whether further testing or treatment is appropriate. A biopsy result, if taken, may be available later.

\section*{Expected Results}
The report describes whether dye passed through each tube and whether visible pelvic abnormalities were found. Results may be normal, show one or both tubes without visible dye passage, or identify other findings. A normal result does not rule out all fertility problems.

\section*{Follow-up}
Review the findings with the gynecology or fertility team and ask what they mean for the full fertility evaluation. Next steps depend on age, medical history, other test results, and personal goals; they may include additional assessment, expectant management, surgery in selected cases, or assisted reproduction. Do not assume that a finding of tubal blockage automatically means a particular treatment.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Follow the discharge instructions and contact the surgical team promptly for fever or chills, worsening abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, heavy or increasing vaginal bleeding, difficulty urinating, or a wound that becomes red, warm, swollen, or drains pus. Seek emergency care for chest pain, severe trouble breathing, fainting, coughing blood, or a painful swollen leg. Use local emergency services for severe or rapidly worsening symptoms.
