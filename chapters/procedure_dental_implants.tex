\chapter{Dental Implants}

\section*{Overview}
A dental implant is a small post, usually made of titanium or another biocompatible material, placed in the jawbone to replace the root of a missing tooth. After healing, it can support a crown, bridge, or denture. Implant treatment includes planning, surgery, healing, and restoration, and often takes several months. An implant is not the visible replacement tooth itself.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item surgical handpiece and implant drill set
  \item implant fixture, driver, and torque wrench
  \item surgical guide and dental imaging equipment when used
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Endosseous dental implant; implant-supported tooth or denture. The visible replacement tooth is called a crown or prosthesis; the connector between the implant and crown is an abutment.

\section*{Principle}
After placement, bone heals around the implant and holds it in place. This process is called osseointegration. Once the implant is stable enough, the dental team attaches an abutment and a custom replacement tooth or denture. Some people need extra procedures, such as bone grafting or a sinus lift, if the jaw does not have enough suitable bone.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
An implant may replace one or more missing teeth or help support a denture. It is one option among implants, bridges, and removable dentures. The choice depends on the mouth, general health, desired result, cost, and the patient's preferences. A dentist or specialist assesses whether implant treatment is suitable.

\section*{Role in Care}
Implant placement is planned surgical treatment, not a diagnostic test. Examination and dental imaging, sometimes including three-dimensional scans, help assess the jawbone, nearby teeth, nerves, and sinuses and plan the implant position.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The team examines the mouth, reviews health conditions and medicines, and plans the implant and final restoration. Implant placement is usually done with local anesthetic; sedation or general anesthesia may be used in selected cases. The clinician makes an opening in the gum and places the implant into the jawbone, then closes the gum or attaches a healing cap. If needed, bone grafting may be performed at the same time or in a separate procedure. The implant is left to heal and join to the bone. After healing, the team may uncover it if it was buried, attach an abutment, and fit the crown, bridge, or denture. In selected cases, a temporary tooth or restoration can be fitted earlier; timing is individualized.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the dental team about medical conditions, allergies, smoking or nicotine use, and all medicines and supplements. In particular, mention bone medicines such as bisphosphonates or denosumab, and cancer treatments. These medicines do not automatically rule out an implant, but some can raise the risk of a rare jawbone-healing problem after surgery. Do not stop or change any medicine without advice from the prescribing clinician. Ask about the proposed plan, alternatives, expected treatment time, possible need for grafting, costs, anesthesia, and aftercare. Treating active gum disease and improving oral hygiene before surgery can help reduce complications.

\section*{Contraindications}
Implants are not suitable for everyone, but many health conditions require individualized planning rather than an automatic exclusion.

\section*{Precautions}
Poorly controlled diabetes, smoking, active periodontitis, prior radiation to the jaw, bone medicines such as bisphosphonates or denosumab, limited bone, and difficulty keeping the area clean may raise risks or change the plan. The dental team weighs these factors and may recommend treating disease first, modifying the plan, or choosing a bridge or denture instead.

\section*{Risks}
Pain, swelling, bruising, bleeding, and infection can occur after surgery. Less common complications include injury to a nearby nerve, tooth, or sinus; failure of the implant to join to bone; and problems with a bone graft. Nerve injury may cause numbness or tingling in the lip, chin, or tongue; it usually improves but can rarely last. Antiresorptive bone medicines can rarely be linked to medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, in which jawbone heals poorly or remains exposed; the risk is higher with some cancer treatment doses. Inflammation may first affect the gum around the implant (peri-implant mucositis) and, in some cases, progress to loss of supporting bone (peri-implantitis), which can lead to implant loss. The crown, bridge, or denture can also loosen, wear, or break. Smoking, poor plaque control, a history of gum disease, and diabetes are among factors linked with increased risk of peri-implant disease or failure.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the surgeon's written instructions for oral hygiene, food, activity, pain relief, and any prescribed medicines. Some pain, swelling, or bruising is common; swelling often peaks about 24 to 48 hours after surgery and should then begin to improve. Keep the rest of the mouth clean, and clean the surgical area as directed. Avoid smoking during healing and ask the dental team for help stopping. Attend wound checks and suture removal if scheduled. Healing time varies, especially when grafting is needed, so do not put pressure on or use a temporary restoration except as directed.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The team checks healing and implant stability before fitting or loading the final restoration. Success is not guaranteed, and an implant can fail early or develop disease later.

\section*{Expected Results}
When healing is successful, the implant supports a replacement tooth or denture that can restore appearance and chewing. Treatment time and function depend on the number and position of implants, bone and gum health, healing, and the type of restoration. Implants do not decay like natural teeth, but the surrounding gums and bone can become diseased.

\section*{Follow-up}
Keep the review and maintenance appointments set by the dental team. Brush and clean around the implant and restoration every day using the recommended tools. Contact the team if the implant or restoration feels loose, the bite changes, or gums become red, swollen, tender, or bleed. Your maintenance interval depends on your oral health and risk factors.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the treating dental team promptly for worsening pain or swelling, persistent bleeding, pus, fever, new numbness or tingling, or a loose implant or restoration. Seek emergency care for difficulty breathing or swallowing or rapidly spreading facial or neck swelling.
