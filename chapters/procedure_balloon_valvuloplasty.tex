\chapter{Balloon Valvuloplasty}

\section*{Overview}
Balloon valvuloplasty is a catheter procedure that uses an inflated balloon to widen a narrowed heart valve. Narrowing is called stenosis. It is most often used for pulmonary, mitral, or aortic valve stenosis; in rare cases, it may be used for selected tricuspid valve stenosis. The reason for using it and the expected benefit differ by valve and by the person's age and anatomy. The heart team (cardiologists, surgeons, and other specialists) recommends it only when the likely benefit outweighs the risks and other treatment options.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Balloon valvotomy; percutaneous balloon valvuloplasty; mitral balloon commissurotomy (for selected mitral stenosis)

\section*{Principle}
A thin catheter with a deflated balloon at its tip is guided through a blood vessel to the narrowed valve. Brief inflation stretches the opening; in some valves it can also separate fused leaflets. The balloon widens the person's own valve rather than replacing it, so narrowing may return over time. This can improve forward blood flow, but it may also make the valve leak. The result depends on the valve's structure and the cause of the narrowing.

\section*{History}
Balloon dilation techniques for narrowed heart valves developed during the late 20th century. Kanji Inoue introduced a balloon technique for mitral stenosis in the 1980s, and balloon aortic valvuloplasty was introduced for adults with aortic stenosis later in that decade. Balloon dilation is also widely used for selected pulmonary valve stenosis, including in children.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
The procedure may be used for moderate or severe pulmonary valve stenosis when symptoms or effects on right-heart pressure or function support intervention and the valve anatomy is favorable. Intervention may also be appropriate for some people without symptoms, such as those with severe narrowing or right-heart dysfunction. Balloon treatment is often the first choice for isolated pulmonary valve stenosis, including in children. In children and young adults with congenital aortic valve stenosis, balloon dilation may be used when the narrowing puts significant pressure on the left ventricle. It can relieve the obstruction, but may cause or worsen aortic valve leakage; repeat treatment or surgery may still be needed later. Mitral balloon commissurotomy, which widens the valve by separating fused edges, may be offered to people with severe rheumatic mitral stenosis who need an intervention, when the valve anatomy is suitable and there is no important clot in the left atrium or more than mild valve leakage (regurgitation). In rare cases, balloon dilation may be considered for isolated tricuspid stenosis when there is no important tricuspid leakage. Guidelines recommend surgery for severe symptomatic tricuspid stenosis, but a balloon procedure may be attempted first when the stenosis is isolated. In adults with severe aortic stenosis, balloon dilation is rarely used as a definitive treatment. For selected people whose condition is unstable and who cannot have definitive valve replacement right away, or who have symptoms from severe aortic stenosis and need urgent high-risk non-heart surgery, the heart team may consider balloon dilation as a temporary bridge. In this setting, catheter-based valve replacement may also be considered when feasible. Balloon dilation can cause severe, sudden aortic valve leakage (regurgitation) and usually does not provide lasting relief.

\section*{Role in Care}
The role depends on the valve. It can be a main catheter treatment for selected pulmonary stenosis and selected rheumatic mitral stenosis. For adult aortic stenosis, it usually provides temporary improvement or helps with a specific short-term clinical need while the heart team plans definitive treatment.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is done in a cardiac catheterization laboratory. Medicine is given for comfort, and the care team monitors heart rhythm, blood pressure, and breathing. A catheter is inserted through a blood vessel, usually in the groin, and guided to the valve with X-ray and sometimes ultrasound imaging. The route depends on the valve: mitral procedures usually enter through a vein, then pass through a small puncture in the wall between the heart's upper chambers to reach the left side; aortic and pulmonary procedures commonly use an artery or vein, respectively. The balloon is inflated briefly, then deflated and removed. The team checks blood flow and valve leakage before ending the procedure.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews heart imaging, usually an echocardiogram, to assess the valve and plan the procedure. For a mitral procedure, a transesophageal echocardiogram may be used to check valve anatomy and look for a clot in the left atrium. Blood tests and other heart tests may also be needed. Follow instructions about fasting and medicines; any change to blood thinners must be planned by the treating team. Tell the team about allergies, kidney problems, pregnancy, and all medicines.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Suitability depends on which valve is being treated. For mitral balloon commissurotomy, a clot in the left atrium, more than mild mitral leakage, severe or extensive calcium deposits on the valve, major damage to the structures supporting the valve from below, or lack of fused valve edges may make the procedure unsuitable; other heart problems may make surgery a better option. For aortic stenosis, significant existing aortic leakage is an important concern because balloon dilation can worsen it. For pulmonary stenosis, the valve's shape and any existing leakage also affect the plan. The heart team discusses alternatives, including surgery or transcatheter valve replacement when appropriate.

\section*{Risks}
Possible complications include bleeding, bruising, or infection where the catheter enters the blood vessel; abnormal heart rhythms; blood clots, heart attack, or stroke; injury to the heart or nearby structures; fluid buildup around the heart; and increased leakage through the treated valve. After aortic valvuloplasty, this leakage can be sudden and severe. A mitral procedure requires a small puncture in the wall between the heart's upper chambers. This opening usually closes on its own, but if it remains significant, it may need follow-up. The specific risks differ by valve and may be higher if another treatment is performed at the same time. Aortic stenosis often returns after balloon dilation, so it is generally not a permanent treatment for adults.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Afterward, the care team monitors heart rhythm, the catheter entry site, and circulation in the affected limb. An echocardiogram may be done to assess valve opening and leakage. The length of the hospital stay and activity restrictions depend on the valve treated and the person's recovery. Follow the team's instructions for wound care, medicines, and return to activity.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The team checks whether blood flows more easily through the valve and whether the procedure caused or worsened leakage. Echocardiography and symptoms help judge the result. An improved measurement does not guarantee that symptoms will resolve, and repeat treatment or valve repair or replacement may still be needed.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no single expected valve area or pressure result for all patients. The report describes how the valve opening, pressure gradient, and leakage changed compared with before the procedure. These findings are interpreted in light of which valve was treated and the patient's overall health.

\section*{Follow-up}
Regular cardiology visits and echocardiograms are used to monitor valve function and check for recurrent narrowing or leakage. Some people need another catheter procedure or surgery later. Adults treated for aortic stenosis should discuss definitive valve treatment with their heart team because balloon dilation alone usually does not provide lasting relief.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek emergency help for chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting, sudden weakness or trouble speaking, or bleeding from the catheter site that does not stop with firm pressure. Contact the care team promptly for a growing bruise or swelling at the access site, new numbness or coldness in the arm or leg used for catheter access, fever, or a fast or irregular heartbeat.
