\chapter{Laparoscopy}

\section*{Overview}
Laparoscopy is surgery performed through one or more small cuts in the abdomen. A thin instrument with a light and camera (a laparoscope) lets the surgeon see the abdominal or pelvic organs on a screen. Other instruments can be passed through additional cuts to take a tissue sample or treat a problem. Laparoscopy can be used to investigate symptoms, perform an operation, or do both. The exact steps and recovery depend on the reason for surgery and what is done.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item laparoscope, camera, and light source
  \item trocars/ports and insufflator
  \item procedure-specific graspers, scissors, and energy instruments
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Keyhole surgery, minimally invasive abdominal surgery, diagnostic laparoscopy, operative laparoscopy

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Diagnostic laparoscopy may be considered when examination, imaging, or other tests have not answered an important question and direct viewing or tissue sampling could help. Operative laparoscopy can be used for selected procedures involving the abdomen or pelvis, such as removal of the appendix or gallbladder, repair of some hernias, treatment of endometriosis, or surgery for an ectopic pregnancy. Whether laparoscopy is suitable depends on the condition, the person's health, and the surgeon's assessment. Open surgery or another treatment may be safer or more effective in some cases.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Laparoscopy is an operation, not a routine screening test. It may be planned mainly to diagnose a problem, mainly to treat it, or for both purposes. Before consenting, ask what the surgeon expects to look for or do, whether a biopsy or additional treatment may happen during the same operation, and what alternatives are available.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
Laparoscopy is usually done under general anesthesia, so the patient is asleep. The team cleans the skin and makes a small cut, often near the navel. Carbon dioxide gas is usually used to gently expand the abdomen and create room to work. The surgeon inserts the camera and, when needed, other instruments through additional small cuts. A tissue sample or planned treatment may be performed. At the end, the instruments and most of the gas are removed, and the cuts are closed with stitches, skin glue, or dressings. The number and location of cuts, use of a urinary catheter, and length of surgery vary.

\section*{Preparation}
The surgical team gives instructions based on the planned operation and the person's health. These may cover when to stop eating and drinking, which medicines to take or adjust, and whether bowel preparation or other testing is needed. Do not stop blood thinners or other prescribed medicines without instructions from the treating team. Tell the team about allergies, previous abdominal operations, medical conditions, medicines and supplements, and any possibility of pregnancy. Arrange an adult to take you home after anesthesia; the team may also advise someone to stay with you during early recovery.

\section*{Contraindications}
There is no single list that rules out laparoscopy for everyone. The operation may need to be changed to a larger open incision if safe access is not possible, findings require it, or a complication occurs.

\section*{Precautions}
The balance of risks may change with severe heart or lung disease, bleeding problems, pregnancy, prior abdominal surgery or adhesions, infection, or other conditions. Some people can still have laparoscopy with additional planning; for others, another approach may be safer. The surgeon and anesthesiologist assess individual risks and discuss alternatives.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
Temporary effects can include abdominal soreness, bloating, shoulder-tip pain from the gas, tiredness, nausea, or a sore throat after anesthesia. Less common but serious complications include bleeding, wound or internal infection, injury to the bowel, bladder, ureters, blood vessels, or other organs, blood clots in the legs or lungs, and complications of anesthesia. A hernia can form at an incision. The operation may not be completed as planned, may not identify the cause of symptoms, or may need to be converted to open surgery. Risks depend on the specific operation and the person's health; the surgeon should discuss the relevant risks before consent.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After surgery, staff monitor recovery from anesthesia, pain, nausea, breathing, and the wounds. Many people go home the same day after an uncomplicated diagnostic procedure; more extensive surgery or a health concern may require a hospital stay. Mild abdominal or shoulder discomfort, bruising, and fatigue can occur for several days. Gradually increase walking and activity as directed. Follow the team's instructions for wound care, bathing, pain medicines, lifting, work, driving, and follow-up. Restrictions and recovery time vary substantially, especially when laparoscopy includes a major operation.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The result depends on why laparoscopy was done. The surgeon may explain what was seen soon after the operation; laboratory results from a biopsy can take longer. A normal-looking view does not rule out every cause of symptoms. The care team explains whether the intended procedure was completed and whether further tests or treatment are needed.

\section*{Expected Results}
The intended result may be a diagnosis, a tissue sample, treatment of a known condition, or a combination. A small-incision approach often allows faster recovery than open surgery for suitable operations, but it does not guarantee less pain, a cure, or avoidance of complications or conversion to open surgery.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend the recommended follow-up, including review of any biopsy results. Continue medicines and activity restrictions as instructed. Contact the surgical team if the wound is not healing or symptoms are not improving as expected.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Follow the discharge instructions and contact the surgical team promptly for fever or chills, worsening or severe abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, increasing abdominal swelling, wound redness or warmth, pus, worsening bleeding, or difficulty urinating. Seek emergency care for chest pain, severe trouble breathing, fainting, coughing blood, or a painful swollen leg, as these can signal a serious complication. Use local emergency services for severe or rapidly worsening symptoms.
