\chapter{Dental Cleaning}

\section*{Overview}
A professional dental cleaning removes plaque and hardened deposits (tartar or calculus) from teeth. It may be part of a preventive dental visit or treatment for gum disease. The type and timing of care depend on the person's teeth, gums, medical history, and risk of tooth decay or gum disease. Cleaning supports oral health but does not replace daily brushing, cleaning between teeth, or dental examinations.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Professional dental cleaning; dental prophylaxis; scaling and polishing. For gum disease, deep cleaning may mean scaling and root planing.

\section*{Principle}
Plaque is a soft film of bacteria that forms on teeth. If it is not removed, it can harden into calculus, which cannot be removed by ordinary brushing. A dental professional removes deposits with hand instruments or an ultrasonic scaler. Polishing may remove some surface stains, but it does not whiten teeth. Scaling and root planing cleans deposits from below the gumline and smooths root surfaces; it is a separate treatment used when indicated for periodontitis.

\section*{History}
Alfred Fones helped establish dental hygiene as a profession and opened a dental hygiene school in Bridgeport, Connecticut, in 1913.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Cleaning removes deposits that contribute to gum inflammation and helps maintain oral health. The visit may also include an examination and, when appropriate, X-rays or preventive treatments. An examination for oral disease is a separate clinical assessment; cleaning alone does not screen for oral cancer.

\section*{Role in Care}
It is preventive care or, when deeper deposits and gum disease are present, part of periodontal treatment. It is not a test that diagnoses cavities or gum disease by itself.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The dental professional examines the teeth and gums and discusses the care needed. They remove deposits with hand scalers, an ultrasonic instrument, or both. Routine cleaning focuses on deposits above the gumline and accessible areas near it. If periodontal treatment is needed, scaling and root planing may be done under local anesthetic, sometimes over more than one visit. Polishing and flossing may be offered. Fluoride treatment may be recommended for some patients.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the dental team about medical conditions, allergies, pregnancy, medicines, and prior problems with dental treatment. Do not stop prescribed medicines, including blood thinners, unless the prescribing clinician tells you to. Antibiotics before dental care are recommended only for a small group of people at highest risk of serious infective endocarditis, and only for procedures that manipulate the gums, touch the area around a tooth root, or cut the lining of the mouth. Examples of qualifying heart conditions include an artificial heart valve, a prior episode of infective endocarditis, and certain congenital heart conditions. A joint replacement alone does not usually mean antibiotics are needed. Ask the dental team or treating clinician if you have been told you need antibiotic prophylaxis.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Routine cleaning is safe for most people. The dental team may adapt or postpone care for an acute medical problem, severe oral pain or swelling, or a condition that affects safe treatment. Tell them if you have a pacemaker or other implanted device, a bleeding disorder, or a weakened immune system so they can plan care appropriately. Do not postpone urgent dental assessment for spreading facial swelling, fever, or difficulty swallowing or breathing.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary gum tenderness, slight bleeding, or tooth sensitivity can occur, especially when gums are inflamed or after deep cleaning. After scaling and root planing, sensitivity to hot or cold and gum soreness may last for several days; as swelling goes down, teeth may look longer or spaces between them may become more noticeable. Ask the clinician to explain the expected benefits and risks before treatment.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people can return to normal activities and continue gentle brushing and cleaning between teeth. If gums are tender, use a soft toothbrush and follow the dental team's advice. After fluoride varnish or another fluoride treatment, follow the product-specific instructions; eating, drinking, or brushing restrictions vary by product. After scaling and root planing, follow the clinician's instructions about sensitivity, pain relief, and oral hygiene.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The dental professional may discuss findings from the examination and whether further care is needed. A routine cleaning does not remove every deposit in every location and cannot guarantee that decay or gum disease will not develop. Keep up daily oral hygiene and arrange follow-up at the interval recommended for your oral health.

\section*{Expected Results}
Teeth should feel smoother, and deposits addressed during treatment should be reduced. Gum tenderness or sensitivity may briefly increase. Results depend on the type of cleaning and the condition of the teeth and gums.

\section*{Follow-up}
There is no single cleaning interval that suits everyone. The dental professional recommends a recall interval based on oral health, risk factors, and response to care; some people need more frequent periodontal maintenance. Follow recommendations for any fillings, gum treatment, or other care identified during the visit.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact a dentist for severe or worsening pain, persistent bleeding, increasing swelling, pus, or fever after treatment. Seek emergency care for difficulty breathing or swallowing or rapidly spreading facial or neck swelling.
