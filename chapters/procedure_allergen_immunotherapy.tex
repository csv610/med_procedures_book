\chapter{Allergen Immunotherapy (Allergy Shots)}

\section*{Overview}
Allergen immunotherapy is a treatment that uses repeated exposure to a clinically relevant allergen to reduce allergic symptoms over time. This chapter focuses on subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT), or allergy shots, which are given by injection in a medical setting. Sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT), given under the tongue, is a related option for selected allergies and has different products, risks, and instructions. Immunotherapy may be used for allergic rhinitis, selected patients with allergic asthma, and certain stinging-insect venom allergies.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Allergy shots; allergen desensitization; subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT); hyposensitization therapy

\section*{Principle}
Repeated exposure to carefully selected allergen doses can modify allergic immune responses and reduce symptoms with natural exposure. The precise mechanisms are not fully understood. SCIT usually has a buildup phase, when doses are increased gradually, followed by maintenance injections at progressively spaced intervals.

\section*{History}
Allergen immunotherapy was pioneered by Leonard Noon and John Freeman in 1911, who demonstrated that repeated grass pollen injections reduced allergic symptoms, and it has remained a cornerstone of allergy treatment for over a century.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
SCIT may be offered for allergic rhinitis, with or without allergic asthma, when symptoms remain troublesome despite medication and allergen avoidance, or when a patient prefers a treatment that may modify the allergic response. Asthma must be well controlled. Venom immunotherapy may be recommended after a systemic allergic reaction to an insect sting when testing confirms venom-specific allergy. A positive allergy test alone does not establish that an allergen causes a patient's symptoms. Immunotherapy is not a replacement for urgent treatment of anaphylaxis or asthma.

\section*{Role in Care}
An elective, specialist-directed treatment used alongside routine allergy care. SCIT is distinct from SLIT tablets or drops, and candidacy, risks, dosing, and setting differ by route and allergen.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician matches the patient's history and symptoms with skin-prick or blood tests for allergen-specific IgE to select relevant allergens. Injections of allergen extract are given under the skin using an individualized schedule. In a conventional buildup schedule, injections are often given once or twice weekly for several months with gradually increasing doses; accelerated schedules are used in some settings. Maintenance injections are given at longer intervals, often every 2 to 4 weeks, with schedules varying by product, response, and clinical protocol. SCIT must be administered in a setting equipped and staffed to recognize and treat anaphylaxis. The dose may be adjusted or withheld after a significant reaction, a change in health, or a prolonged gap between visits.

\section*{Preparation}
Before each injection, the care team checks for new illness, worsening allergy or asthma symptoms, reactions to the previous dose, and other relevant health or medication changes. An injection is generally postponed when asthma is uncontrolled or symptoms have worsened. Tell the team about pregnancy, beta-blockers, prior severe allergic reactions, and any new medicines. Do not stop a prescribed medicine without the prescriber's advice. Remain under observation at the clinic for at least 30 minutes after each injection; some reactions occur later, so follow the team's emergency plan after leaving.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Do not start immunotherapy during pregnancy or give SCIT when asthma is uncontrolled. If pregnancy occurs during a stable maintenance course, the specialist may consider continuing without increasing the dose after reviewing risks and benefits. Beta-blocker use, a history of anaphylaxis, significant cardiovascular disease, or inability to use epinephrine if needed requires individualized specialist assessment; these circumstances may make treatment inappropriate. Patients should not independently stop prescribed medicines. The clinician giving immunotherapy must be able to recognize and treat anaphylaxis.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary redness, itching, or swelling at the injection site is common. Less often, a reaction affects other parts of the body and may include hives, swelling, nasal symptoms, cough, wheezing, vomiting, dizziness, or low blood pressure. Anaphylaxis is uncommon but can be life-threatening. Most serious reactions begin within 30 minutes, but later reactions can occur. The clinic must have staff and medicines to treat anaphylaxis, including epinephrine.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The patient is observed for at least 30 minutes after each injection and is advised to report any symptoms before leaving. The care team provides instructions for recognizing and responding to delayed reactions; patients at increased risk may be instructed to carry an epinephrine autoinjector and should know how and when to use it. Keep appointments and contact the clinic about missed doses rather than resuming the previous dose without advice. Symptom control, medication use, asthma status, and reactions are reviewed during treatment.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Response is assessed by symptom control, medication use, and daily function. Benefit may take months and is not guaranteed. Repeat skin or blood allergy testing is not routinely used to measure response during treatment. Venom immunotherapy substantially reduces the risk of systemic reactions to future stings, but it does not remove all risk; the specialist advises whether an epinephrine autoinjector is still needed and how long treatment should continue. Sting challenge is not a routine way for patients to confirm protection.

\section*{Expected Results}
Many patients have fewer symptoms and need less allergy medication, but the degree of benefit varies and some do not respond. Local injection-site reactions may occur, and systemic reactions remain possible throughout treatment.

\section*{Follow-up}
The treating specialist reviews benefit, adverse reactions, asthma control, and dosing throughout the course. For inhalant allergy, patients who are improving are generally treated for at least 3 years; the decision to continue or stop depends on response and the individual plan. Venom immunotherapy is commonly given for 3 to 5 years, but longer or ongoing treatment may be advised for people with high-risk features. Routine repeat allergy tests are not needed to judge whether inhalant immunotherapy is working.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Tell clinic staff immediately about symptoms after an injection, especially widespread hives, facial or throat swelling, trouble breathing, persistent cough, vomiting, dizziness, or faintness. These may signal a systemic allergic reaction; staff should assess and treat promptly. After leaving, use prescribed epinephrine as directed and call local emergency services for breathing or throat symptoms, fainting, or other signs of a severe reaction. Contact the treating clinic about a large or persistent local reaction or any delayed symptoms.
