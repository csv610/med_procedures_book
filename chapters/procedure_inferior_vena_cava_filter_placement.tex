\chapter{Inferior Vena Cava Filter Placement}

\section*{Overview}
An inferior vena cava (IVC) filter is a small metal device placed in the large vein in the abdomen that returns blood from the lower body to the heart. It can catch some blood clots traveling toward the lungs and lower the chance of a pulmonary embolism. It does not treat a leg clot or prevent new clots from forming, and it can cause complications, especially if left in longer than needed.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item vascular sheath, guidewire, and filter-delivery catheter
  \item fluoroscopy and contrast equipment
  \item ultrasound for access and vital-signs monitoring
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
IVC filter, vena cava filter, retrievable IVC filter

\section*{Principle}
The filter is usually placed in the IVC below the veins that drain the kidneys. Blood continues to flow through it while it catches some clots traveling from the pelvis or legs. The filter does not dissolve a clot. Many filters can be retrieved, but removal is a separate procedure and may become more difficult over time.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
An IVC filter may be considered for a person with a recent pulmonary embolism or a proximal deep vein thrombosis (DVT) who cannot safely take anticoagulant medicine, such as during active serious bleeding. It is not routinely used in addition to effective anticoagulation or to prevent clots in people who do not have a known acute DVT or pulmonary embolism, including most trauma or major-surgery patients. If a clot occurs despite anticoagulation, the care team first checks the diagnosis, dose, adherence, and other reasons treatment may not be working; a filter is considered only in selected cases.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
This is a preventive procedure used in selected people with acute venous thromboembolism when anticoagulation cannot be used safely. It does not replace anticoagulation when anticoagulation is safe, and it does not treat the existing clot.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is usually performed by an interventional radiologist in a hospital or imaging suite, with local anesthetic and sometimes sedation. A vein in the groin or neck is accessed, often using ultrasound guidance. X-ray imaging and contrast dye may be used to check the vein anatomy and guide placement. The compressed filter is advanced through a thin tube and released in the IVC, usually below the kidney veins. Imaging is used to check its position. The procedure usually takes less than an hour, but timing varies.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews the reason for the filter, blood-clot history, medicines, allergies, kidney function, and any prior imaging. Tell the team if you have had a reaction to contrast dye, may be pregnant, or take anticoagulants or diabetes medicines. The team will give instructions about fasting and medicines; do not stop an anticoagulant or other medicine on your own. Ask who will arrange follow-up and filter removal if a retrievable filter is placed.

\section*{Contraindications}
No specific absolute contraindication is identified here; the treating team assesses suitability for the individual.

\section*{Precautions}
Pregnancy, infection, kidney problems, or clot in the IVC can change the plan. Filter placement may not be possible or may need a different approach if the IVC is blocked, unusually shaped, or too large for the available filter. Because filters can increase the risk of DVT and IVC blockage and may move, break, or penetrate the vein wall, the team weighs these risks against the short-term risk of pulmonary embolism. A retrieval plan and responsible follow-up clinician should be identified when a retrievable filter is placed.

\section*{Risks}
Risks include bleeding or bruising where the vein is accessed, infection, injury to a blood vessel, and an allergic reaction or kidney effects from contrast dye. Later problems can include a new DVT, clotting or blockage of the IVC, filter movement or tilt, a filter leg pressing through the vein wall, or filter breakage. Retrieval can also cause bleeding, vein injury, or a filter part breaking. Some complications become more likely the longer a retrievable filter remains in place.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The access site is checked for bleeding, and most people can go home the same day. Anticoagulation is started or resumed as soon as the treating team considers it safe; the filter does not replace it. Before discharge, confirm which clinician will track the filter and how to arrange reassessment and retrieval. A retrievable filter should be removed once protection is no longer needed, unless the risk of removal is greater. There is no single removal date for everyone, so do not wait for symptoms or assume the filter will be removed automatically.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
Technical success means the filter is deployed in the intended position and blood can continue to flow through the IVC. A filter lowers, but does not eliminate, the chance of a pulmonary embolism. The goal is to prevent a serious embolism while anticoagulation is unsafe, then remove a retrievable filter when its protection is no longer needed.

\section*{Expected Results}
A well-positioned filter may reduce the chance of a pulmonary embolism during a period when anticoagulants cannot be used safely. It does not guarantee that an embolism will not occur and does not prevent or treat DVT.

\section*{Follow-up}
Keep appointments with the clinician responsible for the filter. The team will reassess the need for anticoagulation and filter retrieval. Imaging may be used when needed to plan retrieval or evaluate symptoms; routine repeated scans are not required for everyone. Tell all clinicians that you have an IVC filter and whether it is intended to be retrieved.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek emergency care for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, coughing blood, fainting, or severe weakness, which may signal a pulmonary embolism or another emergency. Contact the care team promptly for new or worsening leg or abdominal swelling or pain, bleeding or a growing lump at the access site, fever, or increasing pain. Also contact the clinician responsible for the filter if you are unsure whether it is retrievable or when it should be reviewed.
