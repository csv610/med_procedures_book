\chapter{Cerumen Removal}

\section*{Overview}
Cerumen, or earwax, protects the skin of the ear canal and usually moves out on its own. Removal is considered when wax causes symptoms or prevents an examination of the ear that is needed. Wax that is present but causes no problem usually does not need treatment. A clinician can remove it with a small instrument, suction, gentle irrigation, or wax-softening drops.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item otoscope or microscope with ear speculum
  \item curette, suction tip, or irrigation equipment
  \item light source and collection basin
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Earwax removal; cerumen disimpaction; ear irrigation (one removal method); microsuction (one removal method).

\section*{Principle}
The clinician softens, loosens, or lifts the wax so it can be removed from the ear canal without injuring the canal or eardrum. The method is selected according to the amount and consistency of wax, the ear's anatomy, symptoms, and medical history.

\section*{History}
Earwax has long been removed using irrigation and instruments. Today, clinicians may also use suction and magnification to see the ear canal while removing wax.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Removal may help when impacted wax causes reduced hearing, a blocked or full feeling, ear discomfort, itching, or tinnitus, or when it prevents a needed examination or hearing test. If hearing or other symptoms remain after the wax is cleared, another cause should be considered.

\section*{Role in Care}
This is a treatment, not a test. The clinician examines the ear before and after treatment. Wax may be removed to allow examination of the eardrum or to support hearing-aid care, but routine removal is not needed when the ear can be examined and the person has no symptoms.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician asks about symptoms and prior ear problems, then looks into the ear with an otoscope or magnification. Under direct vision, the wax may be softened with drops, loosened with a small curette or loop, removed with gentle suction, or flushed with controlled irrigation using fluid near body temperature. The clinician checks the ear again afterward. If the wax cannot be removed safely or comfortably, treatment may be stopped and the person referred to a clinician with specialized equipment or training.

\section*{Preparation}
Usually no special preparation is needed. Tell the clinician about an eardrum perforation, ear tubes, previous ear surgery, ear discharge or infection, diabetes, a weakened immune system, bleeding problems, blood-thinning medicines, or a narrow ear canal. Do not use ear drops or try irrigation at home if you may have a hole in the eardrum or have had recent ear surgery unless a clinician advises it.

\section*{Contraindications}
Irrigation is generally avoided if there may be a hole in the eardrum, ear tubes, prior ear surgery, or an active ear infection or discharge. These factors do not always prevent removal; a clinician may choose careful manual removal or suction instead.

\section*{Precautions}
There is no single method that is right for everyone. Diabetes, reduced immunity, blood-thinning treatment, a narrow or altered ear canal, or prior radiation to the ear or head and neck can increase risk or change the method.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary discomfort, cough, ringing, or dizziness may occur. Less common problems include scratching or bleeding of the ear canal, infection, and injury or perforation of the eardrum. Irrigation fluid that is not near body temperature can trigger dizziness, nausea, or, rarely, a slowing of the heart rate. The risk depends on the method, ear anatomy, and medical history.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The clinician checks whether the blockage has cleared and whether the ear canal and eardrum appear healthy. Follow any instructions about keeping the ear dry or using drops. Do not insert cotton swabs, hairpins, or other objects into the ear canal; they can push wax deeper or injure the ear. Do not use high-pressure water devices in the ear because they can injure the eardrum. Avoid ear candling because it does not safely remove wax and can cause burns or injury.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Success means enough wax has been removed to relieve the problem or allow the needed examination, and the clinician has checked the ear afterward. If wax remains, another treatment method or referral may be needed. If hearing loss, pain, or tinnitus continues after the ear is clear, the clinician may arrange a hearing test or assess for another cause. People who repeatedly develop impaction can ask about safe prevention and follow-up.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact a clinician if pain, hearing loss, ringing, or dizziness persists or worsens after removal. Seek prompt care for bleeding, fluid or pus from the ear, fever, or severe pain. Sudden hearing loss needs urgent medical assessment, whether or not wax has recently been removed.
