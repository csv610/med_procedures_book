\chapter{Kidney Biopsy}

\section*{Overview}
A kidney biopsy removes one or more tiny samples of kidney tissue so that a pathologist can examine them. Most biopsies are done through the skin with a needle guided by ultrasound. A biopsy can identify the type and severity of kidney disease and help the care team choose treatment. It is done when the expected information is likely to affect care.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Renal biopsy; percutaneous kidney biopsy; native kidney biopsy; transplant kidney biopsy

\section*{Principle}
The needle removes a small core of kidney tissue. A pathology laboratory examines the sample using methods that can show the structure of the kidney filters, tubules, blood vessels, and immune deposits. The results are interpreted with the person's symptoms, blood and urine tests, and imaging.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A biopsy may be recommended for unexplained worsening kidney function, significant protein or blood in the urine, nephrotic syndrome, suspected inflammation of the kidney filters, or kidney involvement from a systemic disease such as lupus. It may also be used to assess dysfunction of a transplanted kidney. A biopsy is not needed for every kidney problem; the clinician weighs the likely diagnostic value against the bleeding risk and considers whether imaging, genetic testing, or other tests could answer the question.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
This is an invasive diagnostic procedure. It can also guide treatment or help explain why a transplanted kidney is not working as expected. A biopsy result does not always provide a complete diagnosis, and the sample may occasionally be too small to interpret.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
Most biopsies of a person's own kidney are done with the patient lying face down. The clinician uses ultrasound, and sometimes other imaging, to locate the kidney and guide the needle. After cleaning the skin and injecting local anesthetic, the clinician passes a spring-loaded needle through the skin to obtain one or more small tissue samples. The patient may be asked to hold their breath briefly while a sample is taken. A transplant kidney is near the skin surface and is usually biopsied with the patient lying on their back. If a needle biopsy is not suitable, a specialist may consider another approach, such as a transjugular or surgical biopsy.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews the reason for biopsy, imaging, blood pressure, blood count, kidney function, and blood-clotting tests. Tell the clinician about all medicines, especially anticoagulants and antiplatelet medicines such as aspirin, as well as bleeding problems, allergies, pregnancy, and any prior reaction to local anesthetic. Do not stop or restart blood-thinning medicines without a plan from the clinicians managing the biopsy and the medicine; the plan balances bleeding risk against the risk of a blood clot. Blood pressure may need treatment before biopsy. Fasting depends on whether sedation is planned and the clinic's instructions. The team may arrange blood typing or other preparation based on individual bleeding risk.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Uncontrolled high blood pressure, a severe uncorrected bleeding problem, inability to remain still or follow instructions, or infection over the planned biopsy site can make a percutaneous biopsy unsafe or require delay. A small or scarred kidney, multiple cysts, unusual anatomy, anemia, or active kidney infection may increase risk or reduce the chance of getting a useful sample. A solitary kidney is not an automatic prohibition, but the consequences of serious bleeding are greater, so the decision is individualized and another biopsy route may be considered. The team discusses the benefit and risk of the chosen approach before proceeding.

\section*{Risks}
Bleeding is the main complication. A small amount of blood in the urine or a small collection of blood near the kidney can occur; most resolve without treatment. More serious bleeding may require a transfusion or a procedure to block the bleeding vessel, and very rarely surgery or loss of the kidney. Other risks include pain, infection, injury to nearby structures, or an inadequate tissue sample. A small connection between a kidney artery and vein (an arteriovenous fistula) can form; many cause no symptoms and close on their own.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After the biopsy, the patient rests while staff check blood pressure, pulse, pain, the biopsy site, and urine for visible blood. Observation is commonly about 6 to 8 hours for lower-risk patients and may extend to 24 hours or an overnight stay when bleeding risk is higher or symptoms occur. The exact plan varies by patient and facility. Blood tests or imaging are done if the clinical team has a reason; they are not routinely needed after every uncomplicated biopsy. At home, follow the team's activity and wound-care instructions. Heavy lifting, strenuous exercise, and contact sports are usually avoided for about two weeks.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The tissue is examined for changes that can identify a disease pattern, show active inflammation or chronic scarring, and help predict how the disease may respond to treatment. Results may take several days or longer; a preliminary result may be available sooner when the clinical situation is urgent. The nephrologist reviews the findings with the patient and explains how they affect the treatment plan.

\section*{Expected Results}
The goal is an adequate, interpretable tissue sample that helps answer the clinical question. The result may show a specific kidney disease, a range of possible diagnoses, chronic damage, or no clear abnormality. A normal result is not required for the biopsy to be useful.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend the appointment to review pathology results and the treatment plan. Repeat biopsy is considered only when the result is likely to change management, such as in selected cases of transplant dysfunction or disease monitoring; it is not automatically repeated. Follow the clinician's instructions on when to resume blood-thinning medicines and normal activities.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent medical care for bright red or dark urine, blood clots in the urine, inability to urinate, worsening pain in the side, back, or abdomen, dizziness or fainting, marked weakness, fever, or bleeding, redness, or drainage from the biopsy site. A small amount of pink urine can occur shortly after biopsy, but visible blood that persists or returns should be reported to the biopsy team promptly.
