\chapter{Cast Application}

\section*{Overview}
A cast is a firm support placed around an injured arm or leg to limit movement while a bone, joint, tendon, or ligament heals. It is usually made from plaster or fiberglass over soft padding. A cast is not right for every injury: the clinician chooses among a cast, a removable brace, a splint, or surgery based on the injury and how much swelling is expected. A splint is open on at least one side, so it can allow more room for swelling.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Orthopedic cast; plaster cast; fiberglass cast; casting.

\section*{Principle}
The cast supports the injured area and helps keep it in a position chosen by the treating clinician. It may reduce pain and protect the injury while it heals. A cast does not itself make a fracture heal. Some fractures need to be repositioned, treated with surgery, or managed in another way before or instead of casting.

\section*{History}
Antonius Mathijsen, a Dutch military surgeon, introduced plaster-of-Paris bandages for immobilizing fractures in 1851. Fiberglass later became another common casting material.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A clinician may apply a cast to support certain fractures or injuries after they have been assessed and, when needed, repositioned. Casts may also be used after some operations or to protect a healing tendon or ligament. The type and length of immobilization depend on the injury, its alignment, and the person's age and health.

\section*{Role in Care}
Casting is a treatment, not a test. X-rays or other imaging may be used before and after application to assess the injury and check the position of the bones. A cast may be part of nonsurgical care or used after surgery.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician checks the skin, swelling, and blood flow, movement, and feeling below the injury. Soft padding is placed around the limb, followed by wet plaster or fiberglass bandage. The clinician shapes the material to support the injury without pressing too tightly. The material hardens as it sets. The team checks the fingers or toes again and may take an X-ray to confirm the position of the bones.

If swelling is likely to increase, the clinician may choose a splint or may split the cast to make room. Do not adjust or remove a cast yourself.

\section*{Preparation}
The clinician asks about the injury, checks the skin and circulation, and may review or order X-rays. Tell the team about skin problems, reduced sensation, diabetes, circulation problems, allergies to cast materials, and any medicines or health conditions that may affect healing. Follow instructions about pain relief and whether you may eat or drink; these depend on the treatment plan, especially if a fracture needs to be repositioned or surgery is planned.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Marked swelling, an open wound, a skin infection, poor circulation, or reduced sensation may affect the choice or timing of a cast. These do not always rule out casting, but may require a splint, wound care, closer checks, or another treatment. A tight cast around a limb that is swelling can restrict blood flow or raise pressure in the tissues.

\section*{Risks}
Swelling inside a cast can cause pressure on the skin, nerves, or blood vessels. Rarely, dangerous pressure builds within the limb (compartment syndrome) and needs emergency treatment. Other problems include skin sores or blisters, itching, stiffness, delayed healing or loss of bone alignment, and a burn while the cast material hardens. A cast on a leg can also increase the risk of a blood clot, especially when movement is limited; the care team assesses each person's risk.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the team's instructions about weight bearing, walking aids, and how long to keep the cast on. For the first 2 to 3 days, raise the injured limb above heart level when resting to reduce swelling, unless the team advises otherwise. Gently move the fingers or toes and other uncovered joints. Keep the cast dry unless the team confirms that its liner is waterproof. Do not put objects, powders, or creams inside it, and do not cut or trim it. Do not bear weight on a leg cast until the team says it is safe.

Check the exposed fingers or toes for normal warmth, color, feeling, and movement. Mild swelling and discomfort can occur early, but symptoms should improve with the care instructions. A cast may need adjustment or replacement if it becomes wet, cracked, loose, or too tight.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The team checks that the cast supports the injury and that circulation, movement, and feeling remain satisfactory. Follow-up visits and imaging are arranged according to the injury and healing progress. There is no single cast duration: it may be several weeks or longer. After removal, the limb may be stiff, weak, or dry; movement and strength usually return gradually, and exercises or therapy may be recommended.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Get urgent medical help for severe or worsening pain, pain that increases when you gently move the fingers or toes, a cast that feels increasingly tight, new or increasing swelling, numbness or tingling, burning or stinging under the cast, inability to move the fingers or toes, or fingers or toes that become pale, blue, or cold. These may signal pressure on nerves or blood flow, including compartment syndrome. Do not wait for loss of a pulse or inability to move before seeking help. Contact the cast or fracture clinic promptly if the cast gets wet, cracks, becomes loose, rubs, smells bad, or has fluid leaking through it. If you have a leg cast, promptly report new calf pain, swelling, warmth, or redness, which can be signs of a blood clot. Seek emergency care for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, or coughing blood, which may be signs that a clot has reached the lungs.
