\chapter{Hysteroscopy}

\section*{Overview}
Hysteroscopy is a procedure that lets a clinician look inside the uterus using a thin telescope passed through the vagina and cervix. It can be used to investigate symptoms and, in some cases, treat a problem during the same procedure. It may be done in an outpatient clinic or an operating room, with different pain-control options.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item hysteroscope with camera and light source
  \item fluid-distension and management system
  \item speculum, graspers, scissors, or biopsy instruments as needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Uterine endoscopy, diagnostic hysteroscopy, operative hysteroscopy

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Hysteroscopy may help investigate abnormal uterine bleeding or a finding on imaging. It can be used to take a sample of the uterine lining, remove a polyp or a fibroid that projects into the cavity, treat scar tissue or a uterine septum in selected cases, or remove an intrauterine device whose threads cannot be seen. It may also be part of an evaluation for infertility or pregnancy loss when the clinician suspects a problem inside the uterine cavity. The procedure is chosen based on the person's symptoms, imaging, and goals.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Hysteroscopy is a diagnostic and sometimes therapeutic procedure. It may be recommended when an examination, ultrasound, or other tests suggest an abnormality inside the uterus or have not explained symptoms. Not every uterine problem requires hysteroscopy; alternatives may include imaging, a lining biopsy, or observation, depending on the reason for evaluation.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician passes a thin telescope through the vagina and cervix into the uterus. Fluid, and less commonly gas, is used to gently open the cavity so the lining can be seen. A small sample may be taken, or instruments may be used to remove a polyp or treat another finding. A simple outpatient examination may take only a few minutes; treatment of a larger finding takes longer. Pain-control options can include taking pain medicine beforehand, local anesthetic, sedation, or general or regional anesthesia. The approach depends on the procedure, setting, and patient preference. You can tell the clinician if you need a pause or want the procedure stopped.

\section*{Preparation}
Instructions depend on the setting and anesthesia. For an outpatient procedure without sedation, fasting may not be required; fasting instructions apply if sedation or anesthesia is planned. Tell the team about medicines, allergies, bleeding problems, possible pregnancy, recent infection, and any prior cervical or uterine procedures. A pregnancy test may be offered or required. If you could become pregnant, ask whether to use contraception or avoid unprotected sex between your last period and the procedure. In some people who have periods, scheduling soon after a period can make it easier to see inside the uterus, but timing is individualized. Ask what pain relief is recommended and whether you need someone to take you home.

\section*{Contraindications}
Hysteroscopy is usually postponed if pregnancy is possible until pregnancy has been excluded, or if there is an active pelvic or cervical infection. A suspected uterine or cervical cancer is a reason for appropriate specialist evaluation; it is not a blanket contraindication to diagnostic hysteroscopy.

\section*{Precautions}
Bleeding, cervical narrowing, uterine anatomy, and medical conditions affecting anesthesia may require a different plan. If the procedure is too painful or the cervix cannot be entered safely, it may be stopped and another approach discussed.

\section*{Risks}
Cramping or pain during or after the procedure is common and can be intense for some people. Other risks include feeling faint or nauseated, vaginal bleeding, infection, injury to the cervix or a hole in the uterine wall (perforation). Perforation is uncommon and may require observation or further treatment. During operative hysteroscopy, fluid used to open the uterus can rarely enter the circulation in excessive amounts or cause a change in blood salts; the team monitors fluid use to reduce this risk. Risks can differ between a brief outpatient examination and a longer procedure under anesthesia.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Mild cramping and light bleeding or spotting may continue for several days and sometimes up to a week. Most people return to usual activities the same day after an uncomplicated outpatient procedure, but recovery may take longer after anesthesia or operative treatment. Use pads rather than tampons while bleeding, and avoid penetrative sex or putting anything in the vagina until bleeding has stopped or for the period specified by the care team. Follow instructions about pain relief and activity.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The clinician may be able to discuss what was seen during the procedure. If a tissue sample or removed growth is sent to a laboratory, the final result takes longer and should be reviewed with the ordering clinician. Follow-up and further treatment depend on the findings and pathology results. A normal-looking cavity does not answer every question about fertility, miscarriage, or bleeding.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no single expected finding. The procedure may show a normal uterine cavity or identify a polyp, a fibroid projecting into the cavity, scar tissue, or another change. Seeing the openings of the fallopian tubes during hysteroscopy does not by itself prove that the tubes are open.

\section*{Follow-up}
Ask when and how you will receive any biopsy results. Additional care may include no further treatment, medication, repeat imaging, or another procedure, depending on the findings and symptoms.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the care team urgently for bleeding that becomes heavy or does not settle, worsening abdominal pain that is not controlled by the recommended medicine, fever, chills, or foul-smelling vaginal discharge. Seek emergency care for fainting, severe weakness, or another rapidly worsening symptom.
