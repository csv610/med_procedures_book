\chapter{Bone Marrow Biopsy}

\section*{Overview}
Bone marrow is the soft tissue inside certain bones where blood cells are made. A bone marrow aspiration removes a small amount of liquid marrow. A biopsy removes a small solid core of marrow and bone. When a full marrow examination is needed, both samples are usually collected when feasible because they provide different, complementary information. A clinician may order only one for a specific clinical question. The usual site is the back of the pelvic bone, near the hip (the posterior iliac crest).

\section*{Alternative Names}
Bone marrow examination; bone marrow aspiration; trephine biopsy; marrow aspirate and core biopsy

\section*{Principle}
The liquid aspirate shows individual cells and can be used to examine their appearance or run specialized tests. These may include flow cytometry, which identifies cells by their markers, and chromosome or molecular studies, which look for genetic changes. The solid core shows how cells are arranged in the marrow and helps assess marrow cellularity (the balance of blood-forming cells and fat), scarring, and areas of abnormal cells. The aspirate and core provide complementary information. The tests ordered depend on the clinical question and laboratory requirements.

\section*{History}
Bone marrow sampling became an important way to investigate diseases that affect blood-cell production. Current laboratory methods can examine the cells, tissue structure, chromosomes, and specific molecular changes.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
The procedure may help investigate persistent unexplained changes in blood counts or suspected conditions such as leukemia, lymphoma, multiple myeloma, myelodysplastic syndromes (disorders in which the marrow makes blood cells abnormally), or bone marrow failure. It may also help assess marrow scarring, iron stores, selected infections (including some causes of unexplained fever), cancers that have spread to the marrow, or how a blood disorder is responding to treatment. It is used for staging only in selected cancers; many diagnoses can be evaluated with blood tests, imaging, or other procedures instead.

\section*{Role in Diagnosis}
This is a targeted diagnostic procedure, not a screening test. The clinician considers blood tests, symptoms, imaging, and other findings before deciding whether marrow samples are needed. Aspiration and core biopsy provide different information, so the care team selects the sample types and laboratory tests that fit the question. Like any small sample, a marrow sample may not capture disease that affects only a limited area. Sometimes little or no liquid marrow can be drawn (a “dry tap”); the core and other tests may still provide useful information, or the clinician may recommend another sampling plan.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is usually done in a clinic or hospital. Before starting, the team confirms the patient's identity, consent, and sampling plan. The patient lies on their side or stomach. After the skin is cleaned, local anesthetic is injected to numb the skin and tissue over the bone. A sampling needle is passed through the bone into the marrow. If an aspiration is planned, a syringe removes a small amount of liquid marrow; this can cause a brief, sharp pain or pulling sensation in the bone, buttock, or leg even when the area is numb. A separate hollow needle may then be used to remove a small solid core. Pressure and a dressing are applied afterward. Some patients are offered medicine to relax or sedation, depending on their needs and local practice.

\section*{Preparation}
Fasting is not usually needed when only local anesthetic is used, but may be needed if sedation is planned; follow the care team's instructions. Tell the team about blood thinners, aspirin or other medicines that affect bleeding, bleeding problems, allergies, prior pelvic surgery or radiation, and any previous difficulty with the procedure. Do not stop or change a medicine unless the clinician managing it gives you a plan. Ask whether sedation will be used and whether you need someone to take you home.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
The clinician reviews bleeding risk, medicines, blood-test results, anatomy, and the reason for the procedure. A bleeding disorder, low platelet count, or anticoagulant use does not automatically rule out marrow sampling or require the same plan for every patient; the team individualizes precautions and any medicine changes. Skin infection over the planned site may mean choosing another site or delaying the procedure. The posterior pelvic bone is the usual site. A breastbone sample is used only in selected adults and is an aspiration only, not a core biopsy, because the heart and lungs lie close beneath the sternum. It is avoided when myeloma or another bone-resorbing condition may have weakened the bone.

\section*{Risks}
Brief pain during aspiration and soreness or bruising afterward are common. Less common complications include prolonged bleeding, a larger bruise or blood collection under the skin, and infection. A deep bleed in the pelvis or buttock is very rare but can be serious. Serious bleeding is very uncommon overall, but risk can be higher for some people with bleeding problems or medicines that affect clotting.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The care team applies firm pressure and checks that bleeding has stopped before discharge. Keep the dressing in place and dry for the period specified by the team, often about 24 hours. Mild soreness or bruising may last several days and sometimes a week or longer. Use only pain medicine approved by the care team, particularly if bleeding is a concern. Follow instructions about activity and bathing. If sedation was used, do not drive, drink alcohol, or make important decisions until its effects have worn off and the team says it is safe.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The laboratory examines the aspirate and core for findings relevant to the suspected condition. These may include the types and maturity of blood-forming cells, the proportion of marrow cells to fat, abnormal or cancerous cells, and scarring. Iron stores are usually assessed on aspirate material when this test is needed. Additional tests may examine cell markers, chromosomes, or molecular changes. The clinician interprets the results together with blood tests, symptoms, and other findings; no single test result is interpreted in isolation.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no single normal result for every patient. In a healthy marrow, blood-forming cells mature in the expected pattern and there is no unexpected abnormal-cell growth or scarring. The amount of blood-forming tissue normally decreases with age, so cellularity is assessed against the person's age and clinical context rather than a single fixed percentage.

\section*{Follow-up}
Some results are available within days, while specialized tests may take longer. The treating clinician explains the findings, whether additional tests are pending, and what they mean for diagnosis or treatment. Repeat sampling is considered only when it is useful for the person's condition or treatment plan.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
If bleeding starts, press firmly on the dressing without lifting it to check; contact the care team promptly if bleeding continues. Also contact the team for a growing bruise or swelling, worsening pain, fever, increasing redness or warmth, or fluid draining from the site. Seek urgent care for heavy bleeding, severe pain, faintness that does not improve, or new numbness or weakness in a leg.
