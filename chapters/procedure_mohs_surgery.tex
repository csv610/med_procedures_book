\chapter{Mohs Surgery}

\section*{Overview}
Mohs micrographic surgery removes certain skin cancers in thin layers and checks the edges of each layer under a microscope during the same visit. If cancer remains, the surgeon removes more tissue only from the area where cancer cells were found. This allows complete examination of the surgical margins while preserving as much healthy tissue as possible. Mohs is most often used for selected basal cell and squamous cell carcinomas.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Mohs micrographic surgery, Mohs surgery

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Mohs may be recommended when a skin cancer has a higher risk of recurrence, has returned after treatment, has poorly defined edges, or is in an area where preserving tissue matters for appearance or function, such as parts of the face, hands, feet, or genitals. The clinician considers the cancer type, size, location, prior treatment, and other risk features. Mohs is not needed for every skin cancer; standard excision or another treatment may be appropriate.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Mohs surgery treats a skin cancer that has usually already been diagnosed by skin biopsy. The removed tissue is examined during surgery to check the tumor margins. This is different from the original diagnostic biopsy and from sentinel lymph node biopsy, which is used for selected cancers to assess spread.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is usually done in an outpatient office with local anesthetic, so the patient is awake. The surgeon removes the visible cancer with a thin layer of surrounding tissue, marks and maps the specimen, and prepares it for microscopic examination. The patient waits while the Mohs surgeon examines the tissue margins. If cancer cells remain at an edge, the map shows where another thin layer should be removed. These steps are repeated until no cancer is seen at the examined margins. The number of stages and total visit time vary; several hours may be needed.

\section*{Preparation}
Follow the clinic's instructions. Tell the surgeon about allergies, medical conditions, bleeding problems, and all medicines and supplements, especially anticoagulants and antiplatelet medicines. Do not stop prescribed medicines unless the prescribing clinician and surgeon provide a plan. Most procedures use local anesthesia alone, but ask whether fasting or someone to accompany you is needed if sedation is planned. Wear comfortable clothing that allows access to the treatment site.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
The surgeon reviews whether Mohs is suitable for the cancer and whether the patient can tolerate the procedure and positioning. Bleeding risk, infection at the site, medicines, and the tumor's location may affect planning or timing. The treatment plan and wound repair should be discussed in advance when possible.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
Risks include bleeding, bruising, infection, pain, scarring, delayed healing, numbness, and injury to nearby nerves or other structures, depending on the site. A larger-than-expected wound may require a flap, skin graft, healing without stitches, or repair by another specialist. Mohs examines the margins of the removed tissue, but no treatment can guarantee that cancer will never recur or that another skin cancer will not develop.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After all visible cancer has been removed, the wound may be closed with stitches, repaired with a skin flap or graft, or left to heal on its own. The choice depends on wound size, location, and health factors. Follow the surgeon's instructions for dressing changes, cleaning, bathing, pain relief, activity, and stitch removal. Mild soreness, swelling, or bruising can occur. Healing time varies with the repair and body site.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The aim is to remove the cancer while limiting removal of healthy tissue. The surgeon discusses the margin findings and repair plan. If the cancer extends beyond the planned area or another concerning finding is identified, additional treatment or specialist care may be recommended.

\section*{Expected Results}
The examined tissue margins show no remaining cancer cells, and the wound is repaired or allowed to heal. The final scar and recovery depend on the size and location of the cancer, number of stages, and type of reconstruction.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend wound checks and any visit for stitch removal. Continue regular skin examinations at the interval recommended by the dermatologist, since a history of skin cancer increases the chance of developing another one. Use sun protection and report any new, changing, or nonhealing skin lesion.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the surgical team for bleeding that does not stop with the instructed pressure, increasing pain, spreading redness, warmth, pus, fever, or wound separation. Seek urgent care for heavy bleeding, rapidly increasing swelling, fainting, or new weakness or numbness. After surgery near the eye, nose, or mouth, seek urgent help for vision changes, trouble breathing, or rapidly worsening facial swelling.
