\chapter{Implantable Venous Access Port}

\section*{Overview}
An implantable venous access port is a small reservoir placed under the skin and attached to a thin catheter that ends in a large vein near the heart. A trained clinician accesses it through the skin with a special non-coring needle. It can provide repeated access for medicines, chemotherapy, fluids, blood products, nutrition, or blood samples while reducing repeated needle sticks. The port is fully under the skin when it is not being used.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item port reservoir and central venous catheter kit
  \item ultrasound and fluoroscopy or ECG tip-confirmation equipment
  \item sterile drapes, guidewire, and tunneling instruments
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Implanted port, central venous port, subcutaneous port, Port-a-Cath

\section*{Principle}
The catheter carries fluid from the port into a large central vein. The port has a self-sealing top that can be punctured repeatedly with the correct needle. Ports may be placed in the chest or upper arm, and designs differ. For high-pressure contrast injection, both the port and the needle used to access it must be labeled for power injection; staff must follow the device-specific pressure and flow limits.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A port may be recommended when someone needs repeated or longer-term access to a vein, such as for chemotherapy, certain IV medicines, nutrition, blood products, or repeated blood draws. It may be useful when regular IV placement is difficult or when a medicine could damage smaller veins if it leaks outside the vein. The care team weighs the benefits against the risks and alternatives, such as a PICC or another central line.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Port placement is a procedure to create durable venous access, not a diagnostic test or treatment for the underlying illness. It is chosen when repeated access is expected and a port is suitable for the planned treatment.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is usually done by an interventional radiologist or surgeon using sterile technique. The skin is numbed with local anesthetic; sedation may also be used. With ultrasound guidance, a vein is accessed, often in the neck or upper chest, and the catheter is guided into a large vein near the heart. The port reservoir is placed under the skin of the chest or, for some devices, the upper arm, and connected to the catheter. Imaging is used during or after placement to check the catheter position and function. The port may be used right away if needed, or after the team confirms it is ready.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews the reason for the port, medical history, allergies, medicines, bleeding risk, and prior central lines or surgery. Tell the team about blood thinners, implanted devices, possible infection, and allergies to anesthetic, contrast, or materials. Instructions about fasting depend on whether sedation is planned. Do not stop medicines unless the prescribing and procedural teams give you a plan. Ask whether you need someone to take you home after sedation.

\section*{Contraindications}
If there is a serious active infection, placement may be delayed or another access plan used.

\section*{Precautions}
An infection at the intended site or in the bloodstream, a clotting problem, altered anatomy, or a blocked vein may change the timing or location of placement. The team selects the vein and port type based on the planned treatment and individual risks. A port is not the best choice for every patient or every treatment.

\section*{Risks}
Possible problems during or shortly after placement include bleeding, bruising, infection, injury to a nearby artery, and (with some chest or neck access routes) a collapsed lung (pneumothorax). The catheter can occasionally be placed incorrectly or cause a temporary irregular heartbeat. Later complications can include infection in the skin or bloodstream, a blood clot in the catheter or vein, blockage, leakage, catheter movement or breakage, and skin irritation over the port. The risk depends on the placement route, the device, and the person's health.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Mild bruising, swelling, and tenderness are common for a few days. Follow the team's instructions for keeping the incision clean and dry and for when to shower, lift, or exercise. When the port is accessed with a needle, the needle and site are covered by a sterile dressing and cared for by trained staff. When it is not accessed and the incision has healed, no dressing is usually needed. Do not let anyone access or flush the port unless they are trained and following the device's instructions.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
Before use, the team checks that the port is positioned and working as intended. When accessed, staff assess function according to protocol and monitor the site during infusion. Ports can remain in place while needed, sometimes for years, but require regular maintenance. The flush interval varies by device and local protocol; confirm the date of the next flush with the care team rather than relying on a general schedule. The port is usually removed when it is no longer needed or if a complication requires removal.

\section*{Expected Results}
The port should allow the planned medicine or fluid to be delivered through central venous access. It may also be used for blood draws when the specific port and clinical circumstances allow. A port reduces repeated needle placements but does not eliminate infection or clot risk.

\section*{Follow-up}
Keep port-flushing and treatment appointments. Carry the port identification card if one is provided, especially if it is approved for power injection. Tell clinicians that you have a port before scans or procedures, and ask whether the device can be used for that purpose.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the care team promptly for fever or chills, increasing pain, redness, warmth, swelling, bruising, drainage, or an incision that opens. New swelling of the arm, shoulder, neck, or face on the port side can be a sign of a blood clot and needs prompt assessment. Tell infusion staff immediately if fluid causes burning, pain, or swelling around the port. Seek emergency care for sudden chest pain, trouble breathing, fainting, or another severe symptom. Do not force a flush if the port is difficult to use.
