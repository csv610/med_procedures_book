\chapter{Breast Biopsy}

\section*{Overview}
A breast biopsy removes cells or tissue from an area of concern so it can be examined in a laboratory. It may help explain a lump, an imaging finding, or another breast change. Most biopsies use a needle guided by ultrasound, mammography, or MRI. Some require surgery. A biopsy can find cancer, a noncancerous condition, or a high-risk change that may need more assessment.

A biopsy samples only part of an area. The result must be checked against the breast examination and imaging to confirm that the sample explains the finding. A benign result does not always end the evaluation.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Breast tissue biopsy; fine-needle aspiration; core needle biopsy; vacuum-assisted breast biopsy; ultrasound-guided biopsy; stereotactic or tomosynthesis-guided biopsy; MRI-guided biopsy; surgical or excisional biopsy.

\section*{How the Biopsy Works}
A clinician uses a needle or surgical instrument to collect cells or tissue from the target area. A pathologist examines the sample under a microscope. Imaging guidance helps place the needle in the correct area. A small marker clip may be left at the biopsy site so the area can be found later; the clip is not the biopsy sample and usually remains in place.

\section*{History}
Breast biopsy methods have developed from open surgery to include less invasive needle sampling guided by breast imaging. Needle biopsy is commonly used when it can safely and accurately sample the finding; surgical biopsy is still needed in some situations.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A biopsy may be recommended for a suspicious lump or imaging finding, such as a mass, calcifications, or an area of distorted tissue. It may also be used to assess selected nipple or skin changes. The breast imaging and clinical findings help determine whether biopsy is needed and which method is appropriate.

\section*{Role in Diagnosis}
Biopsy is a diagnostic procedure, but the result is interpreted with the examination and imaging. It does not by itself determine the full stage of a cancer or the complete treatment plan.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The method depends on where the target is best seen and what information is needed:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Fine-needle aspiration:} A very thin needle collects fluid or cells, rather than a piece of tissue. It may be used for a fluid-filled area or selected lumps, but it may not provide enough detail for some diagnoses. A core biopsy is often preferred when cancer is suspected.
  \item \textbf{Core needle biopsy:} A hollow needle removes small tissue samples, usually after numbing the skin and breast with local anesthetic. The device may make a clicking sound.
  \item \textbf{Vacuum-assisted biopsy:} A probe uses gentle suction to collect several samples through one small skin opening.
  \item \textbf{Image guidance:} Ultrasound is used when the target is visible on ultrasound; mammography or tomosynthesis can guide sampling of findings such as calcifications; MRI guidance may be used for a target seen only on MRI.
  \item \textbf{Surgical biopsy:} A surgeon removes part or all of the abnormal area, sometimes after a wire or other marker has been placed to guide the operation.
\end{itemize}

For an image-guided needle biopsy, the patient is positioned so the target can be seen. The skin is cleaned and numbed, a small opening may be made, and the needle is guided into the target. Several samples may be taken. Pressure is applied to reduce bleeding, and a dressing is placed. A marker clip may be inserted and checked with imaging. Most needle biopsies are outpatient procedures and do not require general anesthesia.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the care team about medicines and supplements, allergies, bleeding problems, pregnancy or possible pregnancy, and relevant medical conditions. In particular, tell them about anticoagulants, antiplatelet medicines, aspirin, and medicines or supplements that may affect bleeding. Do not stop or change these medicines unless the clinician who manages them and the biopsy team provide a plan; the risks of bleeding and of interrupting treatment are considered individually.

Fasting is usually not needed for a needle biopsy with local anesthetic, but instructions differ if sedation or surgery is planned. For MRI-guided biopsy, the team will check MRI safety and ask about contrast allergies and kidney problems when contrast is used. Tell them if you are or may be pregnant. Gadolinium contrast is generally avoided during pregnancy and is considered only when it is essential, the needed information cannot be obtained another way, and the expected benefit outweighs the unknown risk to the fetus. Wear comfortable clothing and follow the facility's instructions.

If sedation or general anesthesia is planned, arrange for someone to take you home and follow instructions about supervision afterward.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
The team considers whether the target can be safely reached, the risk of bleeding or infection, ability to stay in the required position, pregnancy, allergies, and any imaging-specific precautions. These factors may change the biopsy method or timing; they do not automatically rule out every type of biopsy. If the target cannot be seen or reached reliably on the planned imaging, the team may use another method or recommend follow-up or surgical assessment.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary tenderness, bruising, swelling, and a small amount of bleeding are common. A hematoma (a collection of blood under the skin) can occur. Infection is uncommon. Rarely, a needle biopsy near the chest wall can cause air to collect around the lung (pneumothorax). Surgical biopsy has additional risks related to anesthesia, incision, scarring, and recovery.

Sometimes the sample is too small or does not explain the imaging finding. A false-negative result is possible if the abnormal area was not adequately sampled. The radiologist and pathologist assess whether the result matches the imaging (called imaging-pathology concordance). If it does not match, or if the sample is inadequate or shows a high-risk change, repeat needle biopsy or surgical removal may be recommended.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After a needle biopsy, the site is usually covered with a small dressing. Follow instructions for pressure, ice or a cold pack, pain relief, bathing, and activity. Avoid strenuous activity or heavy lifting for the period advised by the biopsy team; at least 24 hours is commonly advised. Mild bruising and soreness can last several days. A marker clip generally does not need removal.

Recovery from a surgical biopsy takes longer and depends on the size of the incision and anesthesia. The surgical team provides wound-care and activity instructions.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Pathology results may take several days or longer, depending on the tests needed. Ask who will provide the result and when to contact the clinic if no result has arrived. The report may describe a benign condition, a high-risk or atypical change, or cancer. A high-risk change is not necessarily cancer, but some types need closer monitoring, additional sampling, or surgical removal. If cancer is found, additional testing may describe its type, grade, and markers such as estrogen or progesterone receptors and HER2; these help guide treatment but do not alone determine stage.

The clinician checks that pathology explains the imaging finding. When a benign result matches the imaging, routine screening is often enough; certain findings, including some MRI-guided biopsies, may need short-interval imaging follow-up. A discordant or inadequate result may need another biopsy or surgical removal. If cancer is found, the care team discusses further evaluation and treatment options.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the biopsy team for bleeding that does not stop with the instructed pressure, rapidly increasing swelling, worsening pain, spreading redness or warmth, drainage, or fever. Seek urgent care for difficulty breathing or chest pain, which can rarely occur if air collects around the lung.
