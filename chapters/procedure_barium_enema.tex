\chapter{Barium Enema}

\section*{Overview}
A barium enema, also called a lower GI series, is an X-ray examination of the colon and rectum. A liquid contrast material, usually barium, is passed through a short tube into the rectum. X-rays show the shape and inner lining of the large intestine.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Lower GI series; lower GI tract radiography; double-contrast barium enema (a type of examination that also uses air)

\section*{Principle}
Barium coats the inside of the colon so it shows up on X-ray images. The radiology team may add air to expand the colon and make the lining easier to see. This is called a double-contrast examination. If a tear in the bowel is suspected, barium is avoided and the team may use a water-soluble contrast material instead. Water-soluble contrast is used for childhood studies of Hirschsprung disease; the pediatric radiology team chooses the specific product and dilution.

\section*{History}
Barium X-ray examinations became established in the early twentieth century. Newer imaging and endoscopic tests have replaced barium enemas for many uses.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
In selected cases, the examination helps assess the shape or narrowing of the colon, changes after bowel surgery, or symptoms such as a change in bowel habits, constipation, diarrhea, or blood in the stool. It may also check for a leak or assess a bowel join before a temporary stoma is closed. In children, a contrast enema may be used to assess certain bowel problems, including suspected Hirschsprung disease; it can show a change in bowel width that suggests the condition, but rectal biopsy is needed to confirm the diagnosis. A related pediatric contrast enema, usually using air or water-soluble contrast rather than barium, may also be used under imaging guidance to push an intussusception (one part of the bowel sliding into another) back into place. Pediatric radiology teams choose the contrast material and preparation for the specific question. It does not allow the clinician to take tissue samples or remove polyps.

\section*{Current Role}
Barium enemas are used much less often than colonoscopy or CT colonography. They are not recommended for routine colorectal cancer screening; current American College of Radiology guidance rates them as usually not appropriate for screening. A clinician may choose one when another test is unsuitable or when a specific question about the colon's shape needs an X-ray examination. The best test depends on the reason for imaging and the patient's circumstances.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
You lie on an X-ray table, usually on your side. A radiologist or technologist gently places a lubricated tube in the rectum and lets contrast flow into the colon. Air may also be added. The team takes real-time and still X-ray images while you change position; you may be asked to hold your breath briefly. After imaging, most of the contrast drains through the tube. You pass the rest in a toilet, and the team may take a few more images. The examination usually takes about 30 to 60 minutes.

\section*{Preparation}
For most adult lower GI series, the bowel needs to be empty for the images to be clear. Follow the radiology team's instructions about diet, laxatives, or a cleansing enema; the preparation varies. When obstruction or twisting of the colon is suspected, bowel-cleansing preparation is generally omitted. Do not start laxatives or enemas unless the team instructs you to. Some pediatric contrast enemas, including studies for suspected Hirschsprung disease, are done without bowel preparation. For a Hirschsprung study, a recent digital rectal examination should be avoided when possible, and a recent rectal biopsy may affect when the enema is scheduled or how the images are interpreted. Tell the pediatric radiology team about recent examinations, washouts, or biopsies and follow its instructions. Tell the team if you may be pregnant, have severe abdominal pain or recent bowel surgery, or have a known bowel condition. Also report your medicines and any previous reactions to contrast materials. Do not stop or change prescribed medicines unless your clinician tells you to.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Severe acute abdominal pain or a recent colonic or rectal biopsy may require the team to delay or change the examination. Barium enemas are generally avoided in an unstable patient or when bowel perforation, poor blood supply to the bowel, toxic megacolon (dangerous widening of the colon), or peritonitis (inflammation of the lining of the abdomen) is suspected. Suspected colonic obstruction or twisting of the colon (volvulus) needs prompt, individualized imaging; the American College of Radiology recommends water-soluble contrast rather than barium when a contrast enema is chosen. If perforation is suspected, the radiology team may carefully use water-soluble contrast instead of barium. X-rays are generally avoided during pregnancy unless the expected benefit justifies the exposure.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary fullness, cramping, and an urgent need to have a bowel movement are common. The examination uses X-rays, so it exposes the body to radiation; the radiology team limits exposure to what is needed to obtain useful images. Rare complications include a tear in the bowel, inflammation if barium leaks through an unrecognized tear, or a blockage if barium hardens in the bowel. Allergic reactions to barium are extremely rare; reactions to water-soluble iodine-based contrast are also rare.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people can return to their usual diet and activities unless the care team gives different instructions. Stools may look white for a day or two as the barium leaves the body. Drink extra fluids for about a day if your care team has not restricted your fluids. Barium can cause constipation; ask your clinician whether you should use a laxative if stools become hard or difficult to pass.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
A radiologist reviews the images for changes in the shape or width of the colon, narrowing, blockage, pouches, or growths. If an area needs a closer look, follow-up may include colonoscopy, CT, or another test. Colonoscopy can directly examine the lining and take a biopsy or remove a polyp.

\section*{Expected Results}
In a normal examination, contrast outlines the colon and rectum with their usual shape and position, without a blockage or suspicious change.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow-up depends on the reason for the examination and the radiologist's report. Your clinician will explain whether the images answer the original question or whether another test is needed.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact a clinician promptly for severe or worsening abdominal pain, increasing abdominal swelling, fever, or repeated vomiting. Contact the care team if you cannot pass stool or gas, or have no bowel movement for more than two days after the examination. Seek advice if your stool does not return to its usual color after a few days.
