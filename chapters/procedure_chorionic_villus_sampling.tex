\chapter{Chorionic Villus Sampling}

\section*{Overview}
Chorionic villus sampling (CVS) is an optional prenatal diagnostic test. A clinician takes a small sample of chorionic villi, which are part of the placenta, and a laboratory tests the cells for selected chromosome or genetic conditions. CVS is usually performed between 11 weeks and 13 weeks 6 days of pregnancy. The timing may vary by local guidance and the circumstances of the pregnancy. It should not be done before 10 completed weeks.

\section*{Alternative Names}
CVS; chorionic villus biopsy; placental biopsy.

\section*{Principle}
The placenta and fetus usually have the same genetic makeup, so testing placental cells can diagnose many chromosome and inherited genetic conditions. Rarely, some placental cells have a chromosome difference that is not present in the fetus. This is called confined placental mosaicism. If a result is unclear or suggests mosaicism, genetic counseling and a later amniocentesis may be recommended to clarify whether the fetus is affected. CVS does not test for every genetic condition or birth defect, and it does not detect open neural tube defects such as spina bifida; those are assessed later with screening and ultrasound.

\section*{History}
CVS became established as a prenatal diagnostic option in the 1980s. It allows testing earlier in pregnancy than amniocentesis.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
CVS may be offered after a higher-chance prenatal screening result, an ultrasound finding, a known inherited condition in the family, or another reason to assess the pregnancy's genetic makeup. Screening estimates chance; CVS is a diagnostic test for the specific conditions analyzed. Patients can choose screening, diagnostic testing, or no testing after discussing the benefits, limits, timing, and risks with their care team. Maternal age may affect the chance of some chromosome conditions, but age alone does not determine whether someone can be offered testing.

\section*{Role in Care}
CVS is an invasive diagnostic test, usually offered after counseling and often after screening or ultrasound findings. It can provide an earlier answer than amniocentesis, which is generally performed from 15 weeks onward, but it samples placenta rather than amniotic fluid.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
An ultrasound is used to confirm the pregnancy and locate the placenta. The sample is collected either through the abdomen with a thin needle or through the cervix with a narrow catheter. The route depends on the placenta's position, the pregnancy, and the clinician's assessment. The skin is cleaned; local anesthetic may be used for the abdominal route. The clinician guides the needle or catheter with ultrasound and removes a small tissue sample. The procedure itself usually takes only a few minutes, though the appointment takes longer. Some people feel brief cramping or discomfort.

\section*{Preparation}
The care team explains what the test can and cannot detect, alternatives, and the chance of an inconclusive result or pregnancy loss. Bring questions about how results could affect decisions or care. Follow the clinic's instructions about eating, drinking, medicines, and bladder preparation; these vary and fasting is not universally required. Tell the team about medicines, allergies, bleeding problems, possible infection, and your blood group if known. If you are RhD-negative and not already sensitized, anti-D immunoglobulin is generally recommended after CVS to reduce the risk of developing antibodies; follow your care team's instructions and local guidance.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
CVS may not be suitable at a particular time or by a particular route if the placenta cannot be safely reached, the pregnancy is too early, or the person's health or anatomy raises concern. The team may choose another route, delay testing, or discuss amniocentesis instead. Infection risk, bleeding, and blood-borne infections are assessed individually; these do not have one universal rule for every patient or route.

\section*{Risks}
The main serious risk is pregnancy loss. The additional risk is small and depends on the pregnancy, route, and operator's experience. RCOG advises that the added miscarriage risk after CVS performed by a skilled operator is likely below 0.5% for a singleton pregnancy; it estimates around 1% for a twin pregnancy. Local estimates may differ. Temporary cramping or light spotting can occur. Less commonly, there may be infection, heavier bleeding, fluid leakage, or an inadequate sample that requires another test. RhD sensitization is possible in an RhD-negative patient, so follow the care team's advice about anti-D. CVS performed before 10 weeks is associated with a higher risk of limb defects, which is why it is avoided before that time.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the clinic's instructions about activity and when to resume usual activities; recommendations vary. Mild cramping or light spotting may occur. The team will explain when and how results will be shared. Some rapid results are available within a few days, while detailed chromosome or gene analyses can take one to several weeks, depending on the tests ordered and laboratory.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The result applies to the conditions tested in the placental sample. A result showing no tested condition does not rule out every genetic condition, birth defect, or later pregnancy complication. A result suggesting mosaicism or an uncertain finding may lead to genetic counseling, additional testing, or amniocentesis after 15 weeks. Regardless of CVS results, routine prenatal care and the recommended ultrasound assessment for fetal development remain important. Second-trimester screening for open neural tube defects may also be offered.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the maternity or procedure team promptly for heavy or persistent vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from the vagina, fever or chills, worsening or severe abdominal pain, or regular contractions. Seek urgent assessment if symptoms are severe or you feel acutely unwell. Follow the clinic's specific emergency instructions.
