\chapter{Intraosseous Access}

\section*{Overview}
Intraosseous (IO) access places a short catheter through the hard outer layer of a bone into its marrow space. The marrow contains blood vessels that allow emergency medicines and fluids to enter the circulation. IO access is a temporary option when vascular access is urgently needed and a peripheral vein cannot be reached quickly or reliably. It is used by trained clinicians, not as routine long-term access.

\section*{Alternative Names}
IO access; intraosseous line; intraosseous infusion; intraosseous cannulation; bone marrow access

\section*{Principle}
The bone marrow has venous channels that communicate with the circulation. Medicines and fluids can be delivered through a correctly placed IO catheter, including during shock or cardiac arrest when peripheral veins may be difficult to access. Flow may require a flush and pressure; access can still fail or become displaced, so the insertion site must be checked during infusion.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
IO access may be used in a life-threatening emergency when timely IV access cannot be obtained, including resuscitation, severe shock, or major trauma. It can be used in adults and children with age- and device-appropriate equipment. For adult cardiac arrest, current American Heart Association guidance recommends attempting IV access first; IO is a reasonable alternative if IV access is unsuccessful or not feasible. Protocols for other situations and for children may differ.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
IO access is an emergency route for vascular access, usually serving as a bridge until IV or another suitable access is available. It is not a diagnostic test. Some blood tests may be possible from an IO sample, but sample quality and local laboratory policies vary.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The trained clinician selects an appropriate bone and site based on the patient's age, anatomy, injury, and the specific device. Common sites include the upper shinbone and, in suitable patients, the upper arm bone; pediatric sites and device instructions vary. The skin is cleaned and the needle is inserted through the bone cortex into the marrow space. The clinician checks that the catheter is stable and flushes it while watching for swelling, leakage, or other signs of tissue infiltration. Marrow or blood may be aspirated, but inability to aspirate does not by itself mean placement has failed. Once placement is confirmed, the catheter is secured and medicines or fluids are infused according to emergency protocols. Infusion can be painful in a conscious patient; pain control is managed under the applicable protocol.

\section*{Preparation}
Because IO access is generally used in emergencies, there may be little time for formal consent or laboratory testing. The team checks for fractures, injuries, infection, prior IO attempts, or other problems at possible sites and follows the device instructions and local protocol. In a conscious patient, explain the procedure if circumstances allow. Use sterile equipment and technique.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Do not insert through a fracture or into a bone with a recent IO attempt; fluid may leak into surrounding tissue. Avoid a site with local infection or major tissue injury. Previous surgery, a prosthesis, bone disease, or a nearby fracture may make a site unsuitable, depending on the device and clinical circumstances. Choose another approved site when possible. Monitor closely for swelling, increasing pain, leakage, or reduced circulation below the insertion site. Do not use the catheter longer than the device or local protocol allows; establish another access route as soon as practical.

\section*{Risks}
The most important immediate risk is leakage of fluid into surrounding tissues, which can cause tissue damage or compartment syndrome. Other risks include dislodgement, blockage, bleeding, fracture, infection of the skin or bone, and injury to nearby structures. In children, incorrect site selection can injure a growth plate. Pain during infusion can be substantial in an alert patient. Fat or bone-marrow emboli are rare. The underlying emergency also carries serious risks independent of IO access.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The clinician checks the insertion site and the limb beyond it repeatedly during infusion for swelling, firmness, color or temperature change, altered sensation, increasing pain, or weak pulses. Stop the infusion and assess urgently if leakage or compartment syndrome is suspected. IO access is removed once suitable alternative access is available and according to device instructions; it should not be left in place beyond the specified maximum, commonly 24 hours. Cover the site with a clean dressing after removal and monitor for bleeding, pain, or infection.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Successful placement is supported by a stable catheter and a flush that flows without unusual resistance and without swelling or leakage. Aspiration of marrow or blood can support placement, but is not required in every successful insertion. Failure to flush easily, unexpected resistance, swelling, or catheter movement requires stopping use and reassessment; a trained clinician may need to place access at another site. The team transitions to IV or other longer-term access as soon as appropriate.

\section*{Expected Results}
The catheter provides temporary access for emergency medicines or fluids. It does not guarantee that an infusion will flow well or that the patient will respond to treatment; placement and the limb must be monitored throughout use.

\section*{Follow-up}
After removal, reassess the site and limb for persistent pain, swelling, bleeding, reduced movement or sensation, or signs of infection. Suspected fracture, compartment syndrome, or infection requires prompt evaluation. Imaging is not routinely needed to confirm uncomplicated placement, but may be used if a complication or malposition is suspected.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
While the IO is in use, immediately alert the medical team to swelling, leakage, increasing firmness or pain, color or temperature change, numbness, weakness, or a weak pulse in the limb. After removal, seek prompt medical care for worsening pain or swelling, redness, warmth, drainage, fever, reduced movement or sensation, or bleeding that does not stop with pressure.
