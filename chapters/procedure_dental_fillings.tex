\chapter{Dental Fillings}

\section*{Overview}
A dental filling repairs a tooth with a small or moderate area of damage, often from tooth decay. The dentist removes damaged tooth structure as needed and places a restorative material to rebuild the tooth. A filling restores the shape and chewing surface, but it does not make the tooth immune to future decay or fracture.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Dental restoration; tooth filling; composite (tooth-colored) filling; dental amalgam (silver-colored) filling.

\section*{Principle}
The dentist removes decay or damaged tooth structure and fills the space with a material chosen for the tooth and the patient's needs. Common materials include composite resin, glass ionomer, and dental amalgam. The choice depends on the size and location of the damage, chewing forces, appearance, allergies, availability, and cost. Large areas of damage may need an inlay, onlay, or crown instead of a direct filling.

\section*{History}
People have repaired teeth for thousands of years using a range of materials. Dental amalgam became widely used in the 19th century, and tooth-colored resin materials developed later.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A filling may repair a cavity, a small chip or fracture, or worn tooth structure. The dentist chooses treatment after examining the tooth and, when needed, reviewing dental X-rays. Not every early area of decay needs drilling; some lesions may be managed with fluoride, diet changes, and monitoring, depending on their depth and risk of progression.

\section*{Role in Care}
A filling is restorative treatment, not a diagnostic test. The dental examination and any indicated X-rays help determine whether treatment is needed and how extensive it should be.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The dentist may use local anesthetic to numb the tooth and surrounding area. They remove decay or damaged tissue, clean and prepare the area, then place and shape the filling. For a deep cavity, the dentist may remove less tissue near the pulp (the tooth's soft center, which contains nerves and blood vessels) to reduce the chance of exposing it, then seal the tooth with the filling. Composite resin is applied in layers and hardened with a curing light. Other materials are mixed or placed according to their instructions. The dentist checks the bite and adjusts the filling if needed. If decay is very deep, additional treatment to protect or treat the pulp may be needed; a filling alone may not be enough.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the dentist about medical conditions, allergies, pregnancy, and medicines. Discuss the proposed material, its benefits and limits, and alternatives. The U.S. FDA advises higher-risk groups to avoid new dental amalgam fillings when possible and appropriate for the tooth: pregnant people or those planning pregnancy, people who are breastfeeding, infants and young children (especially those under age six), and people with certain neurological conditions, impaired kidney function, or a known allergy or sensitivity to amalgam. The dentist can help weigh suitable alternatives for the tooth. An intact amalgam filling generally should not be removed solely to reduce mercury exposure, because removal can temporarily increase exposure to mercury vapor and removes healthy tooth structure.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
There is no single filling material that is right for every tooth. A tooth with extensive damage, a crack affecting its strength, or infection of the pulp may need other treatment, such as a crown, root canal treatment, or extraction. Tell the dentist about a prior reaction to dental materials or local anesthetic. The dentist will consider the tooth's condition and the patient's health when choosing care.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary sensitivity to temperature, pressure, or sweet foods can occur. The tooth may feel uneven if the filling is high and may need adjustment. A filling can wear, crack, loosen, or develop decay around its edge. Deep decay may inflame or infect the pulp, sometimes requiring later root canal treatment or extraction. Allergic reactions to filling materials are uncommon.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the dentist's instructions. If local anesthetic was used, avoid chewing and hot foods or drinks until normal feeling returns so you do not bite or burn your cheek, tongue, or lip. Most modern fillings allow eating once numbness wears off, but some materials need extra time to set; follow the instructions you are given. Continue brushing with fluoride toothpaste and cleaning between teeth. Mild sensitivity should improve; contact the dentist if it is severe, worsens, or persists.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The dentist checks the filling's shape and bite before you leave. Contact the dental office if the bite feels high, the filling comes out, or pain does not settle. Follow-up examinations and X-rays are based on clinical need and risk; they are not automatically required at every visit.

\section*{Expected Results}
The repaired tooth should have a comfortable shape for chewing. The filling can help preserve the tooth, but its durability varies with material, size, location, bite forces, and oral care. Good home care and risk-based dental visits help lower the chance of additional decay.

\section*{Follow-up}
Keep routine dental appointments at the interval recommended for your oral health. The dentist may monitor the filling and surrounding tooth over time and recommend repair or replacement if it becomes damaged or decay develops.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact a dentist for severe or worsening tooth pain, pain on biting, a filling that breaks or comes out, or swelling around the tooth. Seek urgent dental or medical care for facial swelling that is spreading, fever with dental swelling, or difficulty swallowing or breathing.
