\chapter{Canalith Repositioning}

\section*{Overview}
Canalith repositioning is a series of head and body movements used to treat benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV). The Epley maneuver is commonly used for BPPV affecting the posterior semicircular canal. It uses gravity to move tiny inner-ear particles out of the canal so they no longer trigger brief episodes of vertigo with certain head movements. Other canal patterns may need a different maneuver.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item examination table or adjustable treatment bed
  \item video goggles or eye-movement recording equipment when assessment is needed
  \item pillows or positioning supports
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Canalith repositioning procedure (CRP); particle repositioning maneuver; Epley maneuver. Other maneuvers include the Semont and horizontal-canal repositioning maneuvers.

\section*{Principle}
In the most common form of BPPV, tiny calcium-containing particles (otoconia) become displaced from their usual location and move into a semicircular canal. Some BPPV patterns may involve particles attaching to the canal's motion-sensing structure. Head movement can then cause abnormal signals about rotation, producing spinning sensations and involuntary, rhythmic eye movements (nystagmus). Specific maneuvers can move the particles out of the affected canal or, in some patterns, free them from the motion-sensing structure.

\section*{History}
John Epley published the canalith repositioning procedure in 1992. The Dix-Hallpike test, used to diagnose posterior-canal BPPV, was described by Margaret Dix and Charles Hallpike in 1952.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
The Epley maneuver is used for posterior-canal BPPV, which often causes brief episodes of spinning when lying back, rolling in bed, looking up, or bending over. A clinician confirms the likely affected side using the history and positional testing. For posterior-canal BPPV, the Dix--Hallpike test typically provokes brief vertigo with characteristic twisting, upward-beating eye movements. If symptoms suggest horizontal-canal BPPV, a supine roll test and a different repositioning maneuver may be needed.

\section*{Role in Care}
This is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. The Dix-Hallpike test helps diagnose posterior-canal BPPV; the Epley maneuver treats it. The maneuver can also be performed after a diagnosis has been established if symptoms recur. Medicines that suppress dizziness, such as some antihistamines or benzodiazepines, do not reposition the particles and are not routinely recommended to treat BPPV.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
For a typical right posterior-canal Epley maneuver, the patient starts sitting on an examination table. The clinician turns the head about 45 degrees toward the affected ear, then helps the patient lie back with the head supported and slightly extended. Once the vertigo and eye movements settle, the head is turned toward the other side. The patient then rolls onto that side, with the face angled toward the floor, before being helped to sit up. Each position is held until symptoms and eye movements settle, often for about 30 seconds or longer. The sequence is mirrored for the left ear.

The clinician may repeat the maneuver or use a modified technique if needed. A different maneuver is used for some other BPPV patterns. The patient may briefly feel spinning or nausea during position changes.

\section*{Preparation}
No fasting or special medicine preparation is usually needed. Wear comfortable clothing. Tell the clinician about neck or back problems, limited movement, recent injury or surgery, vascular conditions, retinal problems, severe nausea, or difficulty lying back or getting up. These may require a modified position, extra support, or another treatment. The clinician should also consider balance, mobility, and fall risk.

\section*{Contraindications}
The standard maneuver involves turning and extending the neck and moving between lying and sitting.

\section*{Precautions}
Significant neck or back disease, restricted mobility, recent surgery or injury, or some vascular or eye conditions may make the usual positions difficult or unsafe. These are reasons for individual assessment and possible modification, not automatic exclusions in every case. A clinician can use a side-lying diagnostic test or modified repositioning approach when appropriate.

\section*{Risks}
The maneuver can briefly trigger vertigo, nausea, vomiting, imbalance, or anxiety. Some people feel mildly unsteady after the spinning stops. Rarely, particles may move into another canal, causing a different positional pattern that can be treated with an appropriate maneuver. Falls are possible if a person stands or walks while still dizzy.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Routine restrictions on head movement, sleeping position, or posture are not recommended after the Epley maneuver for posterior-canal BPPV. Sit up and stand slowly, and wait for significant dizziness to settle before walking. Avoid driving, climbing, or other activities where a brief spell could cause injury while symptoms persist. Follow any individualized instructions from the treating clinician.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
Symptoms often improve after one or more maneuvers, but they may not resolve immediately and can return later. The clinician may repeat positional testing or reassess symptoms. Patients should be reassessed within a month after treatment or observation to check whether symptoms have resolved. If brief position-triggered vertigo persists, the clinician checks for unresolved BPPV, the affected canal or side, or another inner-ear or neurologic cause. Imaging is not routinely needed when the presentation and examination meet BPPV criteria and there are no concerning atypical findings.

Some patients can be taught a home Epley maneuver after the affected side and diagnosis are clear. People with neck or back limitations, balance problems, or a risk of falling should get clinician guidance before attempting it alone. BPPV may recur and can often be treated again.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek emergency care for dizziness with new weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, double vision, fainting, a severe new headache, chest pain, or inability to walk safely. Contact a clinician if vertigo is continuous rather than brief and position-triggered, symptoms worsen, or the maneuver repeatedly fails to help.
