\chapter{Anoscopy}

\section*{Overview}
Anoscopy is an examination of the anus, anal canal, and a short section of the lower rectum using a short, rigid tube called an anoscope. It is usually done in a clinic without sedation and can help investigate symptoms such as anal pain or bleeding. It does not examine the whole rectum or colon.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item short rigid anoscope with light source
  \item gloves, lubricant, and examination light
  \item biopsy forceps and specimen container when sampling is planned
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Anal canal examination. High-resolution anoscopy (HRA) is a related, specialized examination that uses magnification to find suspicious areas; a biopsy can then test the tissue for abnormal cells. Proctoscopy uses a longer instrument to examine farther into the rectum.

\section*{Principle}
The clinician inspects the skin around the anus and may first perform a digital rectal examination (DRE), using a gloved, lubricated finger to feel for abnormalities, if it is safe and tolerable. The lubricated anoscope is gently inserted with its rounded obturator in place. The obturator is removed so the clinician can inspect the lining while slowly withdrawing the instrument. If needed, a biopsy or a separate treatment may be performed through the anoscope after discussion and consent.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Anoscopy may help evaluate anal pain, itching, discharge, a lump, or bleeding. It can identify internal hemorrhoids, anal canal inflammation, ulcers, warts, polyps, or a suspicious area. For anal-cancer prevention, screening may include an anal swab to look for cell changes (anal cytology) and/or high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV). An abnormal screening result may lead to high-resolution anoscopy (HRA), a magnified examination that helps identify suspicious areas for biopsy. Screening is not recommended for everyone. The 2024 International Anal Neoplasia Society guidance recommends offering screening to some higher-risk groups:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Men who have sex with men and transgender women with HIV, starting at age 35.
  \item Other people with HIV, and men who have sex with men and transgender women without HIV, starting at age 45.
  \item People who have received a solid-organ transplant, starting 10 years after the transplant.
  \item People with HPV-associated high-grade vulvar precancer or vulvar cancer, starting within a year of diagnosis.
\end{itemize}
These screening pathways assume access to HRA so that abnormal results can be evaluated. If HRA is not available, IANS recommends a digital anorectal examination (DARE)—a gloved-finger examination of the anus and lower rectum—to look for a palpable cancer. A DARE is also recommended at screening visits for people at increased risk.
For people aged 45 or older with other risk factors, screening may be considered through shared decision-making when timely HRA is available. These factors include a history of high-grade cervical or vaginal precancer or cancer, anal-area warts, persistent cervical HPV-16 infection, or selected autoimmune conditions. The IANS guideline gives rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, and Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis treated with systemic medicines as examples. Recommendations and access vary by location. During a standard anoscopy visit, the clinician may also inspect the skin around the anus for external hemorrhoids, fissures, or other visible problems. A biopsy may be taken from a suspicious area. Anoscopy may be one part of assessing a possible abscess or fistula, but it does not always establish the diagnosis or replace other examination or imaging.

\section*{Role in Care}
An office examination that adds direct viewing to the medical history, external inspection, and sometimes a DRE. It is particularly useful for viewing internal hemorrhoids and other changes in the anal canal. It is not a substitute for proctoscopy (examination farther into the rectum), sigmoidoscopy, or colonoscopy when a deeper examination is needed.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The patient usually lies on their side with knees bent, though another position may be used. The clinician explains each step, offers a chaperone for this intimate examination, and inspects the area around the anus. If appropriate and tolerable, a digital rectal examination is done first. The lubricated anoscope is gently inserted a few centimeters with its rounded inner insert (obturator) in place; the insert is then removed. The clinician inspects the lining as the anoscope is slowly withdrawn. A small tissue sample may be taken if needed; this can cause a brief pinch and slight bleeding. High-resolution anoscopy uses magnification and may apply a solution such as dilute acetic acid to make abnormal areas easier to see. It is performed by a clinician with specific training, often after an abnormal anal screening result.

\section*{Preparation}
Usually no bowel preparation is needed. The clinic may ask the patient to have a bowel movement or empty the bladder beforehand; an enema or other preparation is used only if specifically instructed. For high-resolution anoscopy, the clinic may also ask the patient to avoid anal medicines, other products, or anal sex for 24 hours beforehand. Tell the clinician about severe anal pain, recent anorectal surgery, bleeding problems, or medicines that affect bleeding. Do not stop prescribed medicines unless the clinician tells you to. Sedation is usually unnecessary, although local anesthetic may be used, particularly if a biopsy is planned.

\section*{Contraindications}
The clinician may postpone or modify the examination if it would be too painful or difficult, such as with a severe anal fissure, a very painful thrombosed hemorrhoid (a hemorrhoid with a blood clot), marked narrowing of the anal canal, or recent surgery. Suspected infection is not by itself a reason to avoid anoscopy; the clinician chooses the appropriate examination and tests based on the suspected problem.

\section*{Precautions}
Heavy active bleeding or signs of instability require urgent assessment and stabilization; anoscopy may be considered as part of the evaluation when appropriate. If the examination cannot be tolerated, the clinician can discuss other tests or an examination with anesthesia.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary pressure, discomfort, or the feeling of needing to pass stool is common. Minor bleeding can occur, especially after a biopsy or when hemorrhoids are present, and mild soreness or bleeding may last for a few days. Serious complications are uncommon. The risks differ if a separate treatment, such as hemorrhoid banding, is done at the same visit.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people can return to usual activities soon afterward. Mild soreness or a small amount of bleeding may last for a few days, especially after biopsy. Follow any separate instructions if a biopsy or treatment was performed; after biopsy, the clinician may advise avoiding anal insertion until bleeding and pain have stopped.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The clinician discusses visible findings, such as internal hemorrhoids, inflammation, ulcers, or growths. A suspicious area may need a biopsy, which is examined in a laboratory; visual appearance alone cannot confirm cancer. Finding hemorrhoids does not prove that they are the only cause of bleeding, and a normal anoscopy does not rule out disease higher in the rectum or colon.

\section*{Expected Results}
The lining of the anal canal and the portion of lower rectum seen appear normal, with no visible abnormal tissue or bleeding source. A normal result applies only to the area examined.

\section*{Follow-up}
Further tests, such as sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy, may be recommended to investigate bleeding based on its pattern, other symptoms, and personal or family history. Routine colorectal cancer screening is also needed when due. Anoscopy alone cannot check for a source of bleeding higher in the bowel. Repeat anoscopy or high-resolution anoscopy may be used when follow-up of a known lesion or abnormal cells is needed.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the clinic for bleeding that is more than light spotting, bleeding that continues or worsens, increasing pain, fever, or signs of infection. Seek urgent medical care for heavy bleeding, severe pain, fainting, or feeling very unwell. Follow any procedure-specific instructions, especially if a biopsy or treatment was performed.
