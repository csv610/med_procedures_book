\chapter{Laser Skin Treatment}

\section*{Overview}
Laser skin treatment uses a focused beam of light to target a specific structure in the skin or to resurface part of the skin. Different devices and settings are used for different goals. Some lasers remove or heat columns of skin to improve texture and scars; others target blood vessels, pigment, or hair while leaving more of the surface intact. The right treatment and expected recovery depend on the diagnosis, skin characteristics, device, and treatment depth.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item procedure-specific laser and handpiece
  \item protective eyewear for patient and staff
  \item cooling, smoke evacuation, and skin-protection supplies as appropriate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Laser dermatologic treatment, laser resurfacing, vascular laser treatment, pigment laser treatment

\section*{Principle}
Laser light is absorbed by a chosen target, such as water in skin, melanin pigment, or hemoglobin in blood. The resulting heat or tissue removal is intended to affect that target. Ablative lasers remove tissue; nonablative lasers heat tissue without removing the full surface layer. Fractional devices treat microscopic areas separated by untreated skin. These approaches are not interchangeable, and intense pulsed light (IPL) is a light treatment but is not a laser.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Depending on the specific device, laser treatment may be used for selected acne scars, wrinkles and sun-related skin changes, visible blood vessels or vascular birthmarks, unwanted hair, some pigmented lesions, or tattoos. A clinician should first identify what the skin finding is; not every spot or lesion is suitable for laser treatment. Results vary, and some conditions need several sessions or another treatment.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Laser treatment is a procedure, not a diagnostic test. A skin examination and medical history help determine whether laser is appropriate and which device and settings may be used. A suspicious or changing lesion may need examination or biopsy rather than cosmetic laser treatment.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician cleans the treatment area and provides eye protection for the patient and staff. Depending on the device and area, cooling, topical numbing medicine, local anesthetic, or another pain-control method may be used. The operator directs laser pulses at the treatment area. A session may last minutes or longer depending on the size and type of treatment. Ablative resurfacing may create open areas that need wound care; nonablative or targeted treatment may cause temporary redness, swelling, or darkening of the treated area.

\section*{Preparation}
The clinician should review the treatment goal, skin type and prior pigment changes, tendency to scar or form keloids, history of cold sores, recent tanning or sun exposure, medical conditions, and all medicines and skin products. Avoid tanning and protect the area from sun as instructed; postpone treatment if the skin is sunburned or recently tanned when that increases risk. Instructions to pause retinoids or other products vary by procedure. Tell the clinician about current or recent isotretinoin use; a fixed waiting period is not appropriate for every laser or procedure, and the risk should be assessed for the specific treatment. Evidence does not support delaying some superficial and nonablative procedures solely because of isotretinoin use, while fully ablative resurfacing requires greater caution and is generally not recommended during systemic isotretinoin treatment. Antiviral medicine may be prescribed for some people with a history of herpes simplex, especially before resurfacing around the mouth. Do not stop prescribed medicines without medical advice.

\section*{Contraindications}
Active infection, open wounds, or inflamed skin in the treatment area may require postponement.

\section*{Precautions}
Pregnancy, eye conditions, and other medical factors may also affect whether an elective treatment is suitable. Recent tanning, medicines that increase light sensitivity, a history of abnormal scarring, and conditions that affect healing may change the risks or choice of treatment. Darker skin tones can be treated with lasers, but some devices and settings carry a greater risk of burns or lasting dark or light marks; an experienced clinician should select and test an appropriate approach. These considerations are not a universal checklist; the clinician should assess the person and device individually.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
Temporary redness, swelling, tenderness, crusting, or peeling may occur. Depending on the laser and skin, complications can include burns, infection, cold-sore reactivation, scarring, and skin becoming darker or lighter than before. Eye injury is possible without appropriate protective eyewear. Ablative resurfacing generally causes more wound care and recovery than nonablative treatment. Risk varies with the device, energy settings, operator experience, skin type, and aftercare.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the treating clinician's instructions, which differ for targeted, nonablative, and resurfacing treatments. Keep treated skin clean and moisturized as directed; do not pick scabs or peeling skin. Protect healing skin from sun with shade, clothing, and the recommended broad-spectrum sunscreen once appropriate for the treated area. Avoid products or activities that irritate the skin until it has healed. Redness, swelling, and recovery time depend on treatment depth and may last longer after ablative resurfacing. More than one session may be needed, with healing time between sessions.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The clinician will explain when to expect a result and whether repeat treatment is planned. Improvement may be gradual and incomplete. Pigment can recur, scars may improve without disappearing, and some conditions respond better to other treatments.

\section*{Expected Results}
Results are specific to the condition and device. The goal may be reduced appearance of a scar, vessel, pigment, hair, or skin texture change; smooth or flawless skin is not guaranteed. Some treatments require several sessions, and the response can differ from one person to another.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend the recommended review and complete the wound-care plan. Contact the clinician if the treated area is not healing as expected or if the intended result is unclear. A new or changing skin lesion should be medically assessed rather than repeatedly treated for appearance.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the treating clinician promptly for worsening pain, spreading redness or warmth, pus, fever, new blisters, or unexpected pigment changes. Seek urgent care for severe burns, eye pain or vision changes, rapidly increasing swelling, or signs of a serious allergic reaction or infection.
