\chapter{Botulinum Toxin Injection}

\section*{Overview}
Botulinum toxin injections use a purified medicine to temporarily reduce activity in selected muscles or glands. They are used for some movement and muscle disorders, chronic migraine, excess sweating, bladder problems, and cosmetic concerns. The product, dose, injection sites, and expected benefit depend on the condition. Not every product is approved for every use, and approval varies by country.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item small-volume syringe and fine needle
  \item skin-marking pen and antiseptic swabs
  \item ultrasound or electromyography guidance in selected cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Botulinum neurotoxin injection; botulinum toxin type A or type B injection; chemodenervation; botulinum toxin treatment. Botox is a brand name for one product, not a name for all products.

\section*{Principle}
Botulinum toxin blocks the release of acetylcholine, a chemical signal that helps nerves activate muscles and some glands. This reduces activity in the treated area for a limited time. The effect is temporary as nerve signaling recovers. The amount and duration of benefit vary by product, condition, dose, and person.

\section*{History}
Botulinum toxin was first developed as a treatment for certain eye-muscle disorders and involuntary eyelid closure. Its medical uses have expanded, and some formulations are also approved for cosmetic treatment.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Depending on the specific product and local approval, injections may be used for conditions such as cervical dystonia (involuntary neck-muscle contractions), spasticity (muscle stiffness), chronic migraine, overactive bladder or some bladder problems caused by nervous system disorders, severe underarm sweating, strabismus (misaligned eyes), or blepharospasm (involuntary eyelid closure). They may also be used to temporarily improve certain facial lines. Some uses are off-label, meaning the regulator has not approved that product for that specific use. The clinician should explain the expected benefit and alternatives for the intended treatment.

\section*{Role in Treatment}
This is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. It may be one part of care, alongside rehabilitation, medicines, or other treatment, depending on the condition.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
A trained clinician selects the injection sites and product based on the treatment goal. The skin is cleaned, and the medicine is injected with a small needle into a muscle, skin, or other target tissue. The clinician may use anatomical landmarks, electrical muscle testing, or ultrasound to guide injections, especially when the target is deep or difficult to locate. For bladder treatment, a clinician usually passes a thin viewing instrument (cystoscope) through the urethra into the bladder and injects the medicine into the bladder muscle; numbing medicine or sedation may be used. The visit is usually brief; sedation is not usually needed for routine injections.

Botulinum toxin products have different formulations and product-specific dosing. Their units are not interchangeable and must not be converted from one product to another. Dosing and injection schedules are specific to the condition and product.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the clinician about all medicines and supplements, recent or planned botulinum toxin treatment, allergies, pregnancy or breastfeeding, and any history of swallowing or breathing problems, muscle weakness, or nerve or muscle disease. Medicines such as aminoglycoside antibiotics, muscle relaxants, and other drugs that affect nerve-to-muscle signaling may increase the effect; the clinician may need to adjust monitoring or timing. Do not stop a medicine without medical advice. Fasting is generally not needed for routine injections, but follow the clinic's instructions.

Ask which product will be used and whether it is approved for the intended use by the regulator where you live. Use a trained, appropriately licensed health professional and a product obtained through an authorized, regulated source. The FDA and CDC have warned that counterfeit or unapproved botulinum toxin products have caused serious illness.

\section*{Contraindications}
The injection should not be given to someone with a known hypersensitivity to a botulinum toxin product or one of its ingredients, or an infection at the planned injection site. For example, under the U.S. label for onabotulinumtoxinA (BOTOX), injection into the bladder is contraindicated when a person has a urinary tract infection or urinary retention (difficulty emptying the bladder).

\section*{Precautions}
A nerve or muscle disorder, pre-existing swallowing or breathing difficulty, or frailty may increase the risk of clinically important weakness and requires careful assessment. Pregnancy and breastfeeding should be discussed because safety information may be limited. The clinician also checks prior treatment and the timing of any other botulinum toxin injections. Before bladder treatment, the team checks whether the person can and is willing to use a catheter if needed and explains product-specific requirements.

\section*{Risks}
Common effects depend on where and why the injections are given. They may include temporary pain, bruising, headache, or weakness in nearby muscles. Depending on the site, complications may include a drooping eyelid, dry eye, neck weakness, or difficulty swallowing. Bladder injections can cause urinary retention (difficulty emptying the bladder) or urinary tract infection; some patients need temporary catheterization and follow-up checks of the urine left in the bladder after urinating. Rarely, a serious allergic reaction can occur, with hives, swelling, or difficulty breathing.

Rarely, toxin effects can spread beyond the injection area. This may cause generalized weakness, double or blurred vision, drooping eyelids, hoarse or slurred speech, difficulty swallowing, or difficulty breathing. These symptoms can begin hours to weeks after treatment and may be life-threatening. The risk deserves particular attention in people with conditions that already affect swallowing, breathing, nerves, or muscles.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Most people return to usual activities soon after a routine injection, but instructions depend on the injection site and reason for treatment. The treated area may be sore or bruised. The clinician will explain when to expect a response and whether or when another appointment is needed. Do not arrange repeat treatment earlier than advised; the appropriate interval varies by product and indication.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The clinician assesses whether the treatment improved the intended symptom or function and whether side effects occurred. Benefit often develops over several days, but onset and peak effect vary. Effects commonly wear off over a few months, though duration differs by indication and individual. After onabotulinumtoxinA bladder injections for overactive bladder in adults, the U.S. product label recommends checking the amount of urine left after urinating (post-void residual) within 2 weeks and periodically, as medically appropriate, for up to 12 weeks in patients who do not already use a catheter. Follow the specific product's instructions, and contact the clinician promptly for difficulty urinating. If the response is inadequate or causes unwanted weakness, the clinician may reconsider the dose, target, technique, or treatment plan. Loss of benefit does not by itself prove antibody resistance.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Get emergency medical help right away for new difficulty breathing, swallowing, or speaking; swelling of the face, tongue, or throat; or rapidly worsening weakness. Seek urgent same-day medical assessment for new generalized weakness, double or blurred vision, drooping eyelids, a marked voice change, widespread hives, or another significant unexpected symptom, even if it begins days or weeks after injection; these may be signs of a serious allergic reaction or that the toxin is affecting areas beyond the injection site.
