\chapter{Endometrial Ablation}

\section*{Overview}
Endometrial ablation treats heavy menstrual bleeding by destroying or removing most of the lining inside the uterus (the endometrium). It does not remove the uterus and usually does not change hormone levels. Bleeding often becomes lighter, but it may not stop completely, and some people need additional treatment later. Ablation is for people who do not want a future pregnancy; it is not a form of birth control.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Ablation of the endometrium; endometrial resection; global endometrial ablation. NovaSure is a brand of radiofrequency ablation device.

\section*{Principle}
Energy or another method destroys the endometrial lining so it heals with scar tissue. Methods include radiofrequency, heated fluid or balloon, microwave energy, freezing, and electrical resection. The device and technique used depend on the uterus and the clinician's assessment.

\section*{History}
Endometrial ablation techniques have developed from hysteroscopic surgery to newer devices that treat the uterine cavity without removing the uterus.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
The procedure may be offered to treat heavy menstrual bleeding after the cause has been assessed and when medicines or other suitable treatments have not helped, are not appropriate, or the person prefers another option after discussing benefits and risks. Options may include a hormonal intrauterine system or medicines such as tranexamic acid, depending on the cause and the person's health and preferences. It may be an alternative to hysterectomy for some people, but it is less definitive: bleeding can continue or return, and further treatment may be needed.

\section*{Role in Care}
Endometrial ablation is treatment, not a test for the cause of bleeding. Before treatment, clinicians evaluate abnormal bleeding and rule out cancer or precancer. Depending on a person's risk factors and local guidance, this may include sampling the uterine lining, often during hysteroscopy. Ultrasound or hysteroscopy may be used to assess the size and shape of the uterine cavity.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is usually done through the vagina and cervix, without an abdominal incision. Depending on the method and setting, it may be done in an office or operating room with local anesthetic, sedation, or general anesthesia. The cervix may be gently widened. The clinician may first look inside the uterus with a hysteroscope, then place an ablation device in the cavity and activate it for the time required by that device. With some methods, a camera and surgical instruments are used to remove or destroy the lining directly. Many people go home the same day.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews the reason for bleeding, pregnancy possibility, health conditions, medicines, allergies, and plans for future pregnancy. Tests may include a pregnancy test, endometrial sampling, ultrasound, or hysteroscopy. An intrauterine device must be removed before ablation. Follow instructions about fasting and medicines if sedation or anesthesia is planned. Tell the clinician if you have a bleeding disorder or a history of uterine surgery. Do not stop prescribed medicines unless the clinician advises you to.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Endometrial ablation is not appropriate during pregnancy or when future pregnancy is desired. Pregnancy after ablation can still occur and carries serious risks, including miscarriage and abnormal attachment of the placenta (placenta accreta), so effective contraception is needed until menopause. Ablation is generally not used for known or suspected endometrial cancer or precancer, active uterine infection, or after menopause. Fibroids, polyps, or an unusual uterine cavity may make a particular method unsuitable or affect the chance of success. The clinician discusses whether another treatment would be safer or more effective.

\section*{Risks}
Possible complications include pain, bleeding, infection, injury or perforation of the uterus, and rare heat injury to nearby organs. Some techniques have risks related to the fluid used to expand the uterus. Later problems can include persistent or returning bleeding, painful periods, or trapped blood behind scar tissue (hematometra). Rarely, scarring can make it harder to evaluate the uterine lining later; new bleeding or pelvic pain after ablation still needs medical assessment.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Cramping and light bleeding or watery, blood-tinged discharge are common for days to several weeks. Nausea or frequent urination may occur briefly, depending on the procedure. Follow the clinician's directions for pain relief, activity, bathing, tampons, swimming, and sex; restrictions vary by method and recovery. Arrange a ride and follow anesthesia precautions if you received sedation or general anesthesia. Continue effective contraception because ablation does not prevent pregnancy.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Bleeding may take several months to settle. Many people have lighter periods, and some stop menstruating, but no result is guaranteed. Ablation does not treat all causes of heavy bleeding and does not prevent pregnancy. The clinician arranges follow-up based on symptoms, test results, and the method used. Persistent or returning bleeding may need further assessment or treatment, including hysterectomy in some cases.

\section*{Expected Results}
The goal is to reduce heavy menstrual bleeding and improve related symptoms. Some menstrual bleeding may continue. The uterus and ovaries remain, and hormone production usually continues.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend follow-up as advised and report bleeding that remains heavy, returns after improving, or changes after menopause. Continue routine pelvic care and cervical cancer screening as recommended. Use reliable contraception until menopause unless the treating clinician gives different advice based on an individual plan.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the treating team for severe or worsening pelvic pain, heavy bleeding, fever or chills, foul-smelling discharge, or difficulty urinating. If pregnancy is possible after ablation, contact a clinician promptly because pregnancy can have serious complications. Seek urgent care for very heavy bleeding, fainting, severe weakness, or rapidly worsening symptoms.
