\chapter{Intra-articular Injection}

\section*{Overview}
An intra-articular injection places medicine into the space inside a joint. This chapter focuses on corticosteroid injections, which may reduce joint inflammation and pain for a limited time. Other substances, such as local anesthetic or hyaluronic acid, have different uses and evidence; the medicine and expected benefit should be discussed before the procedure. The injection does not repair damaged cartilage or cure arthritis.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item sterile syringe and joint needle
  \item ultrasound or fluoroscopy for selected joints
  \item skin antiseptic and dressing supplies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Joint injection; intra-articular (IA) injection; corticosteroid joint injection; steroid injection into a joint

\section*{Principle}
A corticosteroid reduces inflammation. When placed in a joint, it may lessen pain and improve function for some people, usually in the short term. Benefit varies by joint, condition, and person, and may be modest or absent. A local anesthetic may be used in some procedures and can provide temporary numbness.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
An injection may be offered for selected painful, inflamed joints, including some osteoarthritis flares or inflammatory arthritis. A clinician may also inject a joint after assessing a crystal-related flare, such as gout, once joint infection has been considered and appropriately excluded. For knee osteoarthritis, corticosteroid injection is an option for short-term symptom relief; it is considered alongside exercise, other nonsurgical care, and the person's preferences. It is not a substitute for treatment that controls systemic inflammatory arthritis.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
This is a treatment procedure. Fluid may be withdrawn from the joint (aspiration) before or instead of an injection to help diagnose the cause of swelling, including infection or crystals. A response to an injection does not confirm a diagnosis.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician reviews the reason for the injection, checks the joint and overlying skin, and explains expected benefits and risks. The skin is cleaned using sterile technique. The clinician places a needle into the joint using anatomical landmarks or imaging guidance when appropriate. Joint fluid may be removed for testing, especially when the cause of a new or unexplained swollen joint is uncertain. The prescribed medicine is then injected, and the needle is removed. The procedure may be done in an office or clinic; preparation and technique depend on the joint and medicine.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the clinician about fever or a recent infection, skin problems near the joint, diabetes, immune suppression, bleeding disorders, medicines that affect clotting, allergies, and prior joint injections or reactions. Do not stop a blood thinner unless the clinician who manages it tells you to; joint injections can often be performed without stopping antithrombotic medicines, but bleeding risk is assessed individually. Ask which medicine will be injected, what benefit is realistic, and when to contact the clinic. Fasting is usually not required for an injection done with local anesthesia alone, but follow the clinic's instructions.

\section*{Contraindications}
An injection should not be given through infected skin. Suspected infection inside the joint requires urgent assessment and usually joint-fluid testing; a corticosteroid should not be injected until infection has been excluded. A prosthetic joint needs specialist assessment; steroid injection into a prosthetic joint is generally avoided in routine practice.

\section*{Precautions}
Diabetes is relevant because corticosteroids can temporarily raise blood glucose. A serious allergy to the planned medicine or an ingredient may require another plan. Repeated injections should be considered individually: there is no evidence-based maximum number or interval that applies to every joint and condition. The clinician weighs prior benefit, alternatives, and possible harms; regular injections every few months are not automatically appropriate.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary soreness, bruising, bleeding, or a short-lived increase in pain can occur. Rare but serious complications include joint infection, significant bleeding, and an allergic reaction. Corticosteroid may temporarily raise blood glucose, particularly in people with diabetes, and can cause flushing or other temporary systemic effects. Skin thinning or a lighter skin color can occur near the injection site. Injury to a nearby tendon is uncommon and is a concern when medicine is placed into or around a tendon. Repeated corticosteroid injections may harm joint structures; a clinical trial found greater cartilage loss with triamcinolone given every three months for two years for knee osteoarthritis, without better pain relief than saline. This finding does not establish the risk from every injection schedule or joint, but supports avoiding automatic repeated injections.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the clinic's instructions. Mild soreness may occur for a day or two; use a wrapped cold pack if advised. Resume ordinary activity as symptoms allow, but avoid unusually strenuous activity for the rest of the day if the clinician recommends it. Strict bed rest or immobilization is not routinely needed. If you have diabetes, ask whether and how often to check blood glucose for several days after a corticosteroid injection, since levels may rise temporarily. Pain relief, if it occurs, may take a few days to begin.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The clinician and patient assess pain, movement, and daily function over the following days and weeks. Relief is often temporary, and some people do not improve. If the injection provides little benefit or symptoms return quickly, the clinician should reassess the diagnosis and discuss other treatments rather than simply repeating the injection. Continue exercise, physical therapy, or disease-modifying treatment when prescribed.

\section*{Expected Results}
Some people have less pain and improved function for a short period; others have little or no benefit. A corticosteroid injection does not restore cartilage, prevent arthritis progression, or replace long-term arthritis care.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow the care plan for the underlying joint condition. People with diabetes should follow the agreed glucose-monitoring plan. Before another injection, review the benefit and duration of relief from prior injections, possible risks, and other treatment options with the clinician.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the treating clinic urgently for rapidly worsening joint pain, increasing warmth or redness, marked swelling, fever, or feeling acutely unwell after the injection; these symptoms need prompt assessment for infection. Seek emergency care for trouble breathing, swelling of the face or throat, or collapse. Contact a clinician for persistent bleeding, new numbness or weakness, or blood glucose that is much higher than the person's agreed action threshold.
