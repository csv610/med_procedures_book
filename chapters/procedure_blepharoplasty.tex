\chapter{Blepharoplasty}

\section*{Overview}
Blepharoplasty, or eyelid surgery, removes or repositions skin, muscle, or fat around the eyelids. Upper-eyelid skin that hangs over the lashes can narrow the upper part of a person's vision. Lower-eyelid surgery often treats bulging fat or loose skin. Eyelid surgery does not correct every cause of a drooping eyelid; a low eyebrow or a weak eyelid-lifting muscle (ptosis) may need a different or additional procedure.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Eyelid surgery; upper-eyelid surgery; lower-eyelid surgery

\section*{Why It Is Done}
The procedure may be considered when excess upper-eyelid skin interferes with vision or when loose skin or bulging fat around the eyelids is a concern. Upper-eyelid surgery may be functional, cosmetic, or both. Lower-eyelid surgery is often performed to change the appearance of under-eye bags. The expected benefit depends on the cause of the concern and the person's eye and eyelid anatomy.

\section*{Preparation}
Before surgery, the clinician reviews eye health, vision, eyelid position and closure, tear production, dry-eye symptoms, medical conditions, and medicines. Tell the surgeon about glaucoma, thyroid eye disease, previous eye surgery, contact lens use, smoking, and any medicines or supplements that may affect bleeding. Do not stop prescribed medicines unless the clinician who manages them gives you a plan. Follow the team's eating and drinking instructions; fasting depends on whether you will have local anesthesia alone, sedation, or general anesthesia. Photographs or visual-field tests may be used to plan surgery or document a functional concern. Arrange a responsible adult to take you home if you have sedation or general anesthesia.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure may be done with local anesthesia, sometimes with sedation, or with general anesthesia. For upper-eyelid surgery, the incision is usually placed in the natural eyelid crease. The surgeon removes or repositions selected tissue and closes the incision. For lower-eyelid surgery, the incision may be just below the lashes or inside the lower lid; fat may be removed or repositioned, and the lid may be supported if needed. The specific approach depends on the anatomy and treatment goal. Many patients go home the same day.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
An active eye or eyelid infection or an uncontrolled health problem may mean surgery should be delayed. Dry eye, reduced ability to blink or close the eyes, glaucoma, thyroid eye disease, bleeding risk, and smoking need individualized assessment. These conditions do not automatically rule out surgery, but may affect the plan or the risk of complications. Discuss realistic goals and alternatives with an appropriately qualified eye or eyelid surgeon.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary bruising, swelling, numbness, irritation, watering, or dry-eye symptoms can occur. Brief blurred vision may result from ointment or a dry eye surface; persistent or worsening vision changes need prompt assessment. The clear tissue over the white of the eye can also swell (chemosis), especially after lower-eyelid surgery. Possible complications include infection, bleeding, scarring, uneven results, double vision, difficulty closing the eyes, or a change in eyelid position, such as the lower lid pulling down (retraction) or turning outward (ectropion). If an eye cannot close fully, its clear front surface (the cornea) can dry out or become injured. Rarely, bleeding behind the eye can threaten vision and needs emergency treatment. Sedation or general anesthesia, when used, has additional risks that depend on the person's health and the anesthetic. Some people need another procedure if the result is not as expected.

\section*{Recovery and Aftercare}
Follow the surgeon's instructions for cleaning the eyelids and using prescribed ointment or eye drops. A wrapped cold pack and keeping the head raised may help reduce swelling. Bruising and swelling often improve over the first couple of weeks, although healing varies and redness or scars may take longer to settle. Avoid strenuous activity, heavy lifting, bending over, swimming, rubbing the eyes, and wearing contact lenses until the surgeon says it is safe. Do not drive until vision is clear and any sedating medicine has worn off.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The surgeon checks healing and eyelid position at follow-up visits; stitches, if used, may be removed at a follow-up appointment. If surgery was intended to improve vision, the clinician may assess whether the visual-field problem improved. Cosmetic results are not guaranteed, and natural aging continues. The clinician will discuss any further treatment if needed.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek emergency care immediately for sudden vision loss or a major change in vision, severe eye pain, or rapidly increasing swelling, tightness, or bulging around the eye, especially if accompanied by vision changes. Contact the surgeon promptly for worsening pain, increasing redness, pus or other signs of infection, persistent bleeding, new double vision, or difficulty closing the eye.
