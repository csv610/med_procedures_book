\chapter{Colonoscopy}

\section*{Overview}
A colonoscopy uses a flexible camera to examine the lining of the rectum and colon. It can be used for colorectal cancer screening, to investigate symptoms, or to monitor a known condition. The clinician can take tissue samples, remove many polyps, and treat some sources of bleeding during the procedure. A normal result does not mean that every bowel disease has been ruled out.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item colonoscope, video processor, and monitor
  \item insufflation, irrigation, and suction equipment
  \item biopsy forceps, snare, or clips when needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Lower gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy; colonoscopic examination.

\section*{Principle}
A thin, flexible tube called a colonoscope is passed through the anus into the rectum and colon. The camera sends images to a screen. Gas, usually carbon dioxide or air, gently opens the bowel so the lining can be seen. Small instruments passed through the scope can take biopsies, remove polyps, or treat some bleeding.

\section*{History}
Fiber-optic colonoscopy and colonoscopic polyp removal were developed in the late 1960s and early 1970s. The technique made it possible to examine the colon directly and remove many precancerous polyps during the same procedure.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Colonoscopy may be offered to screen for colorectal cancer, evaluate blood in the stool, a persistent change in bowel habits, unexplained iron-deficiency anemia, or other symptoms, or to follow up on prior polyps, cancer, or inflammatory bowel disease. In the United States, the USPSTF recommends screening average-risk adults from ages 45 to 75 and selectively offering screening from ages 76 to 85 based on health, prior screening, and preferences. Colonoscopy is one screening option; methods and starting ages vary by country. People with symptoms, a strong family history, prior polyps, or certain medical conditions may need a different test or schedule. Other screening tests are available, and an abnormal stool-based screening test usually needs follow-up colonoscopy.

\section*{Role in Care}
Colonoscopy is a direct examination of the colon. It may be a screening test or a diagnostic procedure, depending on why it is ordered. It can also provide treatment, such as removing a polyp.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The team reviews the plan and places an IV if sedation or medicine is planned. Sedation is common but not required for everyone; pain relief and sedation options vary. The patient usually lies on their side while the clinician gently advances the scope through the colon, often to the cecum and sometimes into the end of the small intestine if needed. The clinician examines the lining as the scope is withdrawn and may take biopsies or remove polyps. The exam often takes 20 to 60 minutes, and may take longer if treatment is needed.

\section*{Preparation}
The bowel must be cleaned so the clinician can see the lining. Follow the endoscopy unit's exact instructions for diet, bowel-cleansing medicine, clear liquids, and when to stop drinking. Split-dose preparations, with part taken the evening before and the rest closer to the procedure, are recommended for most patients. For this schedule, the second portion is generally started 4 to 6 hours before the procedure and finished at least 2 hours before it. A same-day regimen is an acceptable alternative for an afternoon procedure, but is generally less effective for a morning procedure. Local instructions take priority because the regimen and fasting rules vary. The preparation can cause dehydration or changes in blood salts, especially in older adults and people with certain health conditions. The team can select a suitable regimen. If the preparation is not completed or the stool is not clear, the exam may need to be delayed or repeated.

Ask the care team how to manage prescription medicines, especially blood thinners, diabetes medicines such as insulin or SGLT2 inhibitors, iron, and medicines that slow stomach emptying, such as some GLP-1 medicines. SGLT2 inhibitors are usually stopped 3 to 4 days before elective endoscopy because fasting and bowel preparation can trigger ketoacidosis; confirm the timing with the endoscopy team and the clinician who prescribes the medicine. Instructions for GLP-1 medicines depend on symptoms and the sedation plan. Fasting and the bowel preparation can affect medicine safety, so do not stop or change prescribed medicines without instructions. Tell the team about kidney or heart disease, constipation, allergies, sleep apnea, pregnancy, and prior problems with bowel preparation or sedation. Arrange an escort and follow restrictions on driving or important decisions if sedatives are used.

\section*{Contraindications}
The team may postpone the procedure or choose another test when appropriate.

\section*{Precautions}
A suspected bowel perforation or severe acute colitis may make colonoscopy unsafe or require a modified approach. The clinician weighs the need for the examination against risks from the person's current illness and health conditions. Recent heart or lung problems, bleeding risk, medicines, pregnancy, and difficulty tolerating the bowel preparation may require extra planning.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary bloating, gas, cramping, or nausea from the preparation is common. Less common complications include bleeding, especially after a biopsy or polyp removal; a tear in the bowel wall; infection; or a reaction to sedation. Serious complications are uncommon, but risk varies with age, health, the reason for the test, and whether treatment is performed. A small amount of blood can occur after a biopsy or polyp removal; heavy bleeding is not expected.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After the procedure, the care team monitors the patient until they are ready to leave and explains initial findings. Passing gas and mild bloating or cramping can occur and usually improve. If sedation was used, follow the facility's instructions about driving, alcohol, machinery, work, and having an adult accompany you; restrictions commonly last until the next day. Resume food, fluids, and medicines as directed. Biopsy or polyp results may take several days or longer.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The clinician may discuss what was seen before discharge. Any biopsy or removed polyp is sent to a laboratory, and the final plan may depend on those results. The next screening or surveillance interval depends on the quality and completeness of the exam, bowel preparation, findings, personal history, and family history. For example, a high-quality normal screening colonoscopy in an average-risk adult is commonly followed by screening in 10 years in the United States; intervals after polyp removal vary widely. Do not assume that every polyp requires a 3-to-5-year repeat examination.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Get urgent medical advice for severe or worsening abdominal pain, a hard or swollen abdomen, fever or chills, repeated vomiting, faintness, or more than a small amount of rectal bleeding—especially if bleeding continues or includes clots. Call emergency services for heavy bleeding, severe weakness, fainting, chest pain, or trouble breathing. Follow the endoscopy unit's contact instructions, since bleeding after a polyp is removed can begin up to 2 weeks later.
