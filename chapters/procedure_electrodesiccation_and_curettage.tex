\chapter{Electrodesiccation and Curettage}

\section*{Overview}
Electrodesiccation and curettage (ED\&C) is a minor skin procedure used to treat some carefully selected skin cancers and other lesions. The clinician scrapes the lesion with a small instrument called a curette, then uses an electrical current to heat and destroy tissue at the treatment site and control bleeding. The wound is usually left open to heal. ED\&C does not provide the same microscopic check of tumor margins as excision or Mohs surgery, so it is used only when the lesion's risk and location make this approach appropriate.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item dermatologic curette
  \item electrosurgical generator and electrode
  \item local-anesthetic and wound-dressing supplies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Curettage and electrodesiccation; curettage and cautery; ED\&C; C\&E; scrape and cauterize.

\section*{Principle}
The curette scrapes away tissue that has a softer texture than nearby skin. The clinician then uses electrosurgery to dry and destroy remaining tissue at the base and to control bleeding. The scrape-and-treat cycle may be repeated. Unlike excision or Mohs surgery, ED\&C does not produce an intact specimen with edges that can be examined to confirm clear margins.

\section*{History}
Curettage and electrosurgery have been used in dermatology for many decades. ED\&C remains an option for selected low-risk skin cancers when the treating clinician has confirmed the diagnosis and assessed the tumor's risk.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
ED\&C is commonly used for certain small, low-risk basal cell carcinomas on suitable parts of the body, such as the trunk or arms and legs. It may be used for selected superficial or low-risk squamous cell cancers, including some squamous cell carcinomas in situ, when the tumor and location are appropriate. It may also be used for some noncancerous growths or precancerous lesions. It is generally not chosen for tumors that are large, deep, aggressive, recurrent, poorly defined, or in a high-risk location.

\section*{Role in Care}
ED\&C is a treatment, not a diagnostic biopsy. A suspicious lesion is usually assessed and biopsied before treatment so its type and risk can be considered. Scraped tissue may sometimes be sent for testing, but this does not provide a reliable microscopic assessment of the treatment margins.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician cleans the skin and injects local anesthetic. After checking that the area is numb, they use a curette to scrape the lesion and an electrosurgical instrument to treat the base and stop bleeding. They may repeat these steps. The wound is usually not stitched closed; it is covered with a dressing and heals gradually from the base and edges. The appointment is typically done in an outpatient clinic.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the dermatologist about medicines, bleeding problems, allergies to local anesthetics, and implanted electronic devices such as a pacemaker or defibrillator. Do not stop blood thinners or other prescribed medicines unless the prescribing clinician advises you to. Let the clinician know about prior treatment of the lesion, immune suppression, and any concerns about scarring or wound healing. Follow any instructions provided for the treatment area.

\section*{Contraindications}
ED\&C is not suitable for every skin cancer. It is generally avoided when a tumor's size, depth, cell type, location, or previous recurrence raises the risk that cancer may remain or return. Implanted cardiac devices require planning, but do not automatically rule out electrosurgery.

\section*{Precautions}
It is less suitable on areas with dense hair follicles or where a scar could affect function or appearance, such as parts of the face. People with a weakened immune system or recurrent tumors may need a treatment that checks margins, such as excision or Mohs surgery.

\section*{Risks}
The treatment site can bleed, become infected, or heal with a round, flat, depressed, or lighter-colored scar. Temporary tenderness is common. The treated lesion can recur, and because margins are not checked microscopically, recurrence may require another biopsy or a different treatment. The likelihood depends on tumor features, location, and patient factors.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the clinician's instructions for cleaning and dressing the wound. Many wounds heal best when gently cleaned and kept moist under an ointment and dressing, but use the specific plan provided for your site. Healing can take several weeks, depending on wound size and location. Protect the healed area from the sun and attend recommended skin checks. Do not pick at a scab or use unapproved products on the wound.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
The wound's healing does not by itself prove that a skin cancer is fully cured. The dermatologist explains the expected cure and recurrence risk for the specific tumor. If a lesion returns or the wound does not heal as expected, it may need reassessment and another treatment.

\section*{Expected Results}
The lesion is scraped and treated during the visit, leaving a wound that heals over time and usually leaves a scar. The appearance and chance of recurrence vary with the lesion, treatment site, healing, and the clinician's patient selection.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend the skin examination schedule recommended by the dermatologist. People who have had a skin cancer have an increased risk of developing another and may need regular full-skin checks and sun protection. Tell the dermatologist if a bump, scale, sore, or bleeding spot returns at the treated site.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the treating clinician for bleeding that does not stop with the advised pressure, worsening pain, spreading redness, warmth, swelling, pus, fever, or a wound that is not healing. Seek urgent care for severe bleeding or rapidly spreading infection.
