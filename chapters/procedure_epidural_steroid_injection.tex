\chapter{Epidural Steroid Injection}

\section*{Overview}
An epidural steroid injection (ESI) places a corticosteroid medicine into the epidural space around the spinal nerves. It may reduce inflammation and temporarily ease pain that travels from the neck or back into an arm or leg (radicular pain). Relief varies, may be limited to the short term, and is not guaranteed. In the United States, steroid medicines are not approved by the FDA for injection into the epidural space; this use is off-label.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Epidural injection; epidural steroid injection; transforaminal, interlaminar, or caudal epidural injection; ESI.

\section*{Principle}
The clinician places corticosteroid near irritated spinal nerve roots to reduce inflammation. The medicine may be combined with a local anesthetic, which can cause short-term numbness or pain relief. The injection does not remove a disc herniation or reverse narrowing of the spinal canal, and it does not reliably change the long-term course of these conditions.

\section*{History}
Corticosteroid injections near spinal nerves have been used for decades to treat selected forms of radiating pain. Imaging guidance and safety recommendations have developed to improve needle placement and reduce the risk of accidental injection into a blood vessel or other sensitive structure.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
An ESI may be considered for arm or leg pain caused by irritation of a spinal nerve, such as from a herniated disc or spinal stenosis, when other treatments have not provided enough relief. It is not generally a treatment for nonspecific back or neck pain alone. The goal is usually to reduce pain enough to support activity and rehabilitation while symptoms improve or other care is planned.

\section*{Role in Care}
ESI is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. It is one option after assessment of symptoms, examination, and relevant imaging. A 2025 American Academy of Neurology review found that injections probably provide some short-term improvement in pain and disability for radiculopathy, while evidence for longer-term benefit is limited. Response to an injection does not prove the cause of pain.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is usually performed by a trained clinician in an outpatient setting. The person is positioned so the clinician can reach the planned area. Using X-ray guidance (fluoroscopy) and usually contrast dye to check needle location, the clinician directs a needle toward the epidural space. The route may be interlaminar, transforaminal, or caudal, depending on the target and individual anatomy. Steroid, sometimes with local anesthetic, is then injected. For a cervical transforaminal injection, safety guidance recommends a non-particulate steroid to reduce the risk of blocking an artery. Local anesthetic may be used to numb the skin; sedation is not always needed.

\section*{Preparation}
The team reviews symptoms, imaging, allergies, medicines, and medical conditions and explains the benefits, risks, and alternatives. Tell the clinician if you take blood thinners or antiplatelet medicines, have diabetes, an infection, a bleeding problem, an allergy to contrast or medicines, or could be pregnant. Blood-thinning medicines may need an individualized plan based on the drug and procedure; do not stop them unless the prescribing and procedure teams give you instructions. Fasting and transportation instructions depend on whether sedation is planned and local practice.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
An active infection, significant uncorrected bleeding risk, or allergy to a planned medicine or contrast may prevent or delay the injection or require another plan. Diabetes matters because steroids can temporarily raise blood sugar. Pregnancy or possible pregnancy should be discussed because X-ray guidance may be used. Neck and upper-spine injections require particular care because of nearby blood vessels and the spinal cord; the approach and medicine are selected to reduce these risks.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary soreness or a brief increase in pain can occur. Other possible effects include headache from an unintended dural puncture, bleeding, infection, nerve irritation, and a temporary rise in blood sugar, blood pressure, or other steroid effects. Rarely, serious neurologic injury can occur, including spinal cord or brain infarction, paralysis, vision loss, or stroke. Such events have been reported after epidural steroid injections, especially with inadvertent entry into an artery. Imaging, contrast, careful technique, and choice of steroid reduce some risks but cannot make the risk zero.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The care team may observe you briefly and check movement or sensation before discharge. If local anesthetic was used, temporary numbness or weakness may occur and should resolve as it wears off. Some people notice pain relief within hours from the anesthetic; steroid benefit, if it occurs, may take several days. Follow instructions about activity, driving, and when to resume medicines. People with diabetes may need to monitor blood sugar more closely for a short time.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The purpose is to reduce radiating pain and improve function for a limited period so a person can participate in daily activities or rehabilitation. The amount and duration of relief vary. No improvement does not rule out nerve irritation, and improvement does not confirm a diagnosis. The clinician reviews the response and considers other treatment options based on function, symptoms, and imaging.

\section*{Expected Results}
Some people have meaningful short-term pain relief; others have little or none. A lasting cure should not be expected, and the injection does not correct the structural cause. Benefit, if any, should be weighed against the risks and the person's goals.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow up with the treating clinician to review symptom and functional changes. A repeat injection is considered only after assessing the response, risks, and alternatives; a fixed series is not appropriate for everyone. If symptoms persist or worsen, the clinician may recommend rehabilitation, medication changes, further evaluation, or a surgical consultation depending on the cause.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek emergency care for new or worsening weakness, loss of coordination, numbness in the groin or saddle area, loss of bladder or bowel control, severe sudden headache, vision loss or change, facial droop, difficulty speaking, seizure, or trouble breathing. Contact the treating team promptly for fever, increasing redness or drainage at the injection site, or severe or worsening pain.
