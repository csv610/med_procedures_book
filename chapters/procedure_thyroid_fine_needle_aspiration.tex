\chapter{Thyroid Fine-Needle Aspiration Biopsy}

\section*{Overview}
Thyroid fine-needle aspiration (FNA) biopsy uses a thin needle to collect cells, and sometimes fluid, from a thyroid nodule. A pathologist examines the sample. The result is considered together with the nodule's ultrasound appearance, medical history, and other test results to plan follow-up or treatment. Many nodules do not need biopsy; clinicians use their size and ultrasound features to decide whether sampling is appropriate.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item fine-gauge needle and syringe or aspiration device
  \item ultrasound machine when image guidance is used
  \item slides or specimen containers and labeling supplies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Thyroid FNA, thyroid fine-needle aspiration biopsy, thyroid needle biopsy

\section*{Why It Is Done}
FNA is used to investigate selected thyroid nodules that meet criteria based on their ultrasound features, size, and clinical context. It can help identify benign changes, thyroid inflammation, or cancer. It may also be used to sample a suspicious lymph node in the neck. A thyroid nodule's appearance on ultrasound alone cannot always determine whether it is cancerous.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
FNA is a diagnostic sampling test, usually performed after a nodule has been found and assessed by ultrasound. It is not a treatment for most nodules. It may be done with ultrasound guidance so the clinician can see the needle enter the intended area.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is usually done in an outpatient clinic. You lie on your back with your neck gently extended. The skin is cleaned, and the clinician uses ultrasound to locate the nodule. A numbing medicine may be applied or injected. The clinician passes a thin needle through the skin into different parts of the nodule, often taking several samples. You may be asked to keep still and avoid swallowing, coughing, or talking while the needle is in place. Pressure is applied afterward and a small dressing may be used. The sampling itself takes seconds; the appointment often takes up to about 30 minutes.

\section*{Preparation}
Usually, no fasting or special preparation is needed. Tell the team about allergies, bleeding problems, possible pregnancy, and all medicines and supplements you take. Blood-thinning medicines may need an individualized plan. Do not stop aspirin, anticoagulants, or other prescribed medicines unless the procedural and prescribing clinicians tell you how to do so. Wear clothing that leaves the front of your neck accessible and remove necklaces. If sedation is planned, follow the clinic's fasting and transportation instructions.

\section*{Contraindications}
No specific absolute contraindication is identified here; the treating team assesses suitability for the individual.

\section*{Precautions}
Most adults have the procedure without sedation. The team reviews the nodule's location, any skin infection at the planned needle site, bleeding risks, medicines, and whether you can hold still and follow instructions. These factors may change how or when the biopsy is done. A blood-thinning medicine is not an automatic reason to cancel; the team balances bleeding risk against the risk of changing the medicine. Children or people who cannot remain still may need additional planning.

\section*{Risks}
Most people have only brief discomfort. Possible risks include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tenderness, a small bruise, or minor bleeding at the puncture site.
    \item Bleeding into or around the thyroid. A large collection of blood is rare but can press on the windpipe and affect breathing.
    \item Infection or injury to nearby structures, which are uncommon.
    \item A sample that does not contain enough cells for a clear result, requiring repeat FNA or another evaluation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
After the biopsy, the team presses on the site and may ask you to sit up slowly. Mild neck soreness or bruising can occur for a short time. Follow the clinic's instructions for wound care and pain relief. Most people return to usual activities soon, unless they received sedation or were given other restrictions.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Results commonly take several days and may take up to two weeks, depending on the laboratory and whether additional tests are needed. Many laboratories use the Bethesda System, which places thyroid samples into categories such as benign, malignant, atypical or indeterminate, and nondiagnostic (not enough useful cells). The exact wording and next steps depend on the category and the patient's findings.

A benign result is reassuring, but follow-up ultrasound may still be recommended based on the nodule's appearance and clinical context. A nondiagnostic result may lead to repeat ultrasound-guided FNA. An indeterminate result may prompt repeat sampling, molecular testing, monitoring, or surgery. Some thyroid growths cannot be reliably classified as benign or cancerous from cells alone because the distinction depends on seeing how the tissue grows; surgery may be needed to make that distinction. A result that does not fit the ultrasound or examination may also need further evaluation.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no general normal range for thyroid FNA. The pathology report describes the cells sampled from the nodule. A benign result means no cancer was identified in that sample; it does not guarantee that every part of the nodule was sampled or remove the need for follow-up when recommended. The clinician should explain how the result fits the ultrasound findings and what follow-up is appropriate.

\section*{Follow-up}
Depending on the result, follow-up may include periodic ultrasound, repeat FNA, molecular testing, consultation with an endocrinologist or surgeon, or an operation. A malignant result does not by itself determine the exact treatment; the type and extent of treatment depend on the specific diagnosis and the person's circumstances.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Follow the clinic's instructions. Contact the care team for worsening pain, increasing swelling, redness, drainage, or fever. Seek emergency care for rapidly increasing neck swelling, difficulty breathing or swallowing, or bleeding that does not stop with the recommended pressure.
