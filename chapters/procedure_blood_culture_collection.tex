\chapter{Blood Culture Collection}

\section*{Overview}
A blood culture is a laboratory test that checks a blood sample for bacteria and certain fungi that can cause infection. Bacteria in the blood are called bacteremia; fungi in the blood are called fungemia. A clinician collects the sample using sterile technique, usually from a vein in the arm. The laboratory grows and identifies any organisms and may test which medicines are likely to work.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Blood cultures; blood culture test

\section*{Principle}
Blood is placed in bottles containing nutrients that help certain bacteria and fungi grow. Growth can indicate infection, but germs from the skin or collection equipment can contaminate a sample. Clinicians interpret results using the organism, the number and source of positive samples, the patient's condition, and other test results.

\section*{History}
Blood cultures have been used since the late 19th century. Modern automated systems monitor culture bottles for signs of microbial growth.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
The test is used when a bloodstream infection is suspected, including in some patients with sepsis, endocarditis (infection of the heart lining or valves), or a possible infection related to a central venous catheter.

\section*{Role in Diagnosis}
Blood culture is an important test for detecting bloodstream infection. Results help clinicians identify the organism and choose treatment, but must be interpreted in the patient's clinical context.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician confirms the patient's identity using at least two identifiers, cleans the skin with an appropriate antiseptic, and lets it dry fully. The antiseptic is chosen according to the patient's age, allergies, and local protocol. The cleaned site should not be touched again unless the collector is wearing sterile gloves, and the bottle tops are disinfected before blood is added. Blood is drawn into culture bottles, with the amount guided by the laboratory and bottle instructions. Adequate blood volume is important: collecting too little can lower the chance of detecting an infection. In adults, a set commonly contains about 20--30 mL total, and at least two sets are often collected by separate venipunctures. CDC guidance describes collecting two to four sets during the evaluation of a suspected bloodstream infection; the exact number and volume depend on the clinical situation and local protocol. For most suspected bloodstream infections, separate sets can be collected one after another without waiting between them. An adult set usually includes aerobic and anaerobic bottles, which support the growth of organisms that need oxygen and those that grow without it. Children need smaller, weight-appropriate volumes, and the bottle configuration may differ. Each set is labeled with the collection time, source site, and collection method, and the volume in each bottle is recorded according to laboratory policy.

Some conditions, such as suspected infective endocarditis, may require a different number of sets or a specific collection schedule, so the relevant clinical and laboratory protocol is followed. The 2023 European Society of Cardiology guideline recommends at least three sets collected about 30 minutes apart before antibiotics; in an unstable patient, cultures should be collected promptly and must not delay urgent treatment.

If a catheter-related infection is suspected, clinicians may collect blood at the same time from a peripheral vein and from the catheter, following the laboratory's protocol. When the lab will compare how quickly each culture becomes positive, the samples should contain equal blood volumes. Each source must be labeled clearly so the results can be compared. If the same organism grows in both samples and the catheter sample becomes positive at least two hours earlier, this supports a catheter-related infection; the care team interprets this with the patient's condition and other findings. Catheter samples are not a routine substitute for peripheral samples.

\section*{Preparation}
No special patient preparation is usually needed. When feasible, samples are collected before antibiotics because antibiotics can reduce the chance of detecting an organism. In a medical emergency, collecting cultures must not delay urgent treatment.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
There is no absolute contraindication to taking a blood culture. The collector uses aseptic technique, selects an appropriate site, and follows local guidance for antiseptic choice and blood volume. When catheter infection is suspected, catheter and peripheral samples may both be needed.

\section*{Risks}
Risks are usually minor and include brief pain, bruising, bleeding, faintness, or a small risk of infection at the puncture site. Skin contamination can cause a misleading positive result and lead to unnecessary follow-up or treatment.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The puncture site is covered and pressure is applied until bleeding stops. The bottles are sent promptly to the laboratory and monitored according to its protocol. Many routine automated cultures are monitored for about five days, but timing can vary with the suspected organism and the laboratory method. If growth is detected, the laboratory may perform a Gram stain, identify the organism, and test its antimicrobial susceptibility (which medicines may work).

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
A positive result does not always mean a true bloodstream infection. Some organisms are common skin contaminants, but they can still cause infection in certain patients. Clinicians consider the organism, how many separate sets are positive, where samples were collected, and the patient's condition. A negative result means no organism grew under the conditions used; it does not always rule out infection, especially after antibiotics or when organisms are difficult to grow.

Repeat cultures or additional tests, such as heart imaging when endocarditis is suspected, are ordered when the suspected infection and the patient's response make them useful. Follow-up and treatment are determined by the clinical team.

\section*{Expected Results}
No organism targeted by the test grows during the laboratory's monitoring period. The result should be interpreted alongside symptoms, examination findings, and other tests.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
After the blood draw, contact a healthcare professional for bleeding that does not stop with firm pressure, increasing pain, redness, warmth, or swelling at the puncture site, or fainting that does not quickly improve. If the culture was ordered because of a possible serious infection, seek urgent care for severe or rapidly worsening symptoms such as trouble breathing, confusion, or fainting.
