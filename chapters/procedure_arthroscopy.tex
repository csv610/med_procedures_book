\chapter{Arthroscopy}

\section*{Overview}
Arthroscopy is surgery in which a clinician uses a small camera, called an arthroscope, to look inside a joint. Through small cuts, the surgeon may also treat selected problems. It is most often done on the knee or shoulder, but it can be used on other joints. The reason for surgery, expected benefit, and recovery depend on the specific joint and condition.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Arthroscopic surgery; joint scope; keyhole joint surgery

\section*{Principle}
A camera and light are passed into the joint through a small incision, and the image appears on a monitor. Sterile fluid is often used to improve the view. The surgeon may insert small instruments through other incisions to examine or treat selected problems. Small incisions do not mean that every arthroscopic operation is minor; the repair performed inside the joint affects the risks and recovery.
\section*{History}
Surgeons in several countries explored ways to view the inside of joints in the early 1900s. Kenji Takagi examined a cadaver knee with a cystoscope in 1918. Masaki Watanabe later developed improved instruments and techniques that helped establish modern arthroscopy.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Arthroscopy may be used to treat selected problems such as some traumatic meniscus tears, loose pieces of bone or cartilage, or inflamed joint tissue. It can also be part of reconstructive surgery for certain ligament injuries. Some acute meniscus tears, especially displaced tears that block knee movement or tears that may be repairable, may need earlier surgical assessment. Rarely, arthroscopy may help investigate a joint problem when examination and imaging have not answered an important question and the result could change treatment. It is used in the knee, shoulder, hip, ankle, elbow, and wrist.

Arthroscopy is not a routine treatment for joint pain alone. Guidelines agree that washing out or trimming a knee affected by osteoarthritis (wear-related joint damage), without a specific treatable problem, is generally not helpful. They differ on trimming a confirmed meniscus tear when mild-to-moderate osteoarthritis is also present and nonsurgical treatment has not helped: AAOS says it may be considered, while BMJ guidance recommends against arthroscopy for nearly all degenerative knee disease. These recommendations address different groups: the BMJ guidance does not cover a recent major knee injury or a persistent, objectively locked knee.

A knee that is physically stuck and cannot fully straighten (a true locked knee, not just stiffness or pain-limited movement) may need prompt surgical assessment, for example if a displaced tear or loose fragment is blocking movement. Clicking or catching alone is not the same as a true locked knee. Ask whether nonsurgical options, such as exercise or physical therapy and medicines, are appropriate. Expected benefits and alternatives depend on the diagnosis.

\section*{Role in Care}
Arthroscopy is an operation, not a screening test. It is usually planned after symptoms, examination, and imaging have been considered. It can be used to make a diagnosis and, when appropriate and agreed in advance, treat a problem during the same operation. Some ligament reconstructions use an arthroscope to guide part of the surgery.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is usually done in an operating room with general anesthesia or regional anesthesia, which numbs a larger area; the choice depends on the joint, planned operation, and the patient's health. The surgeon makes small cuts near the joint, inserts the camera, and may use additional instruments to perform the planned treatment. The cuts are closed with stitches, strips, or other dressings. Many patients go home the same day, though some need to stay longer.

\section*{Preparation}
Follow the surgical team's instructions about eating and drinking, medicines, bathing, and arrival time. Tell the team about allergies, medical conditions, possible pregnancy, and all medicines, including blood thinners and supplements. Do not stop or change a medicine unless the team gives you a plan. Arrange a ride home after anesthesia and help at home if you may have limited movement.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
The surgeon reviews whether the planned procedure is appropriate and whether other health problems raise the risk of anesthesia or surgery. Infection at a planned incision site may require a different plan. A joint replacement, severe stiffness, or altered anatomy may change how or where arthroscopy can be performed. Arthroscopy may itself be used to wash out an infected joint, so suspected infection is not a blanket reason to avoid the procedure.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary pain, swelling, bruising, or stiffness is common. Less common but important risks include infection, bleeding into the joint, injury to a nearby nerve, blood vessel, tendon, or cartilage, and a blood clot in a leg. Anesthesia also has risks, which vary with the person's health and the type of anesthesia. The risks depend on the joint and the operation performed.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the written instructions for wound care, pain relief, ice or elevation, and any brace or crutches. Keep the incisions clean and follow directions about showering and dressing changes. The surgeon will explain how much weight or movement is allowed; restrictions differ greatly between a simple procedure and a repair. Do not drive while affected by anesthesia or medicines that cause drowsiness, and resume driving only when the surgical team says it is safe and you can control the vehicle in an emergency.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The surgeon records what was seen and what treatment, if any, was performed. Arthroscopy may confirm or treat a problem, but it does not guarantee that pain or function will improve. Recovery and benefit depend on the underlying condition, the repair, and rehabilitation.

\section*{Expected Results}
There is no single expected finding for every arthroscopy. Findings may be normal or may show an injury or disease, and the result depends on why surgery was done. The surgeon explains the findings and how they affect the treatment and recovery plan.

\section*{Follow-up}
Follow-up timing and physical therapy depend on the procedure. The care team checks healing, reviews the findings, and adjusts activity or rehabilitation instructions. Return to work, sports, and other activities may take days to months; follow the surgeon's plan rather than a fixed timeline.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the surgical team promptly for fever or chills, worsening pain or swelling, increasing redness or warmth, drainage from an incision, persistent bleeding, or new numbness or tingling. Seek urgent same-day medical assessment for a painful, swollen calf, which may be a blood clot. Call emergency services for sudden shortness of breath or chest pain, which may signal a clot that has reached the lungs.
