\chapter{Intravenous Cannulation}

\section*{Overview}
Intravenous (IV) cannulation places a short, flexible plastic catheter into a peripheral vein, usually in the hand or arm. It provides temporary access to the bloodstream for fluids and suitable medicines. A peripheral IV is not appropriate for every infusion; treatment type and duration may require a different device, such as a midline or central venous catheter.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Peripheral IV insertion; peripheral intravenous catheter (PIVC); peripheral IV access; IV line; IV start

\section*{Principle}
A needle guides the plastic catheter into a vein. When blood return shows that the needle has entered the vein, the catheter is advanced into the vessel and the needle is removed. The catheter connects to an injection cap or infusion tubing. It must be secured and checked because it can move, become blocked, or allow fluid or medicine to leak into surrounding tissue.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Peripheral IV access is used to give fluids, medicines, blood products, or imaging contrast when these can safely be delivered through a peripheral vein. It may also provide short-term access during urgent care. A clinician chooses the device based on the medicine, concentration, expected duration, vein condition, and the person's treatment plan. Some irritant or tissue-damaging medicines and longer courses of therapy may require another type of vascular access.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
This is an access procedure used to provide treatment. It is not a diagnostic test, although blood may sometimes be collected through an IV catheter when clinically appropriate and permitted by local policy.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The clinician confirms the need for IV access, chooses an appropriate catheter and site, and explains the procedure. Hand hygiene and aseptic technique are used. A tourniquet may help make a vein easier to see or feel. After skin antiseptic is applied and allowed to dry, a needle-and-catheter assembly is inserted into the vein. Once blood return indicates entry, the flexible catheter is advanced and the needle is safely discarded. The tourniquet is released, the catheter is connected, secured with a sterile dressing, and flushed or checked according to protocol. The site and infusion are assessed for pain, swelling, leakage, or other problems.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the clinician about allergies to skin antiseptics, adhesive, or latex; prior difficult IV placement; fragile or painful veins; lymphedema; dialysis access; and any planned surgery or treatment that may need to preserve arm veins. The clinician selects the smallest catheter that can safely deliver the planned therapy and the site that best supports treatment and comfort. Warming the limb, positioning it comfortably, or using ultrasound may help when veins are difficult to access. Repeated attempts should be limited according to clinical policy, with escalation to an experienced clinician or another access method when needed.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
Do not insert through infected, burned, or injured skin, or into a limb with an arteriovenous fistula or graft. Avoid a vein with phlebitis or a limb with significant swelling or impaired circulation. In adults, an upper-extremity site is generally preferred; a lower-extremity catheter should be moved to an arm site as soon as practical. Sites over joints may be less comfortable and harder to keep secure. A prior mastectomy or lymph-node procedure alone is not always an absolute reason to avoid that arm; consider existing lymphedema, available alternatives, patient preference, and local guidance. In people with advanced kidney disease, discuss vein preservation with the treating team. Use a suitable catheter for the planned infusion; highly irritating or tissue-damaging medicines may need central access or another approved route.

\section*{Risks}
Insertion can cause brief pain, bleeding, or bruising. Complications include infiltration (non-damaging fluid leaking into tissue), extravasation (a tissue-damaging medicine leaking into tissue), phlebitis, blockage, dislodgement, infection, and rarely nerve or arterial injury. Some medicines can cause serious tissue damage if they leak. Repeated or prolonged catheter use can injure veins. The risk depends on the catheter, medicine, site, and how the line is maintained.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Keep the dressing clean, dry, and intact, and avoid pulling or bending the catheter. Tell staff promptly about pain, burning, swelling, wetness, redness, leakage, or a change in how the infusion feels. Clinicians inspect the site regularly and remove the catheter as soon as it is no longer needed or sooner if it is painful, inflamed, infected, blocked, displaced, or leaking. Routine replacement timing varies by current guidance and local policy; a catheter does not automatically need replacement solely because it has been in place for 72–96 hours in every setting.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The catheter should remain secure and allow the prescribed therapy to flow without pain, swelling, or leakage. Blood return can support placement but may not be present throughout the catheter's use; clinical assessment and infusion-specific checks are important. If access fails or therapy cannot be safely delivered through a short peripheral catheter, the team may use ultrasound guidance, a longer peripheral catheter, a midline, or central access, depending on the treatment.

\section*{Expected Results}
A working peripheral IV provides the planned short-term access with minimal discomfort and no signs of infiltration, extravasation, phlebitis, or infection. A catheter may still stop working or need removal, even when it was initially placed correctly.

\section*{Follow-up}
The care team checks whether IV access is still needed and removes it promptly when it is not. If IV treatment will continue, the team reassesses the site and whether the type of catheter remains appropriate. After removal, gentle pressure is applied until bleeding stops, and the site is observed for persistent pain, redness, swelling, or drainage.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
While in a healthcare facility, tell a nurse or clinician immediately if the IV site becomes painful, swollen, cool, red, wet, or begins to leak, or if fluid stops flowing or the dressing becomes loose. After removal, contact a healthcare professional for increasing redness, warmth, swelling, pus, fever, persistent numbness or tingling, or bleeding that does not stop with gentle pressure. Severe pain, rapidly increasing swelling, blistering, or skin discoloration during an infusion needs urgent assessment, especially if a tissue-damaging medicine may have leaked.
