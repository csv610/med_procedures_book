\chapter{Lymph Node Biopsy}

\section*{Overview}
A lymph node biopsy takes cells or tissue from a lymph node so a laboratory can look for infection, inflammation, or cancer. The sample may be collected with a thin needle, a larger core needle, or surgery to remove part or all of a node. The best method depends on what condition is being considered, the node's location, and the amount and type of tissue needed.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item ultrasound guidance and sterile probe cover
  \item core-biopsy needle or fine needle
  \item specimen containers and labeling supplies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Lymph node sampling, fine-needle aspiration, core needle biopsy, excisional lymph node biopsy

\section*{Why It Is Done}
Biopsy may be recommended when a lymph node remains enlarged or has an unusual appearance on imaging and the result could change care. It can help evaluate suspected lymphoma, cancer that may have spread to a node, or selected infections and inflammatory conditions. Some enlarged nodes are reactive to infection and do not need biopsy; the decision depends on the history, examination, imaging, and other test results.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
Biopsy is one part of an evaluation and is not needed for every enlarged lymph node. Less invasive tests or observation may be appropriate in some cases. The clinician should explain what question the sample will answer and whether a needle sample or surgical biopsy is most likely to provide enough information.

\section*{Types of Biopsy}
In fine-needle aspiration, a thin needle removes cells or fluid. In a core needle biopsy, a wider needle removes small cylinders of tissue, often with ultrasound or CT guidance. In an excisional biopsy, a surgeon removes an entire node; an incisional biopsy removes part of one. When lymphoma is suspected, an excisional biopsy is often preferred because the node's structure helps classify the disease. A core biopsy may be chosen when surgery carries more risk or the node is difficult to reach. If a core sample cannot provide a diagnosis, an excisional biopsy may still be needed. Sentinel lymph node biopsy is a separate cancer-staging procedure that finds and samples the first node likely to receive drainage from a known tumor; it is not another name for a diagnostic biopsy of an enlarged node.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
Needle biopsy is commonly done with local anesthetic. The clinician cleans the skin and guides a needle into the node, sometimes using imaging. Several samples may be taken. Surgical biopsy may use local anesthesia with sedation or general anesthesia, depending on the node and procedure. The surgeon makes a small cut, removes part or all of the node, controls bleeding, and closes the wound. The sample may need special handling so the laboratory can perform the tests required for the suspected condition.

\section*{Preparation}
Instructions depend on the biopsy method and anesthesia. Tell the care team about allergies, pregnancy or possible pregnancy, medical conditions, bleeding problems, and all medicines and supplements, especially anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs. Do not stop prescribed medicines unless the treating team provides a specific plan. Follow fasting instructions if sedation or general anesthesia is planned. Ask whether you need someone to accompany you home.

\section*{Contraindications}
No specific absolute contraindication is identified here; the treating team assesses suitability for the individual.

\section*{Precautions}
The team considers the node's location, nearby nerves and blood vessels, bleeding risk, skin infection, and whether a safer site or biopsy method is available. A neck, chest, abdominal, or deep node may require imaging guidance or a different approach from a node close to the skin. The clinical team and pathology laboratory may coordinate the sample plan in advance, particularly when lymphoma or infection is suspected.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
Temporary soreness, bruising, or a small amount of bleeding can occur. Other risks include infection, a collection of blood or lymph fluid, and injury to a nearby nerve or blood vessel. Removal of several nodes, especially in the armpit or groin, can increase the risk of lymphedema (swelling from impaired lymph drainage); this is less likely after sampling only a sentinel node. The risks also depend on the node's location and whether sedation or general anesthesia is used.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Many needle biopsies and uncomplicated surgical biopsies are outpatient procedures. Follow the instructions for keeping the site clean, changing the dressing, pain relief, and activity. Mild pain or bruising may last for a few days. Avoid strenuous activity or heavy lifting for the period advised, especially if it causes pain or pulls on the wound. Recovery is longer after deeper or more extensive surgery.

\section*{Outcome and Results}
A pathologist examines the sample. Testing may include cell appearance, tissue architecture, immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, molecular tests, or cultures, depending on the question. Results can take several days or longer. A sample that is too small or does not contain enough of the node may not give a clear answer, and another biopsy or test may be needed. A biopsy result alone does not always determine the stage or extent of cancer.

\section*{Expected Results}
Results may show reactive or normal lymph tissue, infection, inflammation, lymphoma, metastatic cancer, or another condition. There is no single expected normal result; interpretation depends on why the biopsy was done and the tests performed.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend the appointment or contact arranged to review the pathology report and next steps. Depending on the result, care may include no further action, treatment for infection or inflammation, imaging, referral to a specialist, or additional sampling.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the care team for increasing redness, warmth, pus, fever, worsening pain, or bleeding that does not stop with gentle pressure. Seek emergency care for rapidly increasing swelling, heavy bleeding, fainting, new weakness or numbness, or trouble breathing or swallowing—especially after a biopsy in the neck. Follow the discharge instructions for the specific procedure.
