\chapter{Endoscopic Sleeve Gastroplasty}

\section*{Overview}
Endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty (ESG) is a weight-management procedure that changes the shape and capacity of the stomach using stitches placed from inside the stomach. A flexible camera and suturing device pass through the mouth; there are no cuts in the abdomen and no part of the stomach is removed. ESG is used as part of ongoing treatment for obesity, with nutrition, physical activity, behavior support, and sometimes medication.

\section*{Alternative Names}
ESG; endoscopic gastric sleeve; endoscopic stomach suturing; endoscopic gastric remodeling. ESG is different from surgical sleeve gastrectomy, which removes part of the stomach.

\section*{Principle}
The sutures fold the stomach wall inward to create a narrower, sleeve-shaped space. This limits how much food the stomach can hold and may help a person feel full sooner. The procedure does not reroute the intestines and is not the same as a surgical gastric bypass.

\section*{History}
ESG developed as an endoscopic treatment option for obesity. Clinical practice and patient selection continue to evolve. It is one option among nutrition and behavior treatment, anti-obesity medicines, other endoscopic therapies, and metabolic surgery; expected weight loss and risks differ across these treatments.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
ESG may be considered for an adult with obesity who has not achieved or maintained enough weight loss with less invasive approaches and who wants to discuss an endoscopic option. In the United States, the FDA-authorized APOLLO ESG system is indicated for adults with a body mass index (BMI) of 30 to 50 kg/m\textsuperscript{2} who have not succeeded with more conservative measures. Professional guidance and eligibility rules vary by country and program. A specialist team considers health conditions, prior treatments, goals, preferences, and the likely benefits and limits. ESG may also be considered in a broader obesity-care plan, including alongside medication or as a bridge to another treatment in selected cases.

\section*{Role in Care}
ESG is a treatment, not a diagnostic test. It may be offered as a primary endoscopic weight-management procedure or as part of a broader treatment plan. It should be delivered within a structured program that supports both initial weight loss and long-term maintenance.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is done in an endoscopy unit or operating room, usually with general anesthesia. The clinician passes an endoscope with a suturing attachment through the mouth into the stomach. Using a camera view, the clinician places a series of deep stitches that fold the stomach wall inward. The scope is then removed. ESG commonly takes about 1 to 2.5 hours, though the time varies. Most people go home the same day after recovery monitoring; some need overnight observation.

\section*{Preparation}
A specialist team reviews medical history, weight-management goals, medicines, and any conditions that may affect anesthesia or healing. Tests or consultations are arranged as needed. The team gives instructions about fasting and any pre-procedure diet. Blood-thinning medicines and diabetes medicines may need an individualized plan. Do not stop or change medicines without instructions from the treating team. Arrange for someone to take you home if sedation or anesthesia is used.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
ESG may be unsuitable or require additional assessment in the presence of certain stomach or esophageal conditions, prior stomach surgery, pregnancy, an uncorrected bleeding problem, or health conditions that make anesthesia unsafe. The impact of reflux, a large hiatal hernia, delayed stomach emptying, or eating and mental health conditions depends on the person and program. These are not all automatic exclusions; the specialist team decides whether ESG or another treatment is appropriate.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting are common in the early recovery period. Less common but serious risks include bleeding, infection, injury or a hole in the stomach, blood clots, and complications related to anesthesia. Reflux symptoms can occur or worsen. Stitches can loosen or the stomach shape can change over time, with less weight loss or weight regain. Some complications may require another endoscopic procedure, hospital care, or surgery.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
The care team monitors you after anesthesia and gives a staged eating plan. People typically start with liquids, then move to thicker liquids or soft foods and later to regular textures over several weeks; the pace and exact plan vary. Follow the prescribed plan, drink enough fluids, and take medicines as directed. Nausea and discomfort often improve over days, but recovery differs. Attend nutrition and follow-up visits; these help address hydration, food tolerance, medicines, and longer-term weight management.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
Weight loss usually develops over months. Results vary and depend on the procedure, health factors, and continued treatment and follow-up. In published patient information, people completing the program and following recommendations may lose about 10\% to 15\% of their starting body weight in the first year; an individual's result can be lower or higher. ESG can improve some obesity-related health conditions, but it does not guarantee their resolution. Long-term care may include nutrition and behavior support, physical activity, anti-obesity medication, and monitoring of related conditions.

\section*{Expected Results}
The aim is clinically meaningful weight loss and improved health, not a specific guaranteed amount. ESG does not remove stomach tissue or cause intestinal malabsorption. It is not a one-time cure for obesity, and weight regain can occur. The care team discusses whether results meet the person's health goals and what additional treatment may help.

\section*{Follow-up}
Keep scheduled visits with the endoscopy and weight-management team. Follow-up typically covers eating progression, hydration, nutrition, symptoms, weight change, and obesity-related conditions. If weight loss is inadequate or weight returns, the team may discuss adjusting the treatment plan, including medication or a repeat procedure in selected cases. Seek advice before pursuing another stomach procedure.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the treating team urgently for severe or increasing abdominal or chest pain, repeated vomiting or inability to keep fluids down, fever, vomiting blood, black or bloody stool, or worsening difficulty swallowing. Seek emergency care for fainting, trouble breathing, or rapidly worsening symptoms. Use the contact number and instructions provided by the treating center.
