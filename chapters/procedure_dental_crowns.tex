\chapter{Dental Crowns}

\section*{Overview}
A dental crown is a custom-made covering that restores a damaged tooth's shape, strength, and chewing function. A crown may also cover a dental implant, but an implant crown attaches to an implant rather than to a natural tooth. Crowns can be made from ceramic, metal, or layered materials. The best option depends on the tooth's condition, its position, bite forces, appearance, and the patient's preferences and health.

\section*{Common Instruments and Equipment}
The exact setup varies by technique and setting. Common items may include:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item dental handpiece and crown-preparation burs
  \item impression tray/material or digital scanner
  \item crown try-in tools and dental cement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Alternative Names}
Dental cap; full-coverage restoration. A crown placed on an implant is an implant-supported crown.

\section*{Principle}
A crown covers most or all of the visible part of a tooth. It can protect a weakened tooth and restore its shape and function. Preparing a natural tooth usually requires removing some tooth structure, which is permanent. A filling, an onlay, or another less extensive treatment may be suitable when enough healthy tooth remains; the dentist should discuss appropriate options.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
A crown may be recommended for a tooth that is badly decayed, cracked, broken, weakened by a large filling, or in need of protection after root canal treatment. Crowns may also support a dental bridge, cover a dental implant, or improve the shape or appearance of a tooth. Whether a crown is appropriate depends on the tooth's prognosis and the amount of healthy structure remaining.

\section*{Role in Care}
A crown is a restorative treatment, not a diagnostic test. The dentist examines the tooth and may use dental X-rays to assess decay, the roots, and surrounding bone before recommending treatment.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
For a conventional crown on a natural tooth, the dentist numbs the area with local anesthetic, removes decay if present, and shapes the tooth to make room for the crown. They take an impression or digital scan and record how the teeth meet when biting. A temporary crown may protect the prepared tooth while a dental laboratory makes the permanent crown. At a later visit, the dentist removes the temporary crown, checks the permanent crown's fit, contacts with neighboring teeth, and bite, then cements or bonds it in place. Some practices can make and fit a crown in one visit. Crown placement on an implant has different steps and timing.

\section*{Preparation}
Tell the dentist about medical conditions, allergies, pregnancy, and medicines. Discuss the reason for the crown, treatment alternatives, material, costs, and whether the tooth may need a core build-up or root canal treatment. Do not stop prescribed medicines unless the prescribing clinician advises you to. Local anesthetic is commonly used; ask the dentist about options if you have had a reaction or are anxious about treatment.

\section*{Contraindications}
A crown may not be suitable if too little sound tooth remains to support it, the tooth has a poor long-term prognosis, or active decay or gum disease needs treatment first.

\section*{Precautions}
The dentist may recommend a different restoration, root canal treatment, or extraction based on the examination. Bite forces, tooth grinding, oral hygiene, and the ability to keep the area clean can affect the plan and the crown's durability.

\section*{Risks}
Temporary sensitivity or gum soreness may occur after tooth preparation. Other possible problems include a crown that feels too high, loosens, chips, or fractures; irritation of the gum; decay at the crown margin; or injury to the tooth's pulp that later requires root canal treatment. A crown does not make the tooth immune to decay or fracture, and it may eventually need repair or replacement. The amount of risk varies with the tooth, material, and oral care.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Follow the dentist's instructions, especially while wearing a temporary crown. Avoid hard, sticky, or chewy foods that could loosen it, and chew on the other side when practical. Brush gently around the crown and clean between the teeth as instructed. Numbness from local anesthetic can last for a few hours; avoid chewing until normal feeling returns to reduce the risk of biting the cheek or tongue. Once the permanent crown is fitted, continue regular brushing, cleaning between teeth, and dental visits.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The dentist checks that the permanent crown fits securely and does not interfere with the bite. Contact the dental office if the bite feels uneven, the crown is loose, or pain or sensitivity is severe or does not improve. A crown can restore a tooth, but how long it lasts varies; no fixed lifespan can be guaranteed.

\section*{Expected Results}
The treated tooth should have a more protected shape and be usable for biting and chewing once treatment is complete. Appearance and feel depend on the tooth, crown material, and fit. The crown does not replace the need for ongoing oral hygiene or care of the underlying tooth and gums.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend the recommended dental review and routine examinations. If a temporary crown comes off, or a permanent crown becomes loose or breaks, contact a dentist promptly. Keep the crown if it comes off and take it to the appointment; do not use household glue to reattach it.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact a dentist promptly for a lost or loose crown, a new or worsening toothache, pain when biting, or swelling around the tooth. Seek urgent dental or medical care for facial swelling that is spreading, fever with dental swelling, or difficulty swallowing or breathing.
