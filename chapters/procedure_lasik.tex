\chapter{LASIK}

\section*{Overview}
Laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis (LASIK) is an elective operation that reshapes the cornea, the clear front surface of the eye, to reduce nearsightedness, farsightedness, or astigmatism. It may reduce dependence on glasses or contact lenses, but it does not guarantee perfect vision or prevent the normal age-related loss of near focus (presbyopia). Some people still need glasses or contact lenses after surgery.

\section*{Why It Is Done}
LASIK may be considered by adults whose glasses or contact lens prescription has been stable and whose eyes meet the safety requirements for treatment. A comprehensive eye examination is needed to assess the prescription, corneal shape and thickness, tear film, pupil size, and overall eye health. The surgeon also reviews medical conditions, medicines, work or activity needs, and expectations.

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test or Procedure}
LASIK is a vision-correction procedure, not a diagnostic test or treatment for eye disease. It is optional; glasses and contact lenses remain alternatives. Other refractive procedures, such as PRK or an intraocular lens, may be considered when LASIK is not suitable. The best option depends on the eye examination and the person's goals.

\section*{How It Is Performed}
The procedure is usually done in an outpatient setting while the patient is awake. Numbing eye drops are applied. A device holds the eyelids open, and the surgeon creates a thin flap in the cornea, often with a femtosecond laser or sometimes a mechanical instrument. The flap is lifted, and an excimer laser removes a carefully measured amount of corneal tissue to change its shape. The flap is repositioned and usually adheres without stitches. Treatment commonly takes only a short time for each eye. The patient may notice pressure, light, or sounds but should not feel sharp pain; the care team explains what to expect.

\section*{Preparation}
Before surgery, the eye doctor measures the eyes and checks for conditions that could raise risk or affect the result. Contact lenses can temporarily change corneal shape, so the surgeon will tell the patient how long to stop wearing them before measurements and surgery; this varies by lens type. Tell the team about all medicines, eye drops, allergies, prior eye surgery, dry-eye symptoms, herpes eye disease, and medical conditions. Follow instructions about food, medicines, and eye makeup, and arrange a ride home because vision may be blurry immediately afterward. Discuss the possibility of needing glasses, reading glasses, or additional treatment.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
LASIK may be unsuitable when the prescription is changing, the cornea is too thin or has an abnormal shape, or there is significant untreated dry eye or active eye infection. Keratoconus and other conditions that weaken or thin the cornea raise concern for progressive bulging (ectasia) after surgery. Pregnancy and breastfeeding can temporarily change the prescription; surgery is generally deferred until vision is stable. Some systemic diseases or medicines can affect healing. These factors require an individual assessment, and another procedure may be safer for some people.

\section*{Risks and Complications}
Temporary dry eye, scratchiness, light sensitivity, blurred vision, glare, halos, or starbursts can occur, especially early in recovery. Some symptoms may persist. Other risks include undercorrection or overcorrection, need for glasses or a further procedure, infection or inflammation, problems with corneal healing or the flap, and corneal ectasia. Rarely, vision may be worse than before surgery or may be permanently reduced. The surgeon should discuss risks specific to the person's eyes, prescription, and the laser system being used.

\section*{Aftercare and Recovery}
Vision may be blurry or hazy immediately after surgery and often improves over the next few days, but healing and prescription stabilization take longer. Mild irritation or watering can occur. Use prescribed eye drops as directed, attend follow-up visits, and do not rub or press on the eyes. Wear the protective shield if instructed and follow the surgeon's directions about bathing, swimming, eye makeup, driving, work, and exercise. Do not drive until vision meets legal requirements and the eye doctor says it is safe.

\section*{Outcome and Follow-up}
The result is checked during follow-up visits. Many people can do most daily activities without glasses, but the amount of correction varies and vision can change with age. LASIK does not prevent cataracts or presbyopia. Additional surgery is considered only after the prescription is stable and the cornea remains suitable. Keep routine eye examinations even after successful surgery.

\section*{Expected Results}
The goal is clearer distance vision and less dependence on corrective lenses. LASIK cannot guarantee 20/20 vision, eliminate all need for glasses, or correct presbyopia. Night-vision symptoms and dry eye may affect satisfaction even when the measured prescription improves.

\section*{Follow-up}
Attend every appointment scheduled by the eye surgeon, including early checks for healing and later checks of vision and corneal health. Continue any prescribed drops for the full instructed course. Contact the eye clinic if vision is not improving as expected or symptoms persist.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the eye surgeon urgently for worsening pain, increasing redness, discharge, sudden worsening or loss of vision, or new flashes, floaters, or a curtain-like shadow. Sudden vision loss, severe eye pain, or a serious eye injury requires emergency assessment. Do not wait for a routine follow-up if a concerning symptom develops.
